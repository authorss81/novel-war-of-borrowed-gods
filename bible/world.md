# World Bible

## Setting and date

The story takes place in **2042**, nearly half a century after **The First Silence**. The world is recognizably modern: cities have transit, hospitals, factories, social media, clean-water laws, unreliable weather apps, and millions of people who go to work. The supernatural event is not a replacement for infrastructure. It makes every existing network politically and morally fragile.

A seam can appear in a subway tunnel, a hospital, a rice-growing valley, a submarine cable room, a school gymnasium, or a city hall. Its danger is not that a monster walks through it. Its danger is that a person can be present in more than one plausible aftermath and a rescue can make a whole neighborhood disappear from the records everyone else trusts.

## What a seam is

A **seam** is a place where more than one plausible history can affect the same physical location. It is not a doorway to another world and it does not require a machine. A seam forms when a decision has become irreversible and many people are still acting on the assumption that one outcome will win.

A seam may produce ordinary-looking physical evidence: a door with two different handles, a wall marked with a name that no one remembers writing, a train arriving on a track that was removed yesterday, a shadow whose posture belongs to someone who has not arrived yet, or a weather pattern that belongs to a future season. A seam can be harmless for an hour and lethal when a crowd, a current, or a machine makes the branches collide.

Seams are strongest during the ninety-second rhythm associated with the First Silence, but they can open at any time of day. The exact length of a loan is never guaranteed. It ends when a named anchor event, a boundary, or the future lender’s consent ends it.

## What a future self can do

A future self is a possible person shaped by a complete lived branch. The person has a body history, relationships, skills, blind spots, and a finite understanding of what follows their own moment. When a future self appears through a seam, the manifestation is an **echo**, not a ghost and not a copied soul.

A future self can:

- lend a practiced capacity, a memory fragment, or a moment of trained perception;
- warn the present from inside the limits of what they actually know;
- set a boundary and withdraw the loan;
- disagree with another future self or with the borrower;
- ask for a present act that may not succeed.

A future self cannot:

- travel physically backward as a complete person;
- see every possible future;
- guarantee that their branch will become real;
- grant permanent ownership of a gift;
- act as an all-purpose solution to an unrelated problem;
- be compelled merely because the present has a convenient use for them.

A future self may be a version of Elias, Mara, Nell, a stranger, a dead historical rescuer, or someone who has not been born in the present. The future self’s branch can be strengthened, changed, or closed by the borrower’s choices. When a branch becomes impossible, its echo ends; that ending is not retroactive death in the present. The loss is still real to anyone who had formed a relationship with that possibility.

## The Long Emergency

The Long Emergency is not a single apocalypse. It is a series of recurring seam incidents that strain ordinary disaster response. Governments maintain the appearance of control through evacuation plans, continuity ratings, and the Civic Spine. People still celebrate birthdays, commute, argue about rent, attend school, and make jokes about the emergency channel. The difference is that a person may now need to decide whether a loved one is asking for help from this moment, a possible tomorrow, or a memory that has attached itself to the wrong person.

The Long Emergency has produced ordinary institutions as well as magic:

- hospitals use consent forms for loans because a present patient may reject a future version of their own treatment;
- schools teach children how to recognize a duplicated hallway and how to report it;
- insurers charge more for neighborhoods with unstable seam density;
- families leave “echo lamps” in windows as memorials, not guarantees that the dead are calling;
- workers in the Cinder Quarter maintain neighborhood wards for free because central registries have never been able to reach them in time.

## Major locations

### Havenport

A river-and-estuary city of roughly 1.4 million people, built across the Salt Meridian. Its elevated rail, container port, storm wall, and older tunnel network all cross unstable ground. Havenport is not a fantasy capital; it is a working city whose mayor, port authority, hospitals, unions, and residents disagree about who should be allowed to borrow a god. Elias lives in the Cinder Quarter and works for the Havenport Rescue Service.

### The Salt Meridian

A long band of unstable ground following the estuary and buried rail lines. It is officially cordoned, but homes and workshops sit inside it because the city could never move everyone. A seam in the Meridian can connect a flood-control gate, a hospital oxygen line, and a neighborhood school in the same nine-second interval.

### Kestrel Tunnel and Kestrel Station

The tunnel where Samuel Rook died in 2034 and where the story’s opening collapse occurs. The tunnel’s concrete has ordinary rebar and ordinary corrosion; the seam changes the arrangement of the structure without repairing it. Elias’s family history and the present emergency meet here. Kestrel Station contains a major relay; the relay is connected to the remote Mercy Field lock through the old array, not by physical adjacency.

### The Cinder Quarter

A low-rise, heavily repaired neighborhood on Havenport’s western edge. It has volunteer seam wards, informal markets, union halls, and a dense network of people who rely on practical knowledge more than official certificates. Inez Sato’s electrical cooperative is based here.

### The Spine

A converted freight depot beneath Havenport where the Civic Spine keeps emergency records, relay batteries, and a public archive of known seams. It is both a functioning operations center and a political institution. During the first half of the story, its central index is more reliable than its public explanations.

### Mercy Field

A remote desert site where the original Mercy Array began. It contains the first failed relays and the first surviving record of The First Silence. The site is not a sacred temple. It is a dangerous industrial ruin surrounded by a small research settlement and a sealed military perimeter. Its old lock is paired with the Kestrel relay; together they form the final hinge sites, not one physical room.

### The Ninefold Basin

An inland region where several major seams overlap. Its farms, towns, and rail junctions are politically independent but causally entangled. The basin is the first place where rival future coalitions can use the same present-day infrastructure.

### The Unwritten Territories

A growing belt of places where a forced future has erased too many alternatives. Roads can be physically present while belonging to histories no one remembers. Unwritten zones are not ghost towns: people live there, but they often lack documents, recognized relatives, or stable maps. The danger is that entering one can make a visitor’s own history harder to verify.

## Technology and material culture

The world uses modern machines alongside seam glass and borrowed techniques. Most responders still rely on:

- winches, hydraulic cutters, thermal cameras, radios, breathing gear, rope systems, and hand signals;
- battery-backed emergency repeaters and analog maps;
- seam glass, a brittle material that records the shape of a possible outcome when made with shared labor and a clear boundary;
- anchor lamps, portable devices that show whether a nearby future voice has consented to be heard;
- mechanical safeguards, because magic can fail during a surge.

Civic Spine terminals display short offers and warnings. They do not know the future and do not select loans. The interface is a translation layer built by humans, so it can be incomplete, censored, or wrong. A message can disappear because a relay lost power, not because the future withdrew.

## Political and social structure

The Continuity Compact is a treaty among regional governments, not a world government. National governments still control their own armed forces and borders. The Civic Spine coordinates disaster response across those borders, while the Continuity Office in Havenport controls the local registry and licensing. Most people experience these institutions as a choice between a slow public process and an emergency that ignores process.

A new social class has grown around seam work: licensed rescuers, anchor technicians, echo counselors, civic wardens, and people who repair the records of erased neighborhoods. The work is skilled and physically dangerous. It is also vulnerable to corruption. A wealthy developer can buy a stable seam, a corporation can hide a failed loan, and a frightened community can vote to close itself to outsiders.

The central political disagreement is not “magic good, magic bad.” It is whether a society should preserve the ability to choose among uncertain futures, or whether it should trade that ability for one protected outcome. The One-Future Choir calls the trade mercy. The Open Hand calls it surrender.

## Factions

### Havenport Rescue Service and local crews

The people who respond first. They are trained in mundane rescue, seam safety, and the rule that a living voice outranks a perfect prediction. Local crews vary in discipline. Some are brave and improvisational; some are exhausted and dismissive of outsiders. The Rescue Service is where Elias’s competence belongs, not a separate superhero organization.

### Civic Spine

A distributed emergency network that coordinates warnings, hospitals, transit, and registries. Its public promise is that no one should have to face a disaster alone. Its internal weakness is that its oldest systems were designed to suppress contradictory reports. Civic Spine can be a tool of liberation or a tool of central control, depending on who can read and change its rules.

### The Continuity Office

The Havenport branch of the international continuity bureaucracy. It licenses powerful borrowers, investigates unauthorized seams, confiscates unsafe objects, and decides which names enter the official record. The Office is staffed by people who have seen disasters and do not trust untrained miracles. Its worst policy is treating consent as paperwork that can be waived during an emergency.

### The Unbound

A loose network of brokers, illegal rescuers, scavengers, and unlicensed anchor technicians. Some use their gifts responsibly; many sell future voices, falsify returns, or force people into unstable circuits. The Unbound is the first pressure Elias faces because it makes the personal cost of borrowing visible in ordinary crime.

### The Open Hand

A decentralized mutual-aid movement that begins as a loose practice among local crews. It insists on asking rather than assuming consent, keeping the right to withdraw, and recording the names of people whose futures are used. It contains idealists, opportunists, and people who disagree violently about what “local” means. It is not a saintly faction; it is a durable answer being built.

### The One-Future Choir

A political and spiritual movement formed from emergency administrators, engineers, clergy, and people who have survived repeated collapses. It argues that multiple futures are a source of war and that one stable outcome is the only humane way to protect civilization. Its first pilot cells were called the **Bellwether Choir**, a name used for programs that publicly chose one safer outcome for a district. The movement later became the One-Future Choir and the organizing force behind the One-Future Compact. Its members still use ordinary emergency work and genuine fear as cover for authoritarian control.

### The Returning Hand

A coalition from a future branch led by a possible version of Elias Rook. The Hand believes its stable future is the only viable one and tries to make the present conform to it. Its members are not time-traveling invaders; they influence the present through other people’s voluntary loans, coercion, and the creation of anchor networks. The Hand is foreshadowed early and fully identified before it becomes the central antagonist.

### The Mercy Line

A loose lineage of technicians, archivists, and survivors connected to the original Mercy Project. Some want the First Silence exposed; others want the array buried; others believe its safety protocol can be repaired. The Line supplies history and tools but is not a hidden master plan or a final enemy.

### The First Choice

The final coercive operation built from the Mercy Array’s original safety logic. It forces captured Civic Spine relays toward one approved branch. It is an institutional and technological attack, not a new supernatural faction or a free-standing personality. The First Choice can be slowed or stopped by distributed consent; it cannot manufacture consent from a future person who refuses.

## What the public knows by the end of Volume 01

The public knows that seam events are real, that a trained responder can sometimes borrow a future capacity, and that the loan is dangerous. It does not know whether the future self is a person, whether the current timeline is fixed, who built the original array, or why a second political movement wants to choose the future. The early story must show the ordinary work before any lore lecture.

## Reveal boundaries

- The world is not secretly a game, simulation, or computer-generated reality.
- The Mercy Array did not create alternate futures; it made their disagreement physically legible.
- “Borrowed God” is a social nickname, not a species or a separate pantheon.
- The final threat is a policy and a protocol built from early choices, not a newly arriving celestial force.
- The world can be damaged, rebuilt, and politically contested; the ending is a negotiated settlement, not a reset of history.
