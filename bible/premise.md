# Premise: The War of Borrowed Gods

## One-sentence premise

In 2042, disaster responder Elias Rook survives a tunnel collapse by accepting a temporary power from a future version of a person, only to discover that every loan changes the future that offered it—and that the coming Convergence War is being fought by people trying to force the world to keep only one possible history.

## Pitch

Elias Rook is a field rescue technician in Havenport, an estuary city built across a fault in the world’s apparent time. He works with cranes, breathing apparatus, thermal cameras, and the stubborn routines of crews who know that a bad decision can turn a rescue into a list of names. When a commuter train drops into a seam during the Kestrel collapse, Elias hears a woman offer him a gift from seventeen years in the future: a hand that can keep a failing body alive long enough for repair.

The gift works. It also leaves traces in the present, and the future woman can withdraw it. Elias learns that the frightening voices people call gods are not invaders or departed spirits. They are possible people, built from the lives they lived in futures that may or may not happen. Their power is a practiced capacity, not a spell, and their consent is real. The closer Elias gets to the source of the borrowed powers, the more he risks replacing a future person with a useful story about what that person must do.

The immediate mystery is the disappearance of Elias’s sister Nell during the Kestrel collapse. The larger mystery is why the same impossible offer appears in unrelated disasters around the world, who built the emergency network that made the offers legible, and why a political movement now called the One-Future Choir wants to end uncertainty by making one future compulsory. Elias wants to save his sister and stop the coming war. He needs to learn that being responsible for people does not mean owning their choices. His final victory is not to become the strongest borrowed god; it is to make the future less like a weapon and more like a place where people can answer for themselves.

## Central reader promise

Each major volume centers on a different borrowed god: a future nurse whose hands keep a patient alive, a future engineer who can read load paths, a future child who remembers a safe exit, a future archivist who carries names, and many others. Every god is a person with a history, a boundary, a current willingness, and a future that can change after the loan. The ethical problem is never simply “Can Elias use the power?” It is “What does he owe the person whose possible life makes the power available, and what will that use make impossible?”

The action remains grounded in disaster response. The most important victories come from a plan, a team, a repaired pump, a route cleared in time, or a person who is willing to say no. Magic changes the options; it does not remove the need for judgment.

## Protagonist engine

- **External want:** Find Nell, understand the first seam that took her, and prevent the Convergence War from turning every possible future into a weapon.
- **Internal need:** Replace the belief that he alone must decide who is worth saving with the practice of sharing authority, accepting limits, and honoring refusal.
- **Central flaw:** Elias turns care into control. He can read a structure, a crowd, and a body under pressure, but when fear rises he makes a promise before he knows whether he can keep it. He treats people as exits from his own guilt.
- **Fear:** Becoming the responder who decides that one life is an acceptable loss—or becoming the responder who runs away and lets someone die because he waited for a perfect plan.
- **False belief:** If he is fast enough, strong enough, and stubborn enough, he can keep every promise.
- **Final truth:** A person is not a future resource. Saving someone well includes allowing them, even in a possible future, to refuse the role that would save Elias.

## Opening situation

The Kestrel rail-and-tunnel collapse begins as an ordinary structural emergency and opens a seam that repeats the same ninety seconds of disaster. Elias is on shift when the missing train disappears behind a wall that should not be there. Nell’s last message reaches the rescue channel from somewhere on the other side. Elias is offered a future version of Mara Okafor’s “still hand,” the ability to keep one living body from failing while it is repaired. He accepts under pressure and saves a trapped passenger, but the choice changes the immediate scene: a safe exit appears, another exit closes, and the people who remember the rescue no longer agree on what happened.

## Ending contract

The final conflict is between people who want to preserve uncertainty and people who believe a single stable future is the only humane way to prevent extinction. Elias prevents the One-Future Compact from completing the First Choice, but he does so by refusing to become a permanent god. He gives up his guaranteed personal future, his private access to his own possible selves, and the fantasy that a loving relationship can be secured by a correct choice. The war ends through distributed, revocable consent rather than a singular victory. The power survives as a dangerous civic tool, and the world remains imperfect, divided, and capable of choosing more than one tomorrow.

## Genre and style guardrails

- Modern near-future disaster fantasy; no medieval world, game world, or system-dungeon presentation.
- One slow-burn relationship: Elias and Mara Okafor. No harem, no forced soul-mate bond, and no promise made by a magical override.
- Third-person close POV, primarily Elias, with occasional clearly bounded secondary POVs when another future’s agency needs to be felt.
- Ordinary labor, institutions, budgets, grief, injury, and maintenance remain visible. Borrowed power never makes paperwork, transport, sleep, or trust disappear.
- Future selves are limited to the knowledge and relationships of their own branch. They can be wrong, incomplete, fearful, or changed by the present.
- The interface gives one short offer or warning at a time. It never provides a quest log, a skill tree, a loot inventory, a numeric power meter, or a solution to a moral question.
- No new cosmic enemy may appear after the First Choice is introduced. The final antagonists are the established human factions and the established Mercy Array protocol.
