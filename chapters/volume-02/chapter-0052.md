# Chapter 52 — What the Office Holds

The Spine depot runs its public counter on Saturdays and it runs the aisle it opens onto on Saturdays too, and that is the only reason the inquiry's exhibit inspection is on a Saturday at all, because an archive that is a working archive does not close for anybody's convenience, and the man who said so was Hollis Vane and he said it in the first ninety seconds, standing in a flatbed bay with his coat still on.

"I have not chosen to be the custodian of the inquiry's exhibits," he said. "I was the custodian of the site file on the seventeenth and this is the same file with a hat on. I would like that in the room's understanding of what this afternoon is, because in four years somebody will write that the archivist cooperated, and I will not have been there."

"Then what is this afternoon," Elias Rook said.

"It is a Saturday," Vane said. "Ilse Brauer works Saturdays. She does not work Sundays. The aisle is locked at six and it does not matter what an inquiry says about it, and I have been asked about that twice today by people who do not work here." He looked at the clock on the wall of the bay, which was a thing that existed. "You have until six. I am not being difficult. I am telling you where the walls are so that you do not spend a day walking into them."

---

Elias had gone in with a list, and the list was on the back of a sheet in his own handwriting because he had one hand and could not hold a folder and a door and a pen at once, and Joon Park had taken the folder off him at the top of the ramp without being asked, which is what four weeks of a left hand that has gone completely does to a man who has never once been helped with a door.

They went through it in an hour and forty minutes, and it came out the way such afternoons always come out, which is that everybody in the building is holding something and nobody is holding the same thing.

The Office held the Kestrel response record in full, four copies, one of them in a node. It held the seal, the classification, the ninety-day clock that the seal spent, Hollis Vane's Finding 1 with a mechanism, accepted on the seventeenth, and the four findings in the order the Director answered them.

It held the loan record from the first week, which was the strongest document Elias had ever been able to put his hand on, and it held all of it. The offer. The boundary. The gift. The return, which was a wheel. The time. And the countersignature across the bottom, which is Mara Okafor's and the Office's, in two hands.

It held no name.

"The page names him as the body," Elias said. "It named him as the body on the ninth day and it is the only page in the country that says a person was kept alive by something that cannot be asked for twice, and on the sixteenth he withdrew the field with his name in it, and you wrote the withdrawal into the same file in the same hand and made it a finding, because a withdrawal is a record and not an absence."

"That is Finding 3, which I made and which the Director adopted at the cost of five years of forms and a third of her clerks," Vane said. "It is also, this afternoon, the only reason the exhibit list has a hole in it where a name should be. I have not had to explain a single thing to you about that, and I would like you to understand what it has saved me: I have had to explain nothing, and the cost of that is a file in which a nineteen-year-old is a set of facts with no person attached, which is correct practice and which I do not enjoy and which I am not permitted to change."

Elias looked at the list he had written, and then he crossed off a line, because the line was about a name and there was no name to cross off.

Then Vane said, "You have a second question in that file and it is not on my list either, so before you ask it I am going to tell you that I have looked for it."

"What question."

"The one you asked in front of witnesses on the twenty-ninth day about whether a boundary holds for a second body." Vane did not move. "It is not in my exhibit list. It is not in the transcript of any proceeding, because there has not been one since. It is not in the site file, it is not in the medical ledger, and it is not in any of the four copies of the loan record, and the reason is that the question was asked in a hall by a man who was told by a woman with a rule that any offer, any boundary, any refusal and any condition goes to the people who pay for it before it is acted on — and your hall did that, in front of nine people, on the page, in a book that is not mine."

"Who has it," Elias said.

"You do," Vane said. "Joon Park does. And a residents' file is going to be the subject of a demand on Monday morning, and I am telling you this on Saturday, two days early, because I would rather you knew it from me than from a form."

---

Ilse Brauer filled in the field at ten past eleven on Saturday morning with a pen, on a bound ledger, on the flatbed, standing up, while three people from a hall watched.

She had done it without asking anybody first, and then she asked, which is the order the thing actually happens in.

"Am I permitted to."

Joon Park, who had not said a word for an hour, said, "You are filling in a field that has been empty and visible in a printed document since the twenty-third of September, and you have asked a room of laypeople whether you may write on a page of a bound ledger in an archive you do not own. Say the rest of the question out loud so that somebody can answer the whole of it."

Brauer said, "Am I permitted to fill in the subject field on the 14:12 line, given that I was not the person who opened the box and I do not know who was."

Vane said, "You are not. Nobody is. The field is blank because no person has ever come forward who can attest to the subject, and you are not attesting to the subject, and if you put words in there then a person reads them in 2046 and believes a stranger wrote them." He turned the book about ten degrees. "That said, I am not going to stop you, and here is why, and I would like the hall to hear the reason because it is the most useful thing I know about this office. **A field that can be blank will be filled in by somebody who was not there, who will be entirely accurate about their own act and entirely silent about somebody else's, and that is not sabotage. It is a form and a hurry.** In nineteen years the only filling-in of a blank subject field I have seen come out of an archive is a woman remembering her own Thursday and putting it in a Monday's row, and the reason is that nobody ever told her it was a Monday's row."

"What did you write," Elias said.

Brauer read it out. It said: *Routine archive review, aisle 9, shelf B. Item 1 of 3 retrieved, mis-shelved, returned. 11:40 to 12:05. Checked against the shelf list and found in order. No other subject.*

"That is true," she said. "All of it. I did that on the Thursday of the week of the sixteenth, and I am not on the duty roster for that week, so it went in two rows up on a Saturday, and I did not look at the year, and the day it happened is not the day it is written on."

"It is the wrong day," Vane said, "and it is still the only honest sentence anybody has put in that column in a month."

"It is my day," Brauer said. "That is the whole of what I know. If I am not allowed to put my own day in my own book then I have been doing this for nineteen years for nothing and I would like to know that now rather than in a fortnight."

Vane looked at it for a while and then said, "Enter it, and enter the shelf and the date, and do not enter the box number, because a box number on that line is a claim about a box and the only box number anybody in this room is going to say out loud is two-one-four and it is not yours."

"Two-one-four is still out," Joon said.

"Two-one-four has been out since the twenty-fourth of August by the log's own account," Vane said, "and I have asked three people this month who logged it out and got three answers, and two of the three were not in this building, and I am the third, and I did not do it. There is a version of this sentence that reads as an accusation and I am declining to write it, and I want that refusal noted as well."

Joon Park took the sheet out of his own folder, the one that is not in any building anybody can enter, and wrote on it in his own hand: *Field filled, Saturday 11:12, by I. Brauer, in the room, from her own recollection, eighteen days after the column was restored. Author of the entry present. Author of the event not present and not identified. No meaning recorded and none asked for.*

And then he said the sentence, out loud, to a bay with three people from a hall in it, because that is the only way he knows to refuse something and be legible about it.

"I am refusing to write next to that what anybody thinks it means, and I am saying so where you can hear me, because in this building I have learned that silence looks exactly like a decision, and a refusal has to be legible or it is not a refusal." He put the pen down. "And I want one question answered while four of you are standing here, and it is a question about the field and not about the afternoon, and I am asking it out loud so that it is in a room and not in my head. **Did you know it was empty when you filled it in.**"

"I did," Brauer said.

"You had known for eighteen days."

"I had known for eighteen days."

"And nobody told you not to."

"Nobody told me not to," she said, "and I did not think to ask, and I would like to say that I would have asked if it had occurred to me and I am aware that it did not occur to me and I am not going to pretend it did."

Elias Rook did not ask her a second question. He wanted to. It was right there — *who told you the column was there* — and it would have been a good question and it would have taught him nothing, and he had a month of practice at knowing the difference and about nine days of being on the wrong side of a document that had a case number on it.

"Thank you," he said, instead.

Vane walked them out to the roller door at five minutes to six, because the aisle locks at six and he locks it, and he stood aside and let the door down behind them before he said the last thing.

"I am going to offer you nothing and it is going to cost you nothing, and I have never had an easier afternoon than this one and I have not been thanked for it once in nineteen years, so I am not going to stop now. Everything you need to make the case that somebody altered that record is not in this building. Everything you have is in a garage on an ordinary street, in a box, in a building that the Monday order is going to take. And the one thing in your own hands that nobody can be given an order to produce is on the wall of a house in the Cinder Quarter, in chalk, and it is not mine, and I have thought about that for two hours today and I have decided that I find it extremely inconvenient."

---

They got back to Ash Street at twenty past six and Joon Park put the sheet with his own writing on it in the residents' file, and initialled it, and read the whole line out loud in the records room because the records room has four people in it and Petar Lisk was one of them and Petar Lisk asked to be there and nobody argued.

Then Elias Rook stood in a room on Ash Street and understood the thing he had been carrying since Thursday without naming it, which is that the strongest record this hall owns is the one document in the city that lives in a building somebody else can walk into and take, and the safest record is the one on a wall, and neither of those is the same as a good one.

He had spent thirty-four days believing that a record kept by hand, in a book, by people who would be asked before they wrote anything down, was the answer to an institution.

It was the answer to an institution. It was also the reason he could not show anybody anything.
