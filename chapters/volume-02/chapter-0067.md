# Chapter 67 — The Store of Older Sheets

The public hour on a Sunday is ten to twelve, and the key is on a hook, and the woman who sits at the desk under the key is called Bettina Hoyle and has been doing it for six years, and in those six years four people have come, and two of them were lost.

She was not sorry about the two. She said so before anybody asked her to be.

Rufus Aitken came in at five past ten with a bulb in a bag and put it in the fitting over the third aisle himself, on a step stool, while two people from a neighborhood in another city stood at the end of a ladder and did not offer, because he had not asked.

"Two rules," he said. "You ask before you photograph and you photograph what I say you may photograph, and if you have a camera in a store room you are not a visitor, you are a problem with a camera. And I do not read down the aisle while you work. If I read down the aisle, people take things out of my building that I have not seen go past me, and I have been doing this for thirty years and I am not going to start."

---

The 4C sheets were not in the 4C box.

That was the first thing, and it was the thing that turned the morning, and Joon Park noticed it before Elias did because he was reading the shelf marks on the spines the way he reads everything.

"These are not the renumbering sheets. These are something older."

"They are the sheets the renumbering sheets were made out of," Aitken said. "That is the ordinary arrangement. You consolidate the working papers in first, you renumber the mapping onto them afterwards, and the working papers are what you keep, because a number that has been in a working paper for forty years is worth more than a number that is four weeks old in a mapping file. That is not policy. That is what every one of my eleven predecessors did and it is why this building is worth the roof it has."

He put a hand flat on the spines, the way people do in a store room.

"Pulled in the spring of two thousand and thirty-five for the regional mapping consolidation, out of the store of older sheets, and put on the shelf where they have been since. That is a shelf I can find in eleven seconds and I will find it, and then we can go back to the count, which is a real thing and which you have in your hand."

"There is a log," Elias said.

"There is a log." Aitken went and got it. It came back in a brown board folder, bound, with a printed spine, and it smelled of a cupboard.

---

The entry was on the fourth of March, nineteen ninety-eight.

**4C / WEST APRON — SECT. WORKS, EARLIER SERIES — TO CONSOL. STORE 3.**

And then a space, and a unit in a block, and nothing else. No person's name anywhere in the entry, and no person's name in the block, because the block is a unit and a unit signs a movement the way a unit signs a task.

Elias Rook did the arithmetic on the back of his hand.

"The renumbering was in the spring of two thousand and thirty-five," he said. "That is thirty-seven years after this line."

"Yes."

"The sheets were pulled *for* the two thousand and thirty-five consolidation."

"They were, yes," Aitken said.

"And the log says the pull was in nineteen ninety-eight."

"It does." Aitken turned the book round so they could all see it, and put his thumb on the line, and then took his thumb off, because a thumb on a line is a thing you do not do in a store room. "And I am going to say the only thing I am able to say about that, and then I am going to go and stand at the end of the aisle, because it is not enough and you are going to find that out in about four minutes and I would rather be standing up when you do.

"A store is re-purposed. That is the first answer and it is the true one and it is boring. This building was a tram depot and it has been a records office since nineteen sixty-one, and a store is a shelf with a job, and the job changes. A shelf is renumbered, and the book that records which shelf is which is not *usually* renumbered in the same week, because the person who renumbers the shelf and the person who keeps the movement book are different people with different jobs and one of them is in a hurry on a Tuesday. So a book can carry a line for a movement that happened later than the line's own date, and everybody who has ever worked in a store has seen it, and has also told themselves it was not happening to them.

"That is the honest institutional answer. **It is good practice and it is incomplete, and I am not going to pretend it closes the question, because a book is a book and a log is a log and neither of those is an explanation of a date.**"

He went and stood at the end of the aisle.

---

Bettina Hoyle said the thing nobody had said, from a desk, in the first minute of it, in the tone of a woman correcting a date.

"I want to say the other half of that, because I am the one who has had nobody come for six years and I have read that book."

Nobody said anything.

"The other half is that **a book is a thing a person can argue with.**" She turned a page with two fingers. "Whoever wrote that line in March ninety-eight thought the sheets were going somewhere and wrote down where. If it is wrong, it is wrong in ink, in a book, in a room, and a person can be shown the book and can say *that is the wrong year* out loud, and then something happens. That is what a book is *for*. That is the whole difference between a book and a form, and I have watched a lot of people in this city not know what that difference is, and it is the only thing I would tell a person from another city about this building."

She shut the book.

"Fine. Now tell me who is arguing with it, because nobody has come in six years and I would like it to be somebody."

---

Joon Park wrote down what was on the page.

He wrote the heading, the date, the line, the unit, the shelf, and the fact that the pull it recorded was a pull for the two thousand and thirty-five consolidation, which is on the spine of every one of the hundred and six sheets in the box they had been in on Wednesday. And then he stopped, with the pen on the paper, for about nine seconds, in a store room in another city, in front of a custodian and a woman at a desk who had been alone down here for six years.

"I am going to say a thing out loud," he said, "and I want it said out loud, and then I want to stop saying it.

"**This is the shape of the aisle log.** A book somebody can be argued with, and a field in it that is either there or is not, and a person who filled it in accurately about a day that was not the day it was written on, and no conspiracy, and a form and a hurry. I can see it. I have been seeing it since Wednesday morning. And I am not going to write the shape down, and I am not going to write *1998* next to *2035* with a mark between them, and nobody in this aisle is going to say the word *because* about anything, and if the shape is a shape then it is a shape in five hundred stores in this region and it means that people who keep books are working in conditions, and it does not mean anything about this book in particular, and if I write it down it will stop being the first thing and start being the finding."

Elias Rook opened his mouth to say something and found that he did not have it, and that he was about to say it anyway, and that the sentence he was about to say was *this is exactly what happened in aisle nine.*

Joon Park had not looked at him. He was still writing.

"I do not know why the line says what it says," Joon said. "That is the sentence. I have got eleven days of writing things down and it is the first one I have written in this city that is going to make somebody in Havenport angry with me, and I would like it in your book with the date on it, Mrs. Hoyle, because you said a book is what a person can be argued with and I am arguing with myself and I would like the date."

Bettina Hoyle got up and came and read the page, upside down, the way everybody reads a page that is not theirs, and then she went back to her desk and put the date in the store's own day book, because a store keeps a day book, and that was the whole of the argument and it took forty seconds.

---

Elias did not understand why he had kept his mouth shut until the corridor of the transit hall at five past one, with his coat on, in front of a coffee machine that worked.

Then he understood it, and it was not what he expected. It was not that a shape might be a trap. It was that Joon Park had done the thing Elias had spent six weeks unable to do, which was to say *we do not know that* in a room that wanted a sentence, and to say it **loudly, in front of witnesses, in a building where he had no standing and no authority and no office and no instrument to protect**, and that in a hall on Ash Street that sentence had cost him something every time because the hall was his and the walls were his and the sentence was a performance the room let him do.

In a store room in Cordell the sentence had cost Joon Park thirty seconds and a day-book entry and nothing else, because nothing there belonged to him and nothing there was going to be read by strangers.

It cost him almost nothing, and it was still a refusal, and the day it costs nothing is the day it stops being a refusal and becomes a habit.

He wanted to be told. He wanted it badly enough that his hand was on the arm of the chair. He did not push, and he wrote down that he had not pushed, and the not-pushing was the only thing he did in six days that he would not have to take back.

---

"What may we photograph," Elias said, when he had that out of the way.

"Ask properly and I will answer properly," Aitken said. "The movement book, freely, your own hand, and I will initial every frame with the date and the page number and you will initial it too, and one of you stays with me the whole time.

"The working sheets are a different matter. They are the thing under consultation and this is not a records request, it is a store attendance, and I will allow a sheet that has been *replaced* to be photographed because a replacement tells nobody anything. Any sheet that is original, no. Not today, and not from me, and it is not a judgment about you, it is that a person who has been in a store room for two hours with a camera is a person who leaves with a thing in nine weeks that we cannot account for."

"Pages one to four," Elias said, "which are all replacements, and whatever is a replacement in the box, and not the others."

"That I can do." Aitken looked at Joon. "And you are going to pay for the certificate on the movement book, because photographs that leave a store room leave it with a certificate on them, and the certificate is a piece of paper, and paper costs."

"How much."

"Two dollars forty," Bettina Hoyle said, from the desk, without looking up, and then said, "and it is three dollars ten if you want it certified by the center rather than by the store, and I would say get the store one, and I would say that in my own voice and in my own book."

Elias Rook laughed, once, out loud, in a store room, in a city forty minutes and an ordinary road from a building with twenty-eight people in it, and it was the first thing that had made him laugh since the thirtieth of September, and Bettina Hoyle looked up at him and said, "Well," and went back to her day book.

---

They photographed the movement book for forty minutes and got four frames of the fourth of March, nineteen ninety-eight: the line, the unit, the shelf, and the eleven entries either side of it, which were about something else entirely. And Elias Rook wrote in the notebook the thing he had learned, and then decided not to learn anything from it yet — that an archive keeps a book with a date in it that it did not write, and does not correct it, and does not explain it, and puts it on a shelf where somebody can be shown it.

He wrote four words under that: **NOT YET. AND NOT BY US.**
