# Chapter 86 — Who Benefits

He walked over the bridge at ten past six on Friday morning with the week's inventory sheet in a folder, because the Friday slot had been returned in a letter four lines long and a hose is not his and the inventory half is, and R. Dunleavy took the sheet at the bay door, read it standing up, wrote two figures on it in his own hand, gave it back, and said nothing else at all, which is the third Friday in a row and is now a habit on both sides.

There was nothing in the world that wanted anything from him until six that evening, and he got to Ash Street at half past four, and there were already three people in the records room.

Joon Park was at the table with the custody schedule open in front of him and the tin beside it. Inez Sato was not there and had said she would not be. Tomas Vale had been at the table since two in the afternoon with a copy of the revision letter in front of him and about eleven pages of his own handwriting on a pad beside it, and he had not said anything to anybody for two and a half hours, which was a thing he did at the back of a corridor in September and which everybody in that room had learned to be careful of.

"You have been in here since two," Elias Rook said.

"I have."

"You have read that letter about nine times."

"Fourteen," Tomas Vale said. "Sit down."

---

The question came from Joon Park, because the questions in that building came from Joon Park, and he had been carrying it since a Tuesday in October in another city and it had been refused by a Deputy Director and by a Counsel and had been sitting in a records room on Ash Street ever since.

"Mr. Vale," he said. "I am going to ask you a question and it is not a fair question and I have been told twice this week by competent people that it is not theirs, and you are the third person and you are the last one I am going to ask, because after today I am going to write down that I asked three people and I am not going to ask a fourth.

"What does a system gain by having fewer contradictions in it."

Tomas Vale looked at the pad for about eleven seconds.

"Sit down, Mr. Park, and do not write anything for a minute, because I am going to say it once and I would like it said in a room with three people in it and not typed."

Elias Rook sat down. Joon Park put the pen down.

"A branch is a place where what happened and what somebody said happened do not match. That is the whole of what a branch is, before anybody calls it a future and makes it sound like a place people live. One report says one thing. The other says another. You have two reports and they cannot both be right, and the size of the difference between them is the size of the contradiction.

"**A deleted branch produces fewer contradictory reports.**"

He said it the way a man reads a pressure off a gauge, and then he said the rest of it without stopping, which was not a thing he had ever done.

"That is not a moral sentence. It is a count. And I want it written down that I have said that in a room and that nobody in the room argued with it, because I have been waiting since the fourth of September for somebody to put a person in it and there is not one, and I have had two and a half hours and fourteen readings and I have got to the bottom of the thing and the bottom of the thing is a count.

"This city runs a network. It has run it since before any of us were on a street. And the job of that network — the whole of the job, the entire reason it exists and is paid for and has a form for everything — **is to suppress contradictory reports.** That is not what it does. That is what it is *for.*

"And a network that is built for the suppression of contradictory reports is measurably safer with fewer of them in it. Not safer for us. **Safer.** That is the design target. That is the number the design is pointed at, and I have spent thirty-one years being taught to read that number, and I read it correctly on Monday afternoon and I would like to go home.

"So nobody has to decide anything. Nobody has to want it. Nobody has to be careless. **The machine is pointed at a number, and the deletion takes the number up, and the number is the safety, and the machine is right about itself.**"

He stopped for about four seconds.

"It is right. It has been right the whole time, and that is the part I have not been able to get past since Monday, and I have tried twice today, and I am telling you that I cannot argue with it, and I have argued with a Deputy Director and a Counsel and a woman with a mop in ten weeks and I have never once been unable to argue with anything before this week.

"And it reports the whole thing with one word, and you all know the word, and the word is *error*. Because an error is a thing that happened that gets put right in the ordinary course. A contradiction is a thing that happened that gets put right in the ordinary course. And if the loss of a branch is reported as an error, then the loss of a branch is a thing that gets put right, and the person it happened to does not have a word in the report at all — because a person is not a thing that gets put right. **A thing that gets put right has no name in it. That is the whole grammar of it and the grammar is correct.**"

---

He put both hands flat on the table.

"Now I am going to say the other half, and then I am not going to say anything else for the rest of the evening, and I would like somebody to write down that I said the whole of it once.

"**I have spent nine weeks telling this hall that the number of decisions in a corridor is the number of people who get hurt.** That is not wrong. It is the most correct sentence I have said in ten years. It is the sentence that machine is built to act on. I have been saying it in rooms since I was thirty-four and I have never once asked who was counting, and I have never once asked what the count was for, and I did not ask on Monday either, and I want that on the page with my name at the bottom of it.

"That is all. Do not make a speech at me."

---

Nobody made a speech at him.

Joon Park did not move for about nine seconds, and then he took his hand off the notebook, and it was the first time in ten weeks that anybody in that room had seen him do that with a document open in front of him.

"I am not going to do it," he said.

"Do what."

"**The thing I do.** I am not going to write down what anybody thinks that means. I have said that sentence in four rooms and a school corridor and a municipal service office in the Ninth Ward and I have said it seven times and it has cost me something every time, and I have just gone to look for the meaning of what Mr. Vale has said, and **there is nothing there to refuse.** There is no shape. It is a count and a design and a number and a word, and all four of those are on paper somewhere and none of them has a person in it, and there is no inference for me to decline to draw.

"I have never in my life had nothing to refuse." He put his hands flat on the table the way Tomas Vale had. "I have four questions I ask at a counter and I have a habit under them and the habit is Finding 3, and the habit is the only piece of professional training I have ever had, and I have just found out that there is a category of document it does not apply to, and I do not know what I am supposed to do with my hands."

---

Elias Rook found that he could not say the next sentence, and neither could anybody else, and it took him about a minute and a half to work out why, and the reason was not grief.

The next sentence was going to be a question, and there was nowhere to put it. He could not say *so the Office is wrong*, because the answer said the Office was not wrong, and he had spent ten weeks wanting somebody to be wrong and had been told on a telephone in a station in this city that being right is not the same as being safe, and now he had the other half of that sentence and it was sitting on a table in a records room.

He could not say *so we take it to a Deputy Director*, because it was not evidence about anything the Deputy Director was doing, and because a paragraph in a report that explains why a classification is not the whole cause has no person in it either, and would be true, and would not be an exoneration.

He could not say *so we tell the hall*, because the hall had decided on Thursday morning that it does not convene itself about a form, and because a hall that is told what a network gains by having fewer contradictions in it is a hall that has been handed a reason, and a reason is the fastest way there is to turn nineteen people into a committee.

He could not say *so we give it away*, because there was no person in it to give it away to.

He had asked a question on the wall since the second week of October, in chalk, in a nine-year-old's capitals, and it had two halves, and the second half had been a bracket with nothing under it since the third of November, and the whole of what had ever been written under it was *I DO NOT KNOW THE MEANING*.

He had got it. It was not a person and it was not a motive and it did not make anybody wrong and it could be shown to a Deputy Director in the words it was said in, and it could not be put on a wall with a year on it, and it could not be put in a proceeding, and it could not be given to the hall, and it could not be given away.

**It is a true thing, and there is nothing to do with it.**

---

Inez Sato came in at about ten past six, and she had not said she was coming, and she stood in the doorway and read the room in about four seconds the way she reads a room.

"Two things," she said. "One, I am not sitting down, and you are not going to tell me what was said, and I am not going to stop you telling me.

"Two. **You are not giving it to this hall.** I want that said out loud in a room in about two minutes so that it is on the page before anybody in this building hears it from a mouth instead of from a sheet of paper. And I am not deciding that because it is dangerous. I am deciding it because on Thursday morning I made a hall that does not convene itself about a form, and a hall that is handed an answer it did not ask for has been made into something it refused to be, and I would rather be the one who says so than have somebody in here at six o'clock explaining it to a nurse."

Nobody argued with her, and Elias Rook understood while she was saying it that she was right, and that the reason she was right was that she had said the same thing on Thursday for a completely different reason, and that was the third time in a fortnight that the correct decision in that building had been made for a reason that would not survive being explained.

Tomas Vale said, at about twenty past six, "Then it is yours and not ours and I have said it once, and I would like to go and stand in a corridor," and he went and stood in a corridor, and the four dark hours were three hours and forty minutes away, and the tin went back on the shelf with the band round it twice, and the round was on the nail, and the wall had three questions on it and a date at the bottom of it and room for about four more lines.

Elias Rook sat at the table after that with nobody in the room but the schedule and the tin, and did the thing he always did, which was to try it out against the week.

It came out cheap. That was his first surprise. He had carried nine weeks of weight on a frame with a man at one end of it, and the answer that had come in over the bridge that morning had no man at the end of it, and he had braced for the whole thing to get heavier, and it had not, and it was lighter than anything he has carried since September, and it was lighter because it did not weigh anything at all. Nobody had done it. Nothing had been done to anybody. A machine had done a thing it was built to do and had said so in one word, and every true thing the team had collected in ten weeks had been about *people refusing to be in sentences*, and this one was a sentence with everybody out of it and nothing in the empty place.

And he understood, at about half past six, why it was cheap, and it took him another four minutes to understand why that was the most frightening thing that had happened to him since the sixth of September.

Nobody had to hold it. A count with no name in it is the lightest object in the world. It fits in a coat and anybody can carry it.

**He believed, on Friday evening, that because there was no villain in it there was nothing in it to weigh anything. That was wrong, and he did not know it was wrong until the Saturday, and the reason he did not know it was wrong was that he had spent the whole afternoon being pleased.**

Nobody said it again that evening. It is not going to be said again in that building, and the reason is not that it has been written down.
