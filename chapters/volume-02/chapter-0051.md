# Chapter 51 — The Notice

Petar Lisk read the board at seven on Friday morning, the thirty-fourth day, and read the last line twice because it had been on the wall since Thursday night and nobody in the building had answered it.

**WHO EDITED THE KESTREL RESPONSE RECORD SO THAT A LIVING PASSENGER AND A WOMAN'S ROUTE WERE BOTH CALLED ADMINISTRATIVE ERRORS — AND WHAT DOES THE CONTINUITY OFFICE BELIEVE THAT A SINGLE APPROVED OUTCOME WOULD PREVENT?**

It is two questions on one line, and Petar Lisk is nine, and he had put a bracket under the second half of it on Thursday night because it is a different kind of question.

Underneath it, in Inez Sato's chalk, was a line saying the operation ended at ten minutes to six on Sunday, ten minutes before it began, that a seal went on at six with a woman in that chamber, and a name. **PERPETUA OYELARAN.** Nobody had crossed it out and nobody was going to.

The rest of it was the ordinary morning underneath all of that. Lena Mir did the round in fifty-four minutes with no physician in the building, wrote it on one page, and put it on the nail where anybody can read it. Ru Sabar's card went back at ten past six, as she has said every Friday since the second week. Four carded draws and the corridor light. Tarin Voss at the window with a book he has been eleven days from finishing. Cliff Marner asleep in a chair with his coat over him.

Emil Baros had come down the ramp at ten past five, the way he had every morning for fourteen days, and a man in a clean orange jacket met him at the fence and told him the ramp was inside the closure order and that he could not stand there.

He had asked what he was instead.

Nobody had an answer, so he came back up the ramp and sat down in his coat at ten past six. At a quarter to eight Elias Rook came through the door, and Emil Baros stood up and said, with no particular way of saying it, that he had been waiting all morning for somebody to ask him a question, and would they be quick about it, because he had been up since half past four for nothing.

Elias Rook, who has run a corridor for thirty-four days and has a left hand that has gone completely, has never had a morning with nothing to plan.

He stood in the front room and could not think of a single thing to do next. It went on for a quarter of an hour and it was physical — he kept reaching for a board that did not need writing on it, and looked at the corridor twice to see whether a light was out. Both times it was not. For thirty-four days every problem in that building had been a thing he could fix with his hands. What was on the table that Friday morning was paper, and paper does not have a place to stand.

Then it got better, and it got better for a reason he could not have put in a document.

---

The Deputy Director of the Havenport Continuity Office arrived in person at twenty to nine, which is a courtesy and also a tactic, and everybody who came out of that door knew which half was which.

His name was Nathan Rusk, he was fifty-nine, and he did not sit down for eleven minutes. In those eleven minutes he read the board, all of it, at the pace of a man who has read four hundred of them, and he stopped twice: at Tomas Vale's nine lines, which are about a building he has never been inside, and at the fourth promise on the wall, which says in a nine-year-old's capitals that Elias Rook is not the person who asks his own future for anything. When he got to the bracket Petar Lisk had put under the second half of the question, he read that too, then the first half again.

Then he said, "Which of these two is the one you have an answer for."

"The second one is the Office's," Elias said. "It is the only one of the two that is a question about somebody in this room."

"Correct," Rusk said, "and you answered it before I sat down, which I am going to write down." He took a folder from under his arm. "Deputy Director, Kestrel response 2042. I have two instruments, they are in one envelope on purpose, and the reason is on the front of the envelope and I am going to read it to you."

The envelope was on the table. It said, in a plain typed line: *Second instrument issued first because the second instrument does not require anybody to be right.*

"Okay," Elias said.

"The first is a **Notice of Inquiry, Kestrel Response 2042**. It has a case number, HC-42-0417, a schedule of three sittings — Thursday the fortieth, Saturday the forty-second, and a date in October I have not fixed, because I do not know what October looks like — and it has five questions."

"And the second."

"The second is an **Order of Impoundment**." Rusk put a single sheet on top of the first one. It was one page. "No finding. No test. No hearing. It is dated today, which is the only thing about it that required anybody to do anything. It takes property. It is faster than I am and it is not mine."

"Including what," Elias said.

"That is the clerk's paragraph and I am not going to improve on it in the room," Rusk said, and read it: four hundred words of careful language about designated-site records and equipment brought to a designated site, and at the end of it, in the part that mattered, the records of a residents' body.

Emil Baros, from the front room, said, "Is my name on that."

Rusk looked at him. "I do not know."

"That is a strange answer to give a man at seven in the morning."

"It is the correct one, and I would like to say in a transcript in October that I answered you honestly at seven in the morning. I have not read the schedule of property and I will not guess at it."

---

"I want to say the Office's position in plain words before anybody is asked anything," Rusk said, "because I have read a hall full of people who think somebody sent it and I did not."

"The corridor did not behave the way a corridor behaves. That is not my opinion, it is the Office's position, and it is in a file I did not write. You took a capacity that was offered to you — I am not going to pretend it was not, and I am not going to pretend you were entitled to it, because the entitlement is a question for a different room — and you made a decision with it inside a run of concrete that had already changed twice. And it is not a question about a rule. **I am not asking you whether you broke a rule. I am asking whether the thing you did made the ground do that.**"

Nobody said anything, and that was correct, because there was nothing to say that was not agreement or an argument about vocabulary.

"I will tell you three things," Rusk said. "One: Director Arden signed the seal and signed the classification and both stand, and I cannot move either of them. Two: I did not write the nine words. My office wrote them, in a classification workflow, a Director countersigned it, and a man named Vane put it in a file by hand and would not stop me. Three: if you can show me the classification is not the whole cause, I will put the whole cause in my own report, and that is the only thing I have got. I have a stamp on a site file and eleven people dead in it and one report to write in October, and I do not know whether the right one says *the classification was the cause* or *it was a description and the cause is somebody else's*."

"Then ask your questions," Elias said.

"After you ask me for them in writing," Rusk said. "It is a form of the Office's and not a courtesy, and if I hand them over on paper then every word in this room this morning is answerable and nothing in it is a conversation."

He put five numbered questions on the table face up, and Elias Rook read them aloud, in order, to a room that had gone quiet.

The first three were about the collapse Saturday, the plan the decision of the twenty-ninth day was taken under, and what was offered in the first week, by whom, with what boundary, and what was done with it. The fourth asked what accounts for twenty meters, between the thirty-one point zero on the ninth day and the eleven point zero on the twenty-second, in this room's words and anybody else's, and Rusk said he wanted both in the same hand if it could be.

The fifth one was not like the other four.

**Five: who altered the Kestrel response record.**

The room waited for it to be softer. It was not softer.

"The first four are questions about the past," Elias said. "The fifth is a question with a present tense in it."

"Yes," Rusk said.

"I am going to answer it now. It is going to be short and it is not going to satisfy you. The record says nine words — *catastrophic progressive structural failure attributable to legacy installation condition* — and that is a description of what failed. It is not a cause, and you cannot get a person out of a sentence that has no person in it. That is not a defense, it is arithmetic." He put his right hand flat on the table, because the left one is gone. "And I am not going to hand you a name for the fifth one today. I have got one field and not one hand. If I give you a name you will spend a month in a building instead of a month on paper, and it will be a worse month for you."

Rusk wrote for eleven seconds and read it back, which he said he had said he would do.

"That is in the file now, in your words and mine, and I have never had anybody hand me an answer they knew was bad. It is the first useful thing in this file."

"You have not read the file," Elias said.

"No," Rusk said. "I have read the index, which is longer than the file."

---

They said the rest of it in forty minutes and it was procedure. The five questions did not have to be answered by Friday. The inquiry could compel a person to attend and could not compel a person to sign. And at four minutes to eleven, when Rusk was at the door and did not go, Elias learned the last thing he learned that day.

"The impoundment is served Monday at eight in the morning. It is a cart and two clerks and a receipt, signed by a person who is not me and does not have to agree with anybody, and I have no power to hold it. I am telling you in advance because I would rather you had this in a conversation. And I will tell you the one thing about it that will not appear in a document, and then I am going, because it is the only advantage I have and I am spending it.

"The inquiry cannot get the residents' file. There is no box on the form. I have looked. It is not there because of a finding your own archivist made in the second week and adopted as office practice, and that same finding is why the medical page in that file has no name on it and is still a valid record. Both are one line of somebody's handwriting in the same file. I am bound by it and I am not proud of it."

"Somebody in this room thinks that is a good thing," Elias said.

"I have not decided," Rusk said. "I will decide by the fortieth day."

---

He was gone by eleven and the envelope was on the table and nobody in that building had signed anything.

Petar Lisk came over with the pencil he always has, because in thirty-four days he has never been in a room where something was said and gone unwritten. He stood on his toes to read the case number, then the fifth question, then looked up.

"There is a number on it."

"There is a number on it."

"Can I put the number up?"

"You can put the number up."

Petar Lisk wrote it under the question already on the wall, in his own capitals, and read out what he had written, which is what he does every morning and which nobody in that building has ever asked him to stop.

**HC-42-0417. THEY GAVE US A NUMBER FOR THE QUESTION. THAT IS THE FIRST TIME SOMEBODY HAS GIVEN US A NUMBER FOR ANYTHING.**

Then he looked at the second half of the old line, at the bracket under it, and said, "That one is still there."

"That one is still there," Elias said.

---

In the corridor afterwards, on the way down to the front room where the man with the empty function sat in his coat, Elias Rook said the thing out loud once, without a wall and without a witness, and did not say it again for eleven days.

"I do not know what to do this morning," he said. "I have not written one thing down since half past seven. If anybody asks me what happens next I am going to have to say *I do not know*, and I have never once said that sentence about a Friday."

Nobody in the corridor heard him, which is how he knows he said it out loud.
