# Chapter 64 — The Family-Reception Desk

The telephone rang in the corridor of the family-reception hall at eight forty on Thursday morning, the forty-seventh day, and it was Mara Okafor, and she had somebody with her.

"I am not going to take this in a corridor, so I am going to hold one. Put your back to a wall so the room does not get your face."

He put his back to a wall.

"I am staying. I said it on Monday in the words the rule requires and in the order it requires, and this is not that. This is a man's chest and a car. Imogen Sarkar has been looking at him since the thirtieth and she is here with me, and she has said that if she has to be talked out of it she will not argue with me, and I have not tried and am not going to try, because I want the answer to be the physician's and not mine."

"Understood."

"Here is the reason, and I am giving it to you because you are the person who will be standing in this building when I put it to him. **Seventeen days.** Four hours in a train in the third week with a change at Ash Junction, a man who cannot walk unaided, and a coach of strangers. I said no to that and I was right and I would say no again. Forty minutes on an ordinary road in a car with a driver and her in the back seat is not that journey. It is not the same question and I am not the same person I was on the ninth day.

"I am also not going to write that number down anywhere, and if you write it down I will take it out of your hand."

"That is yours to decide," Elias said.

"It is. And now I am putting it to him, and you are going to hear it and say nothing, and if you say something I will stop and say so in front of her."

He said nothing.

"Cliff," Mara said. "I am going to let you answer before I tell you anything. Do you want to go?"

"Yes."

"That is not the end of it and you knew that when you said it, which is why I asked. You have four hours in this building and a bed at the Cordell hospital at eight, and if you are not in that bed at eight you go back in the car and come back on Sunday, and I would like you to be the man who says it, because I will not be there and you will."

"I will be here," Elias said.

"You will not have a say," Mara said. "You will have a job. Do not confuse them, because I have watched a man confuse them this month and I did not stop him in time."

---

The regional transit family-reception hall is the north addition, and it has its own management, its own budget, its own queue, and a coffee machine that works.

Della Marner had been in the same chair by the same window since the sixth of September, day zero, and it was forty-seven days, and when Elias Rook came in she did not stand up. "You are the man who said the two words," she said. "Then I have not got up, because I have got a great deal of sitting left to do."

"She had a case number, a date, a pad, and a plastic wallet with a piece of paper in it that the transit authority gave her in the third week, saying the question of her husband had been referred to the Continuity Office in Havenport and had been acknowledged.

"That is the whole of what two institutions have ever handed me," she said. "One piece of paper saying the question has gone somewhere else. And a pad with a man's name on it in a young woman's hand, and I know what that is not."

Joon Park wrote it down without looking at it, because he had heard her voice come down a line on the eighth day, after Elias had spelled a name with two r's.

---

Yvette Roche was twenty-four and had been on that desk since the second week of September, and she came over when she heard the case number, because a case number is the only thing on that floor she can act on before the customer has finished sitting down.

"Three things," she said. "Do not tell me anything about what happened underground. I have got a queue, it is not my department, and I do not want it. I am going to look at what I am allowed to look at, tell you what is in it, and if it is not enough say so in the same words twice, so that you cannot sit here thinking there is more."

"That is fair," Elias said.

She came back in about nine minutes with a printout and put it face down on the table and did not turn it over.

"Here is what our system has," she said. "**Clifford Aldis Marner**, station attendant, Cordell terminus, rostered on and reporting for the early turn on Saturday the sixth of September. That is a roster. That is who was supposed to be in a building. He clocked on at four minutes to six in the morning and I have had that since the eighth and I have never been able to give it to anybody, because to give it to anybody I would have to say why I am looking, and there is no box on my form for why I am looking.

"And there is no incident recorded against that service on that date. **There is no incident.** There has never been an incident, because the service did not stop and the trains ran and four hundred and some people went to work on it that morning. I have been writing *unresolved* in a box on a case since the sixth of September and I did not know when I started writing it that it was going to be the whole of what there was."

She turned the printout over.

"That is the true thing," she said. "I have only got one of them and it is this, and I have said it once, and I am not saying it again in case either of you wants to hear it twice."

---

Della Marner read the printout twice with a finger on the line, and then said, "It says his name."

"It does," Yvette Roche said. "It is a roster and not an event, and if you take that to anybody who deals with incidents it is worth nothing, and I would like you to know that before you love it."

"I have been at this desk forty-seven days," Della Marner said. "I have been telling myself for six weeks that what I want is a document and not a person, and I would like the record to show that you have given me a piece of paper with his name on it and that I do not care in the slightest that it is not enough. It is paper. Somebody printed it and somebody has to sign for it."

---

Elias Rook named the four doors, because it was on the card the hall had made and she wanted to know whether the number was worth anything.

"Four," he said. "The end-of-line gate and platform at the terminus. The stationmaster's office at the Cordell end, a different room and a different key. The relay hut annexe on the approach, which is where a man goes if the platform signal is wrong. And the duty store where the keys are signed out, and the mess — and that is the one he used most, and it is not even a door anybody would call one."

"That is a number on a card," Della Marner said.

"It is, and it is in a system in this city right now, and it is the only reason I can tell you a badge exists. It is not a way to get a person in. He came up a corridor on a board on the thirtieth and the card did not do that. A woman in our hall wrote his name down twice in her own hand because she does not trust a name once, and a nineteen-year-old on a frame said a word that stopped us, and a hall counted him in a number that was not a count."

Della Marner looked at the printout.

"Write that down," she said. "Not the number — the rest of it. If it is not in a file anywhere then it is in your book."

Joon Park wrote it down, on the back of a case extract, in a hand that was not his and never would be.

---

The name on the paper took four minutes and it was ugly.

It was a family-reception case extract: a case number, the date it was opened, the name as the family gave it, a line for the relationship, and a status. The status field had four words available and the clerk put in the one that meant open, which was **unresolved**, and it was correct, and it was the fifth of the five honest words, and the only one of the five written by somebody with a queue in front of her and a woman eleven feet away.

Yvette Roche signed it, because a clerk signs the extract and not the family, and then asked for the copy to go to the family's address and not to an office, because an extract sent to an office is a document about a case and an extract sent to a house is a document about a woman.

Della Marner put it in the plastic wallet with the other one, did not say thank you, said "Right," and then said, "Is he all right."

"I do not know anything about that and I am not going to tell you I do," Yvette Roche said, "and if you come back to this desk I am going to be the same person and say the same thing."

---

He came at half past four.

A hospital car, a driver, and a woman in a dark coat who was Imogen Sarkar, and Cliff Marner in the back seat with his coat folded over the blanket and a frame he had not been in on the thirtieth, and he came out of that car slower than a man comes out of a car.

He had been seventeen days out and he was fifty-six pounds and going up, and the trousers he had on belonged to somebody else, and he had put on the shoes he had once asked a doctor to correct in person at a desk in two thousand and eleven because the spelling of his own name had gone wrong in a booking system. He had told that story once in five weeks and he did not tell it again.

Two people in that hall had never met each other: a fifty-eight-year-old station attendant from the Cordell terminus, and a twenty-four-year-old clerk who had written his name in a young hand on a pad on the ninth day of September because a woman in another city had asked her to, and had asked whether the lady wanted the surname spelled a particular way.

They met at a counter, because that is where a hall makes people meet, and neither of them had asked for that. Cliff Marner said, "You are the one who wrote it," and Yvette Roche said, "I did, and I asked her whether she wanted it spelled a particular way, and she said no, write it the way he said it, and I have thought about that every day since, and I have never once known what I was being brave about."

Then he sat with his wife for fifty minutes with a plastic wallet open on her knee, and Elias Rook stood six feet off and did not listen, because listening to a thing that is not yours is a form of taking.

---

At half past seven Elias Rook said it, and he had practised it in a stairwell at six o'clock and it came out flat.

"Four hours. Bed at the Cordell hospital at eight. I am the man saying it and I am saying it because a physician in another city asked me to and I am not the one who decided it."

Cliff Marner looked up from a piece of paper with his own name on it that his wife was holding.

"I know," he said. "I have been counting since two o'clock and I knew it was going to be you and not her, and I want to say something about that and then I am going to go, because she has forty-seven days of things to say and I have got a bed at eight."

"Say it."

"You are the fifth one," Cliff Marner said. "The first four had their reasons. You have got hers."

Elias Rook felt better about it than he had felt about anything since the thirtieth, and understood about four seconds later, on the edge of the step, that feeling better about it was not what had happened.

He had come forty minutes and got a piece of paper, a roster, and a number of doors. The woman holding the paper had said *right* and had asked whether he was all right, and neither of those was what he had come for. What he had come for was a hall that had gone five weeks without him in it, standing up on its own two feet, doing the ordinary work, and him not being in it.

He was relieved. It did not help at all, and he noticed that too, and wrote it down in the car with the tin in the boot and the date on the page.
