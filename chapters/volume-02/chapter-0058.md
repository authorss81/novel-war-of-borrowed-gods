# Chapter 58 — Nobody Has Read It

The demand for documents came on Friday morning, the forty-first day, and it came to the records room in an envelope from Counsel Solano and it was eleven pages.

It named every document in the team's custody, individually. Not by category. The notebook, described. The medical ledger, described. The raw audio, by its file name. The manifest. The witness statements, plural, and each of the nine by the surname the witness gave. The survey sheets, plural, and the three that were never printed, by the numbers on them. The refusal log, complete. Both of Tomas Vale's nine-line documents, separately, because they are two documents. The residents' file, separately, as *a document held by a third party*. And the movement record, and the Bell file, and the consent cards, and a page of the ward's power sheet, and a thing nobody had known was a document, which was the wall.

Not one item on the eleven pages was Tomas Vale's notebook of admissions.

Joon Park noticed it at nine twenty and put it on the table face up and said the thing that was going to be the shape of the whole morning.

"It is not on the list," he said. "Not withheld. Not *to be produced subject to argument*. It is not on a list of eleven pages made by a woman who has a very good index. It is not on the list."

Tomas Vale looked at it for a while.

"Then it is free," he said.

Nobody said anything.

"It is free," Tomas said, "and anything free is suspect, and I have been on the receiving end of free for eight years and it has cost me a career and a name in a file and four jacks in a hole. So I am going to say the thing out loud, in a room, with two people in it who will write it down, and then I am going to stop talking about it."

---

There is a notebook of admissions.

It is in a cardboard box in a garage on an ordinary street in this city, taped in the side of the box, and it went into that box in 2034 with a write-off report that the Office cross-indexed to Tomas's name in the third week of September, and it has not been read by anybody, and it has not moved, and it is eight pages.

He said all of that in about forty seconds, standing up, with Mara Okafor on one side of him and Joon Park on the other, and Elias Rook in the doorway with his right hand on the frame.

"I am telling you today because yesterday a Deputy Director demanded every document in your custody by name and my name is in that envelope, and the document is not in it, and I would rather hand it over than be found to have kept it. That is the whole reason. It is not brave and I would like nobody to write down that it was brave."

"Who wrote it," Elias said.

"I did."

"When."

"Over four years, in the third year of it, mostly at night, at a kitchen table, when I had made a decision I could not justify to a person and I could not justify it to a form, and so I wrote down the reasoning instead." Tomas put his hands flat on the table. "Eight pages. That is all I am going to say about it today, including whether it is important, and I would like it noted that I am not going to say whether it is important, and that the not-saying is not a strategy, it is that I do not know."

"Can it be read," Mara said. "Not by us. Not today. Can it be read at all, in a room, with two people in it — say my name and Joon's name — and nobody else, and no copy, and no summary for anybody afterward?"

"Yes," Tomas said.

"Will you agree to that?"

"Yes."

"Then I am not agreeing," Mara said.

"Nor me," Joon Park said, and then, because he is Joon Park and because nine days in Volume 02 he had watched a field get filled in by somebody who was not there, "and I want the refusal on the page and I want it in the same ink as the asking, and I want the reason, and the reason is that we asked a man to hand over the only document nobody has read, on the day after an order that did not ask for it, and if we do it in the same week then in five years it is a story about a team that was given a question and hurried to answer it. I am not saying no forever. I am saying not this week, and I am saying it out loud, and Petar is going to write it down at seven, and it is going to say *asked, and the two people he named both said no, and he said no as well.*"

Nobody agreed, including him, and the notebook stayed in a box in a garage, and it was still unread at the end of the year.

Elias Rook, in the doorway, had said nothing at all, which took him more effort than anything he did on Friday.

He had wanted to say *I will take care of it.* It is the sentence he has said forty-one times. It is the sentence that has a person's future in it. It is on a wall in nine-year-old capitals as a rule and he had four of his own things up there and one of them is *he is not the person who asks his own future for anything*, and asking a woman on a telephone in another city to wait for him was the same sentence with a different person in it, and he caught it about four seconds in, and he said the other one instead.

"Two of us will carry the box to your kitchen table," he said, "and put it on it, and that is the whole of what I will do with it until somebody else decides."

---

He was suspended from seam assignment at twenty past two on Friday afternoon, at the Rescue Service depot on the far side of the estuary, in a conversation that took four minutes and had a form in it.

It is a polite way of saying the most skilled man in that building is going to count rubber.

"Your designation and medical endorsement are unaffected," said the duty officer, who had been in the ash Wednesday and did not make it a speech. "Seam assignment is suspended, effective today, under the designated-site conduct condition, pending inquiry. You are reassigned to hose certification and inventory control at this depot. Tuesdays and Fridays. The hours are the hours. The pay is the pay."

"Pending what," Elias said.

"Pending the outcome of HC-42-0417," said the duty officer, "which I have been told about and have not been asked any questions on, and which I am putting in your paperwork as a number and not as a reason, because I do not know the reason and you will tell me it is confidential and then we will both have wasted a minute."

Elias Rook took the paperwork and did not argue about it in the room.

He has been asked since — by Inez Sato, by Petar Lisk, once by Cliff Marner — why he did not argue, and he has given the same answer each time, and it is the true one and it is not a good one.

"Because I was asked about a corridor," he said, "on the twenty-ninth day, and I said a thing to myself in a hall, and I did not get anything out of it, and the four-C line went out of a socket in this building and stayed out for twenty-seven hours and thirty-nine minutes and it took my sister's voice with it. And I would have argued about a hose test this afternoon if arguing did anything, and arguing has never once done anything, and every time I have refused something in five weeks it has cost a person who was not in the room a thing I did not know about. So I took the hose test."

---

The correction happened at about half past three, on a bench outside the depot, and it is on the record because Elias Rook wrote it down and Petar Lisk read it at seven and copied it into four buildings by Sunday.

Anja Ro said it, and she said it to nobody, on the way out to the van, in the way people talk when four weeks of a corridor have made them say things out loud that they would not have said in September.

"That man in the corridor. The one behind the wall. He is a name somebody decided not to have."

Joon Park said, "No."

"He is —"

"I know what he is," Joon said. "And I am going to say this once and then nobody is going to talk about it again, and if it gets talked about again I will say it again and I will be as unpleasant about it the second time.

"That is the one man in this whole business who decided. At four o'clock on the last Thursday on the schedule, he was asked whether the hall would record him, and he said no, and the hall did not record him, and the reason is in a book with a date on it, and my initials are on it, and there is no name on it, and that is not an absence — a refusal is a record, that is Finding 3, that is the Office's own practice, and I wrote it down. He did not have a name taken from him. He took it out. And the day after tomorrow somebody with a case number is going to come to this building and ask us to prove a thing, and the thing is a record that a person decided, and it is a record, and it is in chalk, and it is the only one either of us will ever get to keep."

Then he got in the van, and nobody said anything else about it, and it was not discussed again in that building until the fourth column went up on the wall in June and somebody asked what a *refused* column was for.

---

Mara Okafor came out to the far bank at ten to five and did not say why, and sat on the tailgate of the van while he did a hose test on a fifty-meter length of forty-five millimeter, which is a number you cannot fake and cannot guess and that he has written down wrong in a logbook for twenty years.

He wrote it as *two hundred and ten*, because the number was in his head, and it is a number that has been in a hundred documents in this city and it is two hundred and ten meters of migrating void under a street people walk over, and it is the number a man's leg feels like in the dark.

Mara read the logbook over his shoulder.

"That is wrong."

"That is what it tested at."

"That is what you wrote," she said. "It is wrong. It is one hundred and eighty-two. And you have written two hundred and ten because you have had two hundred and ten in the front of your head for five weeks, and the number in your hand is the number you have been carrying and not the number the instrument read, and I know that because I have watched you read a chart wrong twice this month in the same way."

He looked at it for a long time. Then he took the pen and drew a single line through his own figure and wrote hers beside it, and signed it, and put the date on it.

"Every day from now on," he said, "that is going to be the number I get wrong, and I am going to write it down every time, and in a year it is going to be a piece of evidence in a file about me, and somebody is going to be able to read it and see exactly what I was thinking about on the fortieth day."

"Yes," Mara said.

She did not say anything else about it, and he did not ask her what she had come out to the far bank for, and she did not tell him, and the two of them sat on the tailgate of a van in a depot yard for about eleven minutes while a hose was drained, and neither of them said a single word about the fourth week, or about a woman on the Meridian, or about what a single approved outcome prevents, and both of them had been thinking about all three of those things the entire time.

There is a wall on Ash Street with twenty-eight lines in a stranger's chalk in the fourth column, and a name in it that nobody can reach, and a name in it that is asking to be found, and neither of those two people in the yard had a way to make either of them better, and one of them had been suspended from the work she was good at eleven days after refusing an offer that would have worked, and neither of them said so out loud, because neither of them was going to be the one who said it first.
