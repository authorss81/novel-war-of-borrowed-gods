# Chapter 78 — The Third Time

The primary school on Sycamore Row is one story and it was built in about 1958 by men who were in a hurry, and the corridor is eleven meters long and has a radiator on the north side that ticks, and at half past four on a Thursday in the first week of November every classroom is dark and every door is locked except the one at the end with a wired glass panel in it.

Hester Lund was mopping the corridor with a mop that had a frayed head, and she was sixty-eight, and she let them in without asking who they were, which is a thing you do in a building you have had the keys to for eleven years.

"I'm not going to talk in here," she said, at the end of the first minute. "You'll want to come and stand where I can see the yard, and I'll tell you why I say that: two of you are about to ask me a thing, and I am sixty-eight, and I would rather not do it with a wall behind me and a window in front of me. It costs the other person nothing to be reasonable about it and it costs me quite a lot."

So they stood at the end of the corridor with the yard door open behind them and the radiator ticking, and the light was a strip tube over a cupboard and it was not good light.

Elias Rook spoke first, before anybody asked anything, and he said it out loud, and he said it to her, and it is the rule and it applied to him.

"Before we ask you anything," he said, "I am going to tell you the whole of what is going to happen and what is not, in the words it is going to happen in, and then it is your turn and not mine.

"There is a Deputy Director's inquiry in my city with a case number on it, and there is a page in a paper book in a records center in this city, and your name is at the bottom of line forty-one on it. We found it on Monday. We have not written your name down anywhere, and we are not going to, and the only reason we are in this corridor is that we were not willing to use the page without asking you one question first.

"**If we use the page, your name goes into a proceeding.** That is the deal and there is no way around it, because the page has your signature on it and a signature is a name. So we are not going to do it, and that is not a favor to you. It is the price of the page, and we are paying it, and you are going to be poorer at the end of this conversation than you were at the start of it, and I would rather you heard that from a stranger on a Thursday than found it in a summary in December."

"Thank you," Hester Lund said, and she went on mopping, which is what she did while she thought.

"Two men have already been asked about that night by two institutions," she said. "That is before you. I will tell you what that was like and then you can decide whether you want to hear it, and if you do not want to hear it I will not be offended and you can go and stand in the yard for as long as you like."

"We would like to hear it," Joon Park said.

---

He asked one question, and he asked it because he is the one who asks, and that had been six weeks of practice and it was the only reason the corridor had a chance.

"Do you remember the night?"

"Which night," Hester Lund said. "You have not told me which night, and I am not going to be the woman who fills in a gap to be helpful. If you tell me which night I will tell you what I remember about that night, and if you cannot tell me which night then you are asking a person a question about a thing you have not looked at, and I have been asked that three times and I have answered it three times and it is the only question in this business I have ever wanted to walk away from."

Joon Park did not fill it in. That is the whole of what he did, and it took him about four seconds, and it was the hardest thing he has done in nine weeks.

"The seventeenth of October, 1998," he said. "A relay cabinet on the wall at the corner of this yard, on the Civic Spine maintenance run, at twenty to five in the morning, and you signed line forty-one at four minutes past four."

"Oh," Hester Lund said. "**That** night. Yes. I remember that night."

And then she gave it to them in the approved form.

That is the thing about it that neither of them had expected, and Joon Park wrote all of it down in his notebook in the order she said it and read it back to her before he closed it. She did not give it in her own words. She gave it in the form, in the same order and very nearly the same words, in a voice that was flat and reasonably quick, the way a person gives a thing they have given before.

She said that a cabinet on the wall at the corner of the school yard had been opened and worked on, and that she was asked to sign for it because she was the person on site and the man on the run had gone home, and that she had asked at the time whether her name was the right one to go in that column and been told that it was the name on the building. She said that she had been asked whether she had observed the work being carried out, and had answered that she had been on site and had not observed it. She said that she did not know the purpose of the work. She said that she had not been asked to say anything about who had requested it, and that if she had been asked she would have given the same answer again, which was that she did not know.

"That is what I said to the district in a form," she said. "And I said it to the operator in a form. Same five questions. Same order. It is a form they have, and there is a right answer on it, and the right answer is what I said, and I have got quite good at it, and I am not going to be a third kind of woman for a third institution, so you are getting the form, and if you want the other one you will have to ask me for the other one and I will not give it to you for a question you have not asked."

Joon Park read the whole of it back. He added nothing.

---

And then she said the thing that made the corridor worth the bus fare.

"You are not going to ask me a second question."

"No," Elias Rook said.

"That is the third time in nine weeks somebody has not asked me a second question, and the first two were a district man and an operator's man and I want you to understand what it does to a person." She put the mop against the wall. "Everybody wants the second question. The second question is the one they did not write down. **You have got the form. I have given you the form. And now there is a second question, and it is hanging in this corridor, and it is the only thing in this business I have ever wanted to be asked and I am not going to ask you for it.**"

Elias Rook stood there with his right hand at his side and said nothing at all, which is what he has been practicing since the second week of September, and it is the only thing he does in this corridor that is not ordinary.

So Hester Lund told them about the vans, and she did not have to, and nobody had asked.

"There were two vans in the yard that night," she said. "That is not in any form. I have never written it in any form and I am not going to now. There were two vans in the yard and one of them had its interior light on, and I looked out and thought somebody had come out to lock it and had left it on, and I went out and locked it for them, and I did the second one too because it was the same night and I was already out. **That is all it is. It does not name anybody. It does not mean anything. There is a light on in a van in a school yard at twenty to five in the morning and I locked two vans. In thirty years of being the one who is here when everybody has gone home I have never told anybody that, and there is no reason in the world for me to be telling two strangers from another city in a corridor.**"

Joon Park wrote it down in his own hand, and then he stopped, and read what he had written, and looked up.

"Ma'am, I am not going to write what anybody thinks that means. I do that in every room I go into and I have done it four times in nine weeks and this is the fifth and it is the first one I have had to do about a pair of vans. **We do not know what that was.** It could have been a heating pipe and a night cleaner. It could have been nothing at all and a tired woman with a key ring. It could have been the most important thing anybody has said to me this year, and I do not know that, and if I write it down as though I did then in four years somebody is going to read a sentence I made out of a woman's two vans and believe that I found it somewhere, and it will be the first document I have ever put in a book that I could not show anybody where it came from."

"You are the third one," Hester Lund said. "I have had a district man and a man from the operator and neither of them said that to me, and one of them said the other thing, which is *of course we understand*, and I would rather have what you said than what he said, and I am aware that what you said is only a rule and not a kindness."

"It is only a rule," Joon Park said.

"I know. That is why it is worth something."

---

Elias Rook did not say much in that corridor. He said the rule out loud before anything was asked, and he did not ask a second question, and at about twenty past five he said thank you and she said the mop was going to be put back and would they mind walking out through the yard because the front door sticks after four o'clock.

On the bus at twenty-five past five he worked out what it was that had happened in there, and it took him until the far side of the estuary.

She had not refused him. She had not helped him. She had been a woman in a corridor who was given a question and answered it in a form because that is the form, and had then told them about two vans for no reason at all, and had asked for nothing, and had locked two vans.

It is easier to be in a room with a person like that than with a person who is refusing you, and it is a great deal easier than with a person who is helping you, and the reason is that neither of the other two is finished. A refusal is a door. A favor is a debt. A woman who answers in a form and then tells you about two vans has not given you a door or a debt.

He has spent nine weeks being extremely careful about who he is owed something by. In the fourth week a woman in a corridor in Cordell said *it is not a favor* before either of them could thank her, and it has been the best sentence anybody has said to him since the collapse, and this was not that either.

---

At half past nine on Thursday night the hall on Ash Street had four dark hours two hours off and Petar Lisk was reading the board, and there were three questions on it and none of them had been answered, and on a table in the front room there was a tin with a rubber band round it twice and a notebook in it with six words in Elias Rook's handwriting that nobody in that building was ever going to be able to use, and about two inches by one of nothing at all.
