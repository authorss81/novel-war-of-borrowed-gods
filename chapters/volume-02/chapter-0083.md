# Chapter 83 — Seventy-One, And A Counter

The hose held on the Tuesday at twenty-five minutes to six and the foreman wrote the time next to his own name and told him the next coupling had a thread in it he would want a wrench for, and it was the third Tuesday in a row that the slot had been his and the third Tuesday in a row that he had been on it at twenty-five minutes to six, and R. Dunleavy had worked out on the first one that a man who turns up four minutes early and says nothing about it is a man who is not going to be asked, and had not asked.

The inventory half of the job was his as well. He went over the bridge in the early afternoon with the week's sheet in a folder, and Kessler Hardware is four streets from the bridge end, and the bridge is the reason he had bought the thing he was about to buy, because the records room door on Ash Street has not latched since the fourth week of October and has been held shut with a folded blanket since, and a folded blanket in a records room is a thing somebody is going to say something about one day.

---

Kessler Hardware is at 112 Ninth Street and it is four doors down from the district service office, and it has been on that street since about 1971, and the sign is the original with the K half of it sun-bleached.

He went in at ten past two on the Tuesday afternoon with the folder under his arm, and he had thought about the folder on the bus, twice, and had decided twice that he was going to put it in the satchel and leave the satchel in the doorway, and it was under his arm when he came through the door, and he noticed that and did not put it down, and could not afterwards work out whether that was a decision or just a thing he had not got round to.

The shop was warm and smelled of sawdust and paraffin and it had a long counter down one side with a wheel display at the end of it, and there were two people in it: a boy of about nineteen on a ladder putting stock on a high shelf, and an old man on the far side of the counter who was not the boy.

The old man had a badge on an apron string and the badge said **D. PREWITT**, and he was doing the one job on a Tuesday afternoon that nobody can do quickly, which is putting the returns away.

Elias Rook stood in the middle of the shop for about four seconds deciding, and what he decided was that he was not going to pretend to be a customer, because being a customer is a form and the last eleven weeks of his life have been about who decides what goes on a form.

"Mr. Prewitt," he said. "My name is Elias Rook. I am the man who is on the wall in a hall in the Cinder Quarter. I am not here about anything you have done."

Duane Prewitt put a box of washers down on the shelf behind him and did not move.

"I am also not here to ask you a question," Elias Rook said, "and I am going to tell you the whole of what I am here for, and then I am going to buy a roll of tape, and you can go back to the returns."

"Go on."

"There is a form. It is a form of the Continuity Office, and it is a form for when something happens at a site, and it is a form for a very old kind of incident. It has a line on it for somebody signing on behalf of a person who cannot sign, and that line was added to that form thirty-nine years ago, and I am trying to find out what that line was for, and I am not asking you about it, and I am not going to mention it again."

"You just mentioned it," Duane Prewitt said.

"I did," Elias Rook agreed. "That was a mistake and I would like to go on to the tape."

"Two-ninety for the roll and the ties are a pound ten and I would not bother with the small ones, they are rubbish." He put the box down. "Are you the one that was in the papers about the tunnel."

"I was in a corridor in the tunnel."

"Mm," said Duane Prewitt, and did not say anything else about that for the rest of the conversation.

---

The thing Elias Rook understood, standing at that counter with a roll of brown tape and a packet of cable ties on it, is that the team has no standing over this man.

Not one form. Not one card. Not one line in anybody's column. There is no process here that anybody can start, and no order anybody could serve on him, and no document in which his name could be a line, and no hearing in which he could be a witness for the team or against the team. In ten weeks Elias Rook had learned to count the ways in. It had never once occurred to him to stand in a shop and find that there were none, and it turns out that is not a relief. It is the specific and particular horror of a man with no way in: you cannot help him and you cannot use him, and you cannot even put a question to him that would be any use, and so the only thing available to you is to buy something and stand there.

"What was it you wanted to know," Duane Prewitt said.

"Nothing."

"Nothing at all?"

"I have got a night in January two thousand and three in a book in a municipal cupboard and a signature in it. **I am not going to show it to you and I am not going to say the name on it.** And I have to tell you the second half of that, which is that what I have just said to you is not true.

"The signature is in the book. The signature is a man's. I have known that since yesterday afternoon and I have come into this shop and let you stand behind a counter and believe there is nothing here about you. I did that because you are seventy-one and you work three days a week and there is nothing whatever I could do with the truth, and because I found out on Monday that a man in a hurry is not a villain, and I did not want to be standing in front of the reason while I said it.

"That is the whole of my dishonesty. It took about eleven seconds, and it cost me nothing, and you are entitled to know it, because you had already decided on your own that a man who comes into a shop talking about a form in a municipal cupboard is a man with something, and I have just made your decision correct."

"You did not have to tell me that," Duane Prewitt said.

"No," Elias Rook said. "I did not."

Duane Prewitt looked at him for a while.

"I told you I don't remember," he said.

"You have not told me anything."

"You are going to ask me."

"No."

"Then I will tell you, because you have been standing at that counter for four minutes and you are not going to leave, and because you have told me you are not going to ask, and a man who tells you he is not going to ask has bought himself about as much as a roll of tape." He put his hands on the counter. "**I do not remember. I have been trying to remember that night for about twenty years and I cannot remember a single thing about it, and I want that written down somewhere, because I have been telling people for twenty years that I do not remember and they all decide I am being difficult about it.**

"And I remember the floor. I remember the floor and I remember that I went down on it twice, and I remember thinking — and I was right about that, and I have never been wrong about it — that there was not one thing on that floor anybody could have written down. And I remember thinking that if I had to leave him on it to go and look for a box on a piece of paper, then whatever I wrote on that paper afterwards was going to be about me.

"That is the whole of what I have got. Thirty-nine years and a floor and about nine minutes."

---

Elias Rook did not ask the second question.

He has not asked the second question since the sixth of November in a school corridor in another city, and he was not going to start in a shop on Ninth Street with a man of seventy-one behind a counter, and the reason is not chivalry. The reason is that the second question is the one somebody did not write down, and a man who is seventy-one and works three days a week in a shop is the last person on earth who should be handed the second question by a stranger who wants something.

"I am not going to ask you anything else," he said. "I have got a rule and it is not a good one, it is just a rule, and I am telling you it exists so that you know the shape of the conversation and not the shape of me."

"Fine," said Duane Prewitt. "Then take your tape. And the small ties are rubbish."

---

Joon Park was outside on the pavement at ten past three with a notebook he had not opened.

"Nine and nine," he said. "The district that asked for the line is District 9, and the ward is the Ninth Ward, and the district office is on Ninth Street, and the shop is four doors down from the district office. I have been sitting on a bench for an hour thinking about the fact that there are three nines in two hundred yards of street and **I am not going to tell you what the three nines are, because I do not know, and because a man who finds a meaning in a street name has not found a meaning, he has found a coincidence and got excited about it.** Nine and nine is not a reason for anything. It is an address."

"Did you write it down."

"I wrote down that I noticed it," Joon Park said, "and that is a different sentence, and I have been sitting on this bench for an hour deciding whether the two sentences are close enough to be the same sentence, and I have decided that they are not, and I have written down that I decided it, and if I am wrong then somebody in four years can see the moment I decided it and can see what I had."

He closed the notebook.

"He is seventy-one and he works three days a week and he is not a resource," he said. "We have no standing over him, we have no line to refuse on, we cannot give him a card and he could not want one, and there is no document in the world that could put his name in a proceeding. **He is a person we went to see, and that is the end of it, and if anybody in this business ever writes him down again they are going to be doing the thing this whole volume is about, and I would like the sentence in the notebook so that it is on the page before somebody wants it.**"

---

He came up the ramp on Ash Street at ten past six on the Tuesday evening and there was a car at the curb with a hospital plate on it and a driver standing in the cold with his hands in his pockets, and inside the front room there was a man of fifty-eight in a chair by the window who had come up a corridor on a board on the thirtieth day at twenty past eight in the evening at fifty-one pounds and had said his own name twice, and who had been in Cordell for thirty-six days, and who was home.

Petar Lisk had a card out and a pencil and was waiting for somebody to tell him what to write at the top of it, and Elias Rook looked at the card and then at the man in the chair and could not think of one word to put on the top of it that would not be a lie.

"Don't," Cliff Marner said, without opening his eyes. "I know what you were going to do. You were going to write *recovered*, and you have been doing that for ten weeks about the wrong people."

"I was going to write *home*," Elias Rook said.

"Write *home*," Cliff Marner said. "And put the time on it, because Mr. Park will want the time, and put the date on it, and do not put anything else on it, and do not put my wife's name on it, because she is forty minutes away and she has been at a desk since the sixth of September and she is not a field on a card."

Petar Lisk wrote it and initialed the time, and that is the whole of what a man of fifty-eight who came up on a board asked for in his own first four minutes in a building he had been carried out of in September, and he asked for all four things in one breath and none of them was about himself.
