# Chapter 71 — The Ordinary Week

Elias Rook wrote two hundred and ten on a form on Tuesday morning, and the form meant two hundred and ten, and the man who countersigned it did not say one word about the number because there was nothing to say about it.

It was a test pressure on a hose, in a column marked TEST PRESSURE, in pounds, and the hose went on a coupling off a rack and it either held or it did not and this one held for nine minutes and then went back on the rack, and the foreman wrote the time next to his name and told him the next fitting had a thread in it he would want a wrench for.

The whole of it took forty minutes. He had done harder things in a wet stairwell in the second week of September with no light and one working hand, and been better at that.

He came back on Thursday for the other half of the job, which was inventory: a lamp count, a battery count, and a form.
---

The hall had a pencil on a string, and Inez Sato told him on his first morning that the pencil was not the hall's.

"The pencil is the office's," she had said. "The string is mine. If the pencil goes I want it written down in the room that it went, and I do not want anybody to write down who took it, because then the next person does not take it, and I would rather we ran out of pencils in April than lose the habit of taking one."

There were four carded draws on Thursday and three were signed in a hand he did not know, and the fourth was Ru Sabar's, and Ru Sabar's had a time on it in her own writing — *18:10* — because her condition is a condition and not a preference, and a preference is a thing you can talk somebody out of.Elias Rook wrote the two hundred and ten on the Thursday form as well, in the inventory column, in his own right hand, and it took him a second and a half, and he had spent six weeks with a number in his head that was not a hose.

---

Mara Okafor came into the front room at half past ten on the Thursday with the round sheet in her hand and read the form over his shoulder while he was still holding the pen. She read it for about four seconds.

"There is nothing wrong with that page," she said.

"No."

"Two hundred and ten pounds."

"Yes."

"And you have written it four times this week."

"Three times this week and once on the sample sheet." He turned the top sheet over and pushed it across. "It is the same figure, and it is not the same number as the two hundred and ten meters under a street, and I want it in writing on this form that they are two different numbers, and I am aware that I am a man writing a line on a form about a distinction nobody in this building has ever confused."

Mara Okafor took the pen out of his right hand, which she is allowed to do, and put a tick in the margin against his signature, and handed it back.

"That tick means the hose passed," she said. "It does not mean the sample sheet agrees with the form. Nobody will read it as meaning that, because everybody in this building knows what a tick on a hose form means, and because you are here on Friday and on Tuesday and there are going to be four more of them."

He put the pen down and she went and did the round, and the tick is on the form, and it is the only notation in nine weeks that has meant exactly one thing.

---

He asked for his slots back on Thursday, in writing, on the depot's own form, with the dates on it, and he initialled the request and put his service number at the bottom.

The letter came back on Friday morning and it is four lines long.

> *Tuesdays and Fridays, hose certification and inventory, effective from the fourth. Your Tuesday slot is returned to you, 06:30, and your Friday slot is returned to you, 06:30.*
> *These will not be held twice. If you do not come, the slot goes to the next man who asks and I do not hold a list.*
> *You are the only man in this building who has asked in writing and asked for both at once and not asked me to wait. I will put that in no file, because there is no file, and I am telling you anyway.*
> *— R. Dunleavy, foreman*

Elias Rook read the third line four times in a kitchen on Ash Street at ten past seven on a Friday morning and did not show it to anybody, and it went into the notebook in the front of the book with the envelope, and he did not write anything under it.

There is no file. That was the point of the third line. A thing with no file behind it is the only kind of kindness this month that cannot be produced by anybody later, and he has spent nine weeks building a practice out of things that *can* be produced.

---

He copied the four sheets out longhand on Wednesday evening and again on Thursday, into a second copy for the tin.

It took him the two evenings. It is nineteen lines in nineteen hands and it is the hardest transcription he has ever done by hand, and he got it wrong twice, and the second time he got it wrong he got it wrong the way a person gets it wrong who is looking at a thing instead of copying it, because the four sheets do not change. Four lines of certified count, the printed headings, two words, and four lines about a line in a book. He wrote them all out and there was nothing in his hand at the end of it that had not been on the wall at ten to ten on Tuesday morning.

He understood something about it at about eleven o'clock on Thursday, sitting on the floor of the records room with his back against a filing cabinet that was not his and never would be, and what he understood was this.

They had two copies now. A copy on a wall that nobody owns, and a copy of that copy on a shelf in a local-history room in a county where the Havenport Continuity Office does not have a building, in a box with a card on it that says the papers are not to be removed without the written consent of the people who wrote them.

Both of them are now the only copies.

That is not a redundancy. A redundancy is a second thing you have got so that the loss of the first one is survivable, and nobody in Cordell and nobody in Havenport can serve an order on either of these, and so neither of them can be produced, and so if either of them is wrong there is no way on earth to find out. Six weeks ago Elias Rook had eleven boxes in a garage and eleven ways of being wrong that he could check. He has a wall that cannot be impounded and a library shelf that cannot be served on, and neither of them can be asked a question.

A record that cannot be taken is a record that cannot be corrected.

He wrote that in the notebook, in his right hand, and read it back to himself in an empty room, and it did not get better.

---

The post came on Thursday in the middle of the afternoon, in a brown envelope with a Cordell postmark and no sender's name on it, and the address was written on the front in a small neat hand.

> *Store attendance*
> *Shelf 3*

Inside there was one card, about the size of a playing card, with a number on it and four words printed at the top.

It is the copy number. The store-certified copy of the movement book, made under a store attendance instead of under the center, at two dollars forty instead of three dollars ten, by a woman of fifty-seven who has never done it in nineteen years and who asked for two things and got one.

One of them was *do not thank me.*

The other was *send the copy number to the day book on the shelf so that one entry in that card index was made by somebody who was asked.*

Elias Rook stood in the corridor at twenty past two on a Thursday afternoon holding a playing card in his right hand and read the number on it four times, and then he went down to the telephone on the shelf that only receives, and asked a directory whether a records center forty minutes and an ordinary road away had a shop on it, and it did not, and whether it had a library, and there was one in the county seat with a local-history room and a telephone number in a book on the desk that was older than the war.

It rang. A woman said the local-history room.

Elias Rook said: "A woman in your records center asked you to log a number in a day book, and I am the man she asked me to tell you about, and I would like to give you the number now, and I would like it written down that I gave it to you."

There was a pause of about two seconds, and then the woman said, "Go ahead," in the voice of somebody who is going to write this on a card with a date on it and is not going to make a speech about it.

He read the number out. She read it back. She spelled the shelf the way it is spelled and he spelled it the way it is spelled and they were the same.

"Four hundred and thirteen," she said, when he asked whether she wanted the entry number. "It will be in the book on Monday. It is the only one on that card this year that anybody rang about."

The card went in the notebook and the notebook went in the tin and the band went round the tin twice, and the tin went on the front-room table at four o'clock with Ru Sabar's round sheet beside it.

He kept the envelope.

Nobody asked him why and he did not say, and the reason was not sentiment and it was not evidence, and when he tried to write it down on Thursday night the honest version came out as this: it was the first piece of post to come to Ash Street in nine weeks that was addressed to a description of a room and not to a person, and a thing that can be delivered to a room can be produced by anybody who can find the room, and he was not ready to let this one go to a shelf yet.

---

He did not sleep much on the Thursday night, for a reason that had nothing to do with the wall.

He has been a field technician since he was twenty-two. Nine weeks ago the job was the same job every day, it did not require him to be a particular man, and there were eleven of them on the Kestrel roster and the roster said who did what on what day, and if you were good at it you were good at it on the Tuesday and you were still good at it on the Friday.

He is now the only man in a building of twenty-eight people who cannot do the thing he was good at, and he has a case number, and he goes to a depot on the far side of the estuary on Tuesdays and Fridays at half past six and writes numbers on forms, and the forms mean what they say.

It is not a bad life. That is the part he cannot get past.

It is a life with nothing in it, and he has been in it nine days, and it is a different life and not a better one, and he cannot tell which of the two he would choose if somebody put it to him on a Tuesday morning. In the second week of September that question would not have existed, because every hour had a name or a hole in a street in it.

At ten past one he got up and went down the corridor past the records room, and the wall was in the dark, because the hall is dark from midnight to four and Petar Lisk walks it with a hand lamp, and Petar was asleep, and the light in that corridor belongs to a woman who comes on at seven and switches it on herself and has never once been asked to switch it on earlier.

He stood in the dark for a minute, and then he went back to bed.

In the morning he asked Inez Sato a question, and it was the first question he had put to her since Wednesday, and he put it in the front room with four people in it, and he put it out loud in the words it was said in, because that is the rule on the wall and the rule applies to him.

"Can this building send anybody to Cordell again," he said, "and if it can, whose card goes back for a week?"

Inez Sato put her pen down, which she does not do.

"Ask me that properly," she said, "with a number in it, and not in front of four people who are about to find out what it costs. **Friday. Front room. Seven in the morning, and not seven in the evening, and I will tell you why on Friday and not now.**"
