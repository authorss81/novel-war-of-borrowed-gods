# Chapter 81 — A Line In A Form

In the second week of September Inez Sato had written a consent card for this ward in a green pad a district nurse had handed her, and she had laid it out the way forms are laid out, in rows, with a rule under every row and a box at the end of every rule, and there have been four of those cards in a wooden box in the front room since the first week of October and there are four this morning.

Elias Rook read one of them from the top to the bottom on Sunday morning with a pencil in his right hand, which is the only hand he has got, and stopped at the last row.

The last row said:

> **CONSENT ENTERED ON BEHALF OF A RESIDENT UNABLE TO GIVE IT — signature of the person entering it, printed name, and the capacity in which it is entered.**

"Whose is that," he said.

"That is mine," Inez Sato said, without looking up from the round sheet she was signing. "And I have been waiting ten weeks for somebody in this building to ask me about that line, and I would have told you it was mine anyway, so do not make a thing of it."

He did not make a thing of it for about a minute and a half.

"How many times has it been filled in."

"Once." She put the pen down. "Mr. Ansel, in October, the week he could not hold a pen for six days. Lena signed it and she put the ward number in the capacity box and there was nothing else to put, because there is no relationship you can write and no capacity that fits, and I watched her do it and I did not ask her what to write and she did not ask me, and I have not written that row on this card since and I am not going to."

---

The line in the ward's card and the line in the Office's own paperwork had been the same line, and it had been sitting in a wooden box in that building for ten weeks, and the only reason he had it at all was a telephone.

In between, he spent the morning on the ordinary business of a Sunday, which is the only kind there is any more. Four carded draws, signed in four hands, one of them Ru Sabar's with the time on it, and the round is not Ru Sabar's after six and the round runs to six and everybody in that building knows both halves of that and has never once been asked to say them out loud. The laundry was running. Ansel Duarte was on his frame in the corridor with a book he was not reading. Petar Lisk read the board at seven, as he does every morning and has done every morning since the second week, and read the two questions and the line under them and the date at the bottom, and did not ask anybody for anything, which is his function and which nobody in the building agrees with.

A man of fifty-eight had come up a corridor on a board on the thirtieth day at twenty past eight in the evening at fifty-one pounds and had said his own name twice, and it was thirty-four days since, and he was forty minutes away by an ordinary road in a bed in Cordell with a lesion from a shored face, and his wife was at a desk four minutes from that bed, on the ground floor of the building the telephone was in, and had been there since the Saturday this all started. It was sixty-four days. Nobody in the ward knew either number, and Elias Rook carried both of them at eleven o'clock on a Sunday morning the way he had carried them since the second week of September, like a man carrying somebody else's weather.

The man behind the wall was sixty-four days in as well, inside a tunnel that was sealed and classified in nine words that were a description and not a cause, and nobody went near him and nobody called the handset line, because calling it would mean opening the seal, and eleven meters is a length with a day written on it and it is going to stay a length.

---

He rang the inquiry's number at eleven twenty from the corridor, because the front room is the one room in that building nobody takes a telephone into.

A clerk answered and said the office was closed at the weekend.

"It is a question," Elias Rook said, "and it is not a request."

"Hold on," she said, and she was gone for about eleven seconds and came back and said, "Somebody is coming to the phone."

He waited. There was a fire door somewhere and a radiator and a chair being moved.

"Mr. Rook," Rusk said. "It is Sunday."

"I know what day it is."

"I am not saying that as a complaint. I am saying it because I have never once been surprised by a telephone on a Sunday and I have twice been pleasantly." Then: "Ask."

Elias Rook asked, and it took him nine words.

"Does the Office's designated-site incident return have a line for somebody signing on behalf of a person who cannot sign."

Rusk did not answer for about four seconds, and Elias Rook understood inside the four seconds that the answer was going to be yes, because a man who has never heard of a thing does not take four seconds to say no.

"Yes," Rusk said. "It does. It is printed. It is on the current set, not on a superseded one, and I want you to understand the difference between those two things before you write anything down about it, because half of what this inquiry will spend its time on is the difference between a form in a drawer and a form in a file.

"It was added in the winter of two thousand and three. Form number DS-4, Revision C, issued the fourteenth of January two thousand and three. It is a consent line and it is the only consent line on that form and it is the last row, which is where somebody put it who was thinking about the shape of the page and not about the argument."

"You know the date."

"I know the date. I have known the date since about four o'clock this afternoon, because a clerk of mine went looking for it when your clerk told her you had asked, and I did not tell her to go looking, and she went anyway." He said it flatly. "There is a rule in this building that questions get answered and requests get queued. It is mine and it is not written down anywhere and I would like you to notice that it is mine."

Elias Rook said, "What is in the file."

"The revision letter is in our forms and returns file set. It carries a form number, a date, a reason in one printed sentence, and a district. **It does not carry a name.**"

"And who asked for it."

"**I cannot tell you, and I want to be exact about why, because you are going to write the sentence wrong and then somebody will correct you.** It is not that I am withholding it. There is nothing to withhold. A revision request is filed by a district and it is signed by whoever was on duty when the thing came up, and thirty-nine years later that person is retired or dead or is a person of seventy-one who is not in this building, and the letter does not have their name on it, so I have not got it and neither has anybody. I have looked. I looked twice this afternoon, once by myself and once with the woman who went and looked, and I would like it minuted that it was not withheld."

Nobody was minuting it. Elias Rook was standing in a corridor on Ash Street with a receiver in his right hand and a pencil behind his ear.

"You cannot produce the form."

"**No.** And I have thought about that for about an hour since your clerk rang, because I am the kind of man who thinks about a thing like that for an hour, and I cannot, and here is why in one sentence: a form store is a room with forty drawers in it and a drawer is not a document and there is no instrument in this office that compels anybody to open a drawer for a man who has not got a case number, and you have got a case number and it is the wrong one for a form. I can have a clerk read you the revision letter out on the telephone, and you can have a copy of the letter as a copy of a letter, and that is the whole of what I have got, and I have never in nineteen years had more than that for anything anybody has ever wanted from me."

---

Joon Park was in the records room, because that is where he is at eleven o'clock on a Sunday, and he had listened to the whole of it on the extension in the corridor, which is the one nobody in that building knows about, and he had not said anything for about four minutes.

"I did not argue," Joon Park said.

"I know."

"I want it written down that I did not argue." He had the notebook open and he was not writing in it. "**I have been waiting since the third of November for somebody to hand me a sentence worse than the one I already had.** Six words about a relay cabinet, a unit in a block, a woman who asked whether her name was the right one, a rectangle of paper gone with a blade, and a form that does its job. And a Deputy Director has just told me that the worst thing in this business is a line somebody typed in a hurry in a building on the Meridian, and I do not know yet whether that is a comfort or a warning, and I am going to write both of those down and not choose between them, which is the first time I have written a sentence down twice."

He wrote it down twice.

---

"And then he said the other thing," Elias Rook said. "The one about his predecessors."

"He did." Joon Park's pen stopped. "He said you have shown him that this office's predecessors typed a line, and that is not evidence about what you did, and it is evidence about what he has to answer for."

"I know. I was on the telephone."

"Then I want to say the thing I have not said and you have not asked me about, and you are going to hate it, and I am going to say it in one sentence and then I am going to make tea. **Nobody argued with him. Not you, not me, and not the eleven of the last six weeks. I have been carrying a thing since the second week of this year that the answer to all of it is going to be one sentence in one office's mouth, and it turns out it is not one sentence, it is a revision letter with a date on it, and it turns out that is the same answer wearing different clothes.** That is not the end of anything. That is the end of the shape I was holding, and I would like a minute before I have to work out what I am holding now."

Elias Rook did not give him a minute. He gave him the kettle.

---

What he was holding, as it turned out, was worse.

He had spent nine weeks building a thing in his head that had a man in it. Not a name — he had been extremely careful about names, and he had put one woman's name in nothing and had gone to a corridor in another city to ask her a single question and had not asked a second one. But a man. Somebody competent who had known. Somebody who had sat in a room with a form in front of him and read it and understood what it was and typed a line into it anyway.

It was a bad shape to have been holding, and he knew it was a bad shape, and he had held it anyway, because a bad shape with a man in it is a thing you can be angry at, and the tenth week of this had nothing in it that he could be angry at.

The line in the ward's card and the line in the Office's return had been the same line. One of them was thirty-nine years old and one of them was ten weeks old, and they were the same line, and somebody had typed it on a Tuesday in a building on the Meridian, in daylight, having read about the previous night in a piece of paper.

It was not worse than a man.

It was the whole of what was underneath a man, and there was no one to hold.

He wrote in the notebook, in the notebook with the envelope in it and the left-hand page he could not write on, with his right hand, on a Sunday at half past one in the afternoon:

**The line is printed. It was typed in a hurry by somebody competent, in daylight, on a Tuesday, off a piece of paper about a Monday night. There is nobody in it. I have been fighting a sentence.**

And then underneath, because he had learned that the second sentence is the one that costs:

**A sentence can be argued with. I have nothing to argue with and I have to go to a hearing tomorrow week with nothing to argue with, and the nothing is real, and I am going to have to be careful that I do not start trying to put a man back in it so that I have something to hold on the way in.**
