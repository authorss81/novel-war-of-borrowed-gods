# Chapter 74 — A Book Is A Thing A Person Can Be Argued With

The bus left at ten past eight on a Sunday morning and took forty minutes on an ordinary road, and the fare was two dollars sixty, and Elias Rook was the only person on it who was going to a records center.

The key was on a hook. There was one on a hook outside the store room door at the Cordell Regional Records Center and it had been on that hook every Sunday for six years, and the woman who sat under it was Bettina Hoyle, and she looked at him for about four seconds before she said anything at all.

"Third time this year," she said. "And the first two were lost."

"How lost."

"One of them went into the wrong aisle and did not come out, and one of them found what he wanted in about nine minutes and left without the page, and I do not know which of those is the worse one to have on a form." She put a card on the desk and wrote a time on it. "Ten past nine. You are the third visitor of the year and I have written the time down, and you will find that I write everything down, and I do not write down what I think a thing means, and the two of those are the whole of my job."

"Mr. Hoyle—"

"It is Bettina. Mr. Hoyle was a man who worked here in nineteen sixty-one and he did not like anybody and he was right about most of it." She looked at his coat. "You are not from the county."

"No."

"You are from a town that lost a tunnel."

"Yes."

"Then I am going to say a thing to you now and I am going to say it once, and it is going to sound like a refusal and it is not one." She put her hands flat on the desk. "**I am not going to hand you a run of volumes because you asked nicely.** I have been on this desk for six years and I have had four visitors and I am not sorry about the two that were lost, and I am not going to be the woman who gives five books to a man from a town that had an event, and lets it be a story about a man from a town that had an event, in my building, on a Sunday, out of my key."

He had come here with three sentences ready and all three of them were gone.

"What do you want," he said.

"Two things, and one of them is a yes and one of them is a no, and I have not decided which is which, and I want to know what you are before I decide."

---

Joon Park had come up on the Saturday bus and had said one sentence to him on the step of the hall at four o'clock and then had gone inside and shut the door.

"The thing you are about to do is the thing you did with the nine lines," Joon said. "You are about to construct a request out of what you already know, so that a stranger does the walking and you do the finding. **A request that is shaped around what you already know is not a request. It is a conclusion with a form on it.** You are the man I said that to seven days ago in a room in that city about nine people, and you have not stopped being the man since, and neither have I, and I am telling you on a Saturday so that you can hear how it sounds from the outside before you hear it from a woman with a key on a hook."

He had said it once and he had shut the door, and Elias Rook had stood on the step for a minute and a half, and then he had got on the bus.

---

The volumes came out on a trolley at ten o'clock, five of them, and Bettina Hoyle came round the front of the trolley herself and pushed it, and the trolley had to have a person on each end because a bound paper volume on a steel shelf on castors is not a book you carry, it is an object you move.

They were paper, and they were bound, and they were dirty at the corners in the specific way of a thing that has been on a shelf in a building for a long time.

"Sixteen millimeters of board," she said. "Five volumes, and the run is ninety-six to two thousand and one, and it is a working log. Not a report. **A working log is a book a person wrote while they were doing a job.** It is not indexed by anything. It is not indexed by field, it is not indexed by subject, and there is no card in this building or any other that says who asked for it, because nobody has, and because the thing it is a log of is a relay, and a relay is a piece of equipment in a duct and the duct is in a wall and nobody has to know anything about it for forty years."

She stopped the trolley at the end of the aisle and put her hand on the top volume without lifting it.

"Paper gets kept because paper is kept. That is the whole reason it is here. There is no policy. There is no instruction. Somebody in a county office in about nineteen eighty decided that a record of maintenance is a record, and a record is a thing you keep, and then about forty people in this building for forty years agreed with them and that is how a stack of paper ends up in a room with a key on a hook. **It is not important that it was kept. It is important that keeping it cost nothing, and that is why it happened.**"

"How many pages in the run," Elias said.

"Six hundred a year, and a hundred and ten in the year they changed the form." She said it without looking at anything. "You can have them for an hour and a half. I will do it, and I will do it because a preservation levy pays for this room to be open to the public on a Sunday and you are the public, and I am not going to be a woman who invents a way of being kind to a man from a town that had an event.

"And here is the condition and it is in the form of a condition and not a favor, so write it down. **Nobody photographs a page that has not been through a clerk's hands first.** Not one page, not a corner, not a thumb, and I do not want to be told that you wanted a picture of one thing. I have been here six years and I have watched a person in a hurry put a camera on a trolley and that is the day a building stops being able to say what left it."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair, it is old, and I have never been able to make those two words behave."

---

They opened the top volume at the front and looked at nine pages, and it was a working log, and the pages were ruled in a hand that was not anybody's handwriting but a printed grid, and every line had four printed headings over it and a date at the left.

Elias Rook put his right hand flat on the page beside line twelve and did not touch the paper.

"Forty lines a page," he said. "A hundred and ten pages in a year. So four thousand four hundred lines a year, and five years of it, and there is a name on about one page in thirty."

"What are you looking for?"

"I am looking for a line," Elias Rook said, "and I cannot tell you which line, and I cannot tell you what a line would look like, and I have been in three buildings this month trying to get to that question and every one of the three has handed me a form that is a sentence about the field and not about the person, and every one of the three was right to."

Bettina Hoyle looked at him for a while.

"Then you are not going to get it in an hour and a half," she said, "and I am going to tell you why, and it is not because I do not want to help you. It is because a bound volume that has been on a shelf for forty years does not open. It opens *at a place*. And a man who knows where a book opens is not reading a book, he is looking, and looking in a store room is a thing a clerk has to be standing next to him for, and I am the clerk, and I am not going to stand next to a man from another city for an hour and a half in a room the public is allowed into for two hours on a Sunday, because a search is not a public hour."

---

At eleven thirty she wrote the day-book entry and turned the book round so he could read it, and it said what she had said it would say.

> *Sunday, 10.00–11.30. Public hour. Five volumes, working log, Civic Spine relay maintenance, Meridian, brought out on trolley 3. Visitor: one, from out of county, no form. Pages consulted: 9, front matter and leaves, no other.*

She signed it and put her initials beside the time and turned it round again.

"That is the whole of it," she said. "That is what a day book is for. It records that five volumes came out and how long they were out and who looked at them and how many pages. It is a book about movements. It is not a book about what somebody thought a movement meant, and if I write *believed this contains evidence of alteration* in a store's day book, then in four years somebody who was not here will read it and think somebody here believed it, and there will be no way on earth of telling the difference between a clerk's record of a morning and a clerk's opinion about a morning. **A day book is not a place to put what somebody thinks a movement means.** You can have that in a letter, and a letter is a thing you can disagree with, and I will write you a letter, and I will sign the letter, and the letter is mine and not the store's, and you can argue with me about the letter for as long as you like."

"You will not write the reason in the book."

"I will not write the reason in the book," Bettina Hoyle said, "and I want you to notice that you did not ask me to. You asked me for a page I would sign and I have given you a page I would sign, and I have refused you a page I would sign, and the difference between those two things is not a trick of mine. It is the difference between a movement and an opinion, and it is the only thing I know how to do, and I have been doing it since before you were born."

---

He understood the thing at about five past twelve on the bus coming back, forty minutes on an ordinary road, two dollars sixty.

She had not withheld anything. There was nothing to withhold. In nineteen years that woman had been handed four visitors and two lost and a key on a hook and a preservation levy and a card index and a rule about cameras, and in all that time **the archive has not refused the team one single thing**, and it has never refused anybody anything, and it does not have a door it closes, and the reason it is not withholding is that there is nothing in that building to withhold.

Elias Rook had spent nine weeks learning that a record which cannot be taken is a record that cannot be corrected, and he had put that sentence in a notebook and felt that it was the hardest thing he had learned in October.

It is a much worse thing to be owed than a refusal. A refusal is a wall you can put a door in. A building that has never hidden anything from you is a building where everything you ever get is everything there is.

He wrote that down on the bus with his right hand, on his knee, and it came out wrong, and he crossed it out and wrote it again, and on the second attempt it came out as six words and he has never found a better way to say it.

**THEY HAVE NEVER HID ANYTHING FROM US.**

At ten past two the bus did not stop, because Ash Street is not a stop, and he walked the last mile, and there were nineteen lines in nineteen hands on a wall in a building where nobody had said a year out loud in ten days.

Somewhere on a shelf in a building three miles from a school there were five volumes of paper and about four thousand lines in each of them, and somewhere in one of those lines there was a person and a time, and the person had never once been asked what it meant.
