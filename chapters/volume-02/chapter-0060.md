# Chapter 60 — A Box and No Name

Petar Lisk read the board at seven on Sunday morning, the forty-third day, standing on the same crate he has stood on since the second week, and then read twice the one line he had put a bracket under on Thursday night.

**WHO EDITED THE KESTREL RESPONSE RECORD SO THAT A LIVING PASSENGER AND A WOMAN'S ROUTE WERE BOTH CALLED ADMINISTRATIVE ERRORS — AND WHAT DOES THE CONTINUITY OFFICE BELIEVE THAT A SINGLE APPROVED OUTCOME WOULD PREVENT?**

Below it the case number he had put up on Friday, **HC-42-0417**, and to the right, in a small neat upright print twenty-eight people in that building knew as well as their own handwriting, the fourth column: twenty-eight lines, one of them corrected in a second ink with two sets of initials.

Nobody was still asleep by the time he got to the second half of the question. Ru Sabar's card went back at ten past six. The dark hours are four instead of two now, and the sheet says so with a time on it.

Emil Baros came down the ramp at ten past five, the way he had every morning, and stopped at the fence, where a man in a clean orange jacket was standing.

---

Elias Rook came out of the records room at half past ten and found the day already done.

Not by him. That was the part. Lena's round was on the nail, the four draws were signed off in three different hands because Ru Sabar had gone to the clinic with Osei Kwarteng's wife and had left her card to somebody else and told that somebody else what to do with it, the laundry was running, Ansel Duarte was on his frame in the corridor with a book, and nobody in the front room looked up, because there was no reason to.

He checked the round anyway, because he cannot help it.

"It is right, and you checked it on Thursday," Lena Mir said, without looking up. "It is right today. You are going to stand there until somebody tells you that, and I am not going to be the one who says it twice."

---

Emil Baros asked him at eleven, in the front room, with four people in it and no ceremony.

"I want to ask for something," he said, "and I would like to be asked first, so that I am not asking in front of a wall. The lip is inside the closure order, and has been since Friday morning. I have been stopped at that fence for nine days and I have not said anything to anybody because I did not have a question yet."

"You have one now."

"The sheet has my name on it and nothing after it. Nineteen functions and a name against each, and then Emil Baros, and a space," Baros said. "I have been looking at that space since Friday. I would like a function, and I would like to be specific, because if I am not specific somebody will give me a job to keep me busy and we will both be embarrassed."

"Go on."

"I drew the map," Baros said. "The one on the wall with nothing missing off it, the only document in five weeks without a hole in it, and I did it because I do not draw things that are not there. If that column is the record of what was taken out of this building, somebody here has to keep our copy of it, check it against theirs when theirs turns up, and know the difference by heart. That is not a comfort job. It is a job with a pencil in it and a person who can be asked to prove a thing on a Tuesday when a Deputy Director is in the room." He put his hands in his pockets. "I have been waiting four days for somebody to ask me a question. Then I decided the question was mine."

Inez Sato said, "I will put it to the body and I am not promising you the answer. Two things make it hard and you should have them now. Your name stays on the sheet whether or not you get a line, everybody can see the shape of it while it is being decided, and I have not found a way to make that comfortable. And it will not be quick: it will be after Tuesday, because four of the five of us are in another city on Tuesday and I will not have a vote on a function in a room with nine people in it. Lena is not giving you the keeper's line. She explained that once, in her own words, it is in a book, and I am not having her say a second thing about it."

"That is fair," Baros said. "I know about Lena."

---

Cliff Marner was in the chair by the window with his coat over the blanket, and at about half past two he asked Tarin Voss a question Elias Rook heard from four feet away and did not interrupt.

"Have they got you."

Tarin Voss put his book down. He was nineteen and had been in that building since the first week.

"I looked," he said. "Two weeks ago, on that terminal, before it went, with Joon standing next to me. You type a surname and it gives you a page. It gave me *patient, nineteen, male, capacity applied, target of record withdrawn at subject's request*, and not one letter of my name on it, and a form saying the withdrawal is a finding and not an absence, with the archivist's name at the bottom."

"And?"

"I cannot be found," Tarin said. "Not by anybody. I took the name off the page that says what was done to my body, in my own words, on the sixteenth, and it was correct. It is their Finding 3, adopted because a man was seventeen and did not want to be a story about a corridor. It is also the only reason that page is still valid: with my name on it, in four years somebody can look at nineteen-year-old Tarin Voss and say a man with a capacity nobody can explain put a hand on him, and that is a sentence about a boy. The page is better without me on it, it is the strongest thing any of you hold, and I would like both in one sentence, and all four people I have said them to wanted to fix it."

"Does it need fixing," Cliff Marner said.

"No," Tarin Voss said. "That is the part I have not got anybody to hear. In ten years somebody is going to need a name taken out of a thing, and there is a form in this city for exactly that, and I have used one, and it works. It is a form you fill in about yourself, every clerk in this city knows it by heart, and that is exactly the problem. In ten years it is going to be somebody else's name, and the only version of this that exists is a man of nineteen filling in a page about his own body. I would like somebody to write the other one, and I am not going to be the person who writes it at nineteen and gets it wrong."

Cliff Marner sat with that. "So you are in there and I am not," he said. "That is a good thing to have happened to you, and I would like somebody to explain it in a way I can repeat to a woman who has been at a desk in another city for forty-three days, and I do not think anybody can."

---

Elias left the records room at four, and Cliff Marner caught him at the door, standing up out of the chair, which took a while.

"One question, and then I will sit down for good," he said. "Is my name going to be in it."

"I do not know," Elias said.

Cliff Marner looked at him for a second.

"That is the fourth true thing anybody has said to me since I came up on the thirtieth," he said. "The other three were the spelling, my own physician telling me to my face that she was not going to be the one who put me on a train, and a man at this table telling me there is no response list and there has never been one. I am going to hold on to this one, and if it is the last one I get, then it is the last one I get and I will still have four."

---

The call came at half past four to the corridor fitting that is not a light anymore, and a woman answered and said her name was not the one he wanted and that Mr. Aitken was in a store room with a cart and would not come out for a telephone. Then she said, "Say the number and I will look while I have you."

Elias Rook read a holding number off a schedule dated the eleventh of May 2039, and a unit number, and a date, from a piece of paper he was holding in his right hand.

There were about nine seconds of a keyboard.

"It is out," she said. "It went on a cart on Friday to a conservation run and it is on the second cart, which comes back Tuesday. If you want another day it goes back on a shelf Wednesday, and then it is a shelf and not a box, and you are looking for a shelf. I would rather you knew that today than on Wednesday."

"Who asked for it," Elias said.

"Nobody told me. There is a form on it with a reference and a time and a destination, and no requester's name, because there is no box for one. **Public records requests are anonymous by design.** You turn up, and if you want a copy you sign for it. Nobody is supposed to be able to find out who asked."

Joon Park said, "Say both readings out loud where I can hear them, because I am going to refuse to choose and I want the refusing on a record."

"Which two," she said.

"Either somebody has been looking at our papers and is allowed to be anonymous, and there is exactly one kind of requester who is," Joon said, "or a clerk pulled a box for a conservation run and the form has nobody's name on it because the form has never had one. Two different days. I am not choosing between them, and that goes on the page as well."

"That is fair," the woman said. "It happens about forty times a week."

Elias Rook was standing at the wall with the schedule in his hand, and he said the thing out loud once, and then stopped where the evidence stopped.

"Director Arden put a letter into that building on the twenty-third of September and said it would be read on the Wednesday by somebody with a queue," he said. "Nobody in this room has ever been told what came back. A request with a box number on it is not a letter, and a queue is a queue, and two pieces of paper can both be anonymous by design and still be two pieces of paper in two different hands. I am not going to stand here and tell you which one this is."

"Then I am not going to write anything on it either," Joon said, and wrote both sentences down in the same ink.

He booked the visit for Tuesday. It took four minutes and it required a name, and the name was the hall's records keeper, and Joon gave it without a speech and then said out loud that the hall's records keeper was not him.

Elias had a hose certification slot on Tuesday morning at the depot on the far bank and had it moved, and the depot gave it back in writing with a number on it, and he noticed that the depot was the only institution he had dealt with in five weeks that had given anything back without a hearing.

---

In the corridor he said it out loud to Mara Okafor, because he had said it out loud once already to nobody and underlined it in a notebook, and because on Tuesday he was in a car.

"I am faster," he said. "It has been building since Thursday and it is worse than on Friday, and I know exactly what it is, and I am telling you because you are the only person in that building who will not do anything about it for me."

"That is the feeling you had on Tuesday of the fourth week," Mara said, "and you know what it is, and so do I."

"I know."

"Neither of us is going to say anything else about it tonight," she said, "and on Tuesday morning you will be in a car going to a building where somebody keeps a queue, and that is not a rescue and it is not a road and it does not get anybody out of a tunnel. Say it back."

"Neither of us," Elias said.

She went into the front room. Nothing has happened between them and no future version of either of them has promised anything, and she asked once, in a room with witnesses, for that to be on the record.

---

Petar Lisk came in at seven with the pencil, read the board once more because that is what he does, and wrote under the fourth column, in his own capitals:

**THEY CAN TAKE THE BOXES. THEY CANNOT TAKE THE WALL. I READ IT EVERY MORNING.**

And then, in smaller writing, because he is nine and he is running out of wall, a second question, which went *underneath* the old one and did not replace it:

**WHO BENEFITS WHEN A BRANCH IS OFFICIALLY DELETED?**

He read them out loud to twenty-eight people. Inez Sato's name is still in chalk under a seal, and a woman is still under the street, and on Tuesday morning a man with one hand is going to get into a car.
