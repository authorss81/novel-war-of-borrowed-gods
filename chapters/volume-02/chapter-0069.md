# Chapter 69 — What They Bring Back

On Tuesday morning, the fifty-second day, Elias Rook and Joon Park put four sheets of paper on the wall of the front room on Ash Street, in nineteen hands, and it took about forty minutes, and nobody was allowed to help, which was the rule Petar Lisk made and Inez Sato backed and which was, Elias understood about nine minutes in, the only correct rule anybody could possibly have made.

Nineteen people wrote, one after another, in chalk, in their own hands, at their own height, on a wall that already had a Deputy Director's fourth column on it and two questions above that.

The four sheets were: the certified count of nine, with the seal of the Cordell Regional Records Center on it; the printed heading and the eleven field labels and the exact wording over the second box, copied out in a hand that was not theirs; the two words themselves, **not applicable**, with the date and the year and the store; and four lines describing a line in a movement book in a store room in the same city, with the page and the frame numbers on it, and no explanation.

They went up in chalk and not into the residents' file, and the reason was Inez Sato's and she gave it in the front room with six people in it.

"**The file can be demanded.**" She said it flatly. "Item eleven of that order is a demand for the file of the residents' body and there is still no line on it for a resident to refuse on, and I have put my refusal in a public instrument once this month and I am not doing it twice for a piece of paper with a stamp on it. And the wall cannot be demanded, because the wall is a wall, and a man in an orange jacket came to this room on the thirty-seventh day and wrote a fourth column on it and he does not own it and I do not own it and Mr. Park does not own it and the Deputy Director stood in front of it for four minutes in a hallway and could not find anybody who had written any of it."

"That is the whole of the argument for," Nadi Bell said. "And here is the other half of it and I would like somebody to write it on the wall underneath, because in three years somebody is going to come in here with a court order and ask us to produce it, and on the wall we cannot produce it, because we do not have it. We have a photograph of it in nineteen hands. **Being safe from being taken and being able to be asked are two different things and today we are choosing the first one on purpose, and I am the person who wrote down a movement record in a past tense precisely so that we would have two versions, and I am not going to pretend that is not a cost.**"

Elias Rook wrote that underneath it, in chalk, with his right hand, and there were nineteen hands on that wall by half past ten and he had put one of them there and had got nothing for it.

He had spent six weeks treating a document as a thing to be kept, and he had been wrong about that in a way that had taken him a floor of a records center in another city to see, and the correction was thirty-five words long and a woman who had been in the ward for a month wrote it standing up, and he did not get a promise out of her and he did not ask for one.

---

Emil Baros's line was on the sheet and he had not been in the room when it went on.

It said: **FOURTH COLUMN, HALL'S COPY — CHECK IT AGAINST THE OFFICE'S WHEN THEIRS TURNS UP — KNOW THE DIFFERENCE BY HEART.**

And under it, in a different ink, in Inez Sato's hand, because Baros had insisted on the telephone from a corridor in a clinic on Wednesday morning and had made her write it while he was listening:

**Not the keeper's line. He asked for this and not for that. Twelve for, eleven against, five not voting.**

Inez had read the condition over the telephone and Baros had said one thing back, and she had written it: *the line says what it is and not what I wish it was.*

"He asked me to telephone him," Inez Sato said to the room, on Tuesday, to about nine people. "Eleven minutes ahead of the vote he did not know was going to happen, and he asked me to telephone him, and I said the day after, and he said the day after. So he has known for six days that he is going to be out of a room, and he has had six days to decide whether to put his name against it, and he put it against it, and Lena Mir voted no and did not say why and nobody asked her twice."

Lena Mir was in the doorway with a round sheet in her hand.

"I voted no," she said. "It is on the sheet. I am not going to explain it and you were not going to ask."

---

Nathan Rusk telephoned the hall at twenty past two in the afternoon, and he telephoned the hall and not the depot and not Elias Rook personally, and the woman who answered offered him Joon Park, and he said he would take that, and then he said, "Put Mr. Rook on as well, because I am going to ask one question and I would like the man who has been in two buildings this week to be the man who answers it."

He asked it in the fourth minute, with no preamble at all.

"Did you find a name."

"No," Elias Rook said.

The line did not move for about four seconds.

"Then I want you to understand something about that answer," Rusk said, "because in nine days' time I am going to be asked to write a paragraph and this paragraph is going to have to say what forty minutes on an ordinary road produced, and if you had found a name I would be able to write a paragraph that says *a regional archive was searched and a holder was identified*, and that is a sentence, and it is a decent sentence, and it would be in my report and it would be in the summary before the summary went anywhere. **And it would be a document about a person produced by a team with a holding number, and nobody in this inquiry would ever have been able to check it, because you would have found the name yourself and put it in a form and handed it to me, and I would have had nothing to check it against.**"

"Yes," Elias said.

"I am not going to say you did the right thing. I do not know that you did the right thing. I have said four true things to you and given you nothing you can hold, and this is the fifth, and it goes the same way: **a search that produces nothing is the cheapest thing an institution ever does, and I am the institution, and I have just watched one of mine describe six days of somebody else's work as a paragraph.** What did you bring back."

"Two certificates, four frames of a book, and a sentence," Elias said. "The sentence is the only thing in it that will matter and it is this: **nobody lost nine people in two thousand and thirty-five. The renumbering was built so that losing them was a correct thing to do.** There is a printed box on the form for a field that cannot be carried, and the question over the box is about the field, and the correct answer to a question about a field is about the field, and it is two words long and it is correct, and the reason there is no name anywhere in this business is that nobody was ever required to decide anything about one."

"That is a sentence," Rusk said.

"That is the sentence," Elias said. "And the second half of the question on that wall in your hearing room is still unanswered, and it is unanswered in your office and it is unanswered in mine, and I spent Friday in a building where a system with fewer contradictions in it kept everything perfectly and told us nothing, and I have still not been able to say the word out loud, and neither have you, and I would like it minuted that neither of us could."

"I will have it minuted," Rusk said, and then said something with no preamble either. "I have written a report on a Monday and I have not written it well, and I am telling two men in a ward that I have not written it well, and neither of you is going to do anything with that except possibly use it, and I would like it on your page."

"Which of the five," Joon Park said. "Day forty-two, or yesterday."

"Day forty-two was *I don't know of one*," Rusk said. "It was a true answer to a question that had a bad shape, and you said it to my face and a man at that table looked at the table, and I have not mentioned it since and I am not going to mention it now, and if that was a lie then you will both know it before I do. **This one is a different shape and it is the second one in ten days and I have noticed that about you as well.**"

Then he asked to speak to Ms. Sato, and said good afternoon, Ms. Sato, and that he had read the transcript of the twenty-ninth day twice, and that he was not asking her for the file, and that Item eleven of the impoundment remains a demand and not a seizure, and that he was sorry about the wording of the letter and not sorry about the position. Inez Sato said that was the correct answer and that she was not going to say thank you and that she had not said thank you. Rusk said that was the correct answer too.

---

The tin went on the front-room table at half past four and Elias Rook took the rubber band off it, and then put it back on, because Ru Sabar was in the room.

"Nothing goes in a file in this building," Joon Park said. "That is not mine to decide and Inez decides it and she has decided it. So it goes where it cannot be served on and where nobody can be handed an order for it, and the answer to that in this county is a woman on Bell Row with a bookbinder who has never asked us for anything, and in a city it is a woman with a day book and a card index on a shelf."

"And a copy of the copy," Elias said.

"A copy of the copy," Joon said, "left on Saturday in the county library's local-history room in Cordell, in a box with our name on it and no address on it, and a card in the room's own catalogue that says *papers, Cinder Quarter Ash Street, produced by the residents' body, not to be removed without the written consent of the people who wrote them*, and that card is in a public building that the Havenport Continuity Office cannot serve an order on, in a county it does not have a building in, forty minutes and an ordinary road from here."

Nobody had asked for that either. That is the second time in nine days that the best thing anybody did came out of a rule and not out of a favor, and Elias Rook wrote it in the notebook with the date, and then read the two lines above it, which were the four-to-six-week interval on a wall chart in another city, and the near end of it, which was a date in November that would be answered in a room neither of them would ever stand in, and about which neither of them said a word, because it was not a thing to say out loud on a Tuesday in a hall with nineteen hands on a wall.

Nobody in that building had ever been told what came back to Sable Arden's letter of the twenty-third of September, and nobody asked, and the tin was on the table, and the band was on the tin, and it was a quarter to six in the evening and the hall's four dark hours were four hours away and all four of them were going to happen.
