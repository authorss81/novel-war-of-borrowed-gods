# Chapter 87 — The Fear, Not The Anger

Petar Lisk read the board at seven on Saturday morning, the fifteenth of November, the seventieth day, and read it out loud twice to a room with twenty-three people in it, which was the most there had been on a Saturday since October, and then he said the wall was the same and he was not writing anything new on it that morning.

The board still had three questions on it in his own capitals, in the same hand, and the second half of the oldest was still a bracket with nothing under it. Under everything was his own line about a shape, and under that a date with a dash and a P. L., and the date was four days away.

---

Inez Sato stood in the front room at twenty to eight with no meeting, nothing on the pad and no form, and she said eleven sentences and did not sit down at any point in them.

"I am going to say a thing and then leave the room, and I want to say the first part before the rest of it. **On Thursday this building decided it does not convene itself about a form.** That decision stands. There is no meeting, no line on the pad, nothing to sign and nothing to vote on.

"And I said in that room last night that you were not getting this, and I have changed my mind in about thirteen hours, and I would like the change minuted, because the second of my two reasons has got worse overnight. **I said I would rather be the only person on this street who knew a finding the rest of you knew. Somebody has to be the only one, and it is going to be me, and I will get used to it in a fortnight, and that is how it works on anybody.**"

"Here is the other half and it is the half that matters. **There is a finding. It was arrived at on Friday evening by three people, it is about how a machine this city runs actually works, and it is true, and it is not going to be acted on this week or any week.** I am telling you it exists. I am not going to say it."

"There are three of us who have it. Mr. Park, Mr. Rook and me. I am not going to say who said it, because the man who said it asked me in writing not to have his name on it, and he is right."

"**It was said once, in a room, on Friday evening, and nobody in that room could say a sentence after it, and I am not going to be the person who says it a second time in a room of twenty-three people, because a thing that has to be said twice was never said once.**"

"So it is not going in this room. What I am going to do instead is what we do with the round sheet, and you can say no, and if you say no I will not come back, and nobody will be told that you said no."

She went out, and she went down the stairs, and she did not close the door.

---

It was not said in the room. It took about fifty minutes in a corridor, the way a round sheet is signed: standing up, in a voice pitched for one person and not for the room. **Seven people were handed it and one was not, and it was not written down anywhere which seven.**

Elias Rook did four of them and refused to do the fifth, because Petar Lisk is nine, and Inez Sato told him in the corridor that she had already decided the same thing and had not been going to ask him. **His four were Miss Bell, Mr. Kwarteng, Mrs. Osei and Mr. Voss, and hers were Lena Mir, Mr. Duarte and Mr. Marner, and Mr. Lisk was the one who was not.**

Tarin Voss was the fourth, and listened to the whole of it without saying anything, and then said, "Say that last part again," and Elias said it again, and Tarin said, "Right," and went back into the front room and stood by the door, which is where he stands.

---

They did not talk about it for an hour, and the hour was the loudest thing that has happened on that street in nine weeks.

It was not an argument. It was worse than an argument. Four people said the same thing in four different ways and every one of them was right.

Nadi Bell said the two kinds of safe and said the hall had picked the wrong one in the first week and had been getting away with it.

Osei Kwarteng said his wife was asleep in the next room on a battery the clinic had charged forty-one dollars for, and that he had spent nine weeks being glad about a thing this room had just explained to him.

Comfort Osei, home eight days, said, "Then it is the safest building in the city and I am the most expensive thing in it," and somebody laughed, and she did not, and neither did anybody else.

And Lena Mir said nothing at all for a quarter of an hour, and then said she was not going to be the one to say the thing, and Tarin Voss said it.

---

"I said a sentence in this room in November," Tarin Voss said, "and it was the best one anybody has said in this building since October, and **it is not on the wall.** That is what has been going round in my head for two days. Lena asked for a page of her own and was refused, and I have got a sentence nobody has ever written down and it has been in there since the twenty-ninth of September.

"And I have never been so glad of it either," he said. "Because here is the thing nobody is going to say this morning and I am going to say it, and I have got nothing to lose and I am nineteen.

"**A hall that agrees is safer. A hall that argues is a hazard. And that is not a slogan, that is Friday evening, it is a count, and Mr. Vale has been doing that job for thirty-one years and he is right.**

"This hall argues. We have argued about the laundry, the round, whose card, and whether a thing on a wall is a fact or a feeling, every Tuesday and twice on a Thursday, and Lena has voted no four times and never explained and nobody has asked her twice, and Mr. Baros has an argument on the pad every week and it has been a *better* argument than mine about half the time. **We are the most argumentative building on this street and it is the only reason any of the last eleven of us are alive.**

"And the safe version of us is the version where we stop." He took his hands out of his pockets. "**I am not angry, and I want to be clear about that, because angry is a thing I know how to do and I would like to be allowed to do it. I am frightened, and the fright is correct, and I would like somebody to check the fright and tell me it is not correct, and nobody is going to, and I have had the fright since about eleven o'clock last night and I have not been able to put it down.**"

"Somebody check it," Nadi Bell said.

"I have checked it," Tarin Voss said. "It is correct. That is the problem with checking it."

---

Ansel Duarte said it from the corridor, where he was, because that is where he is.

"Two things and I am not going to be asked for a third.

"One. I am on a frame and I cannot walk to a hospital. On the twenty-fifth there is a clinic and a ramp that is not at this building, and Mrs. Sabar is driving me and Lena is signing a form with my name under it, and if this hall had one opinion and it was the right one, I would be at home on a chair with a blanket on it and I would not have got the appointment.

"So the arguing is the appointment. **That is what the hazard is, and it is my legs, and I would like somebody in this room to feel slightly bad about how comfortable they find it.**

"Two. Nadi has a movement record in a file in the next room, in a past tense, and none of us are going to read it out this morning, and I am saying out loud that it exists so that nobody can later say the hall was quiet about it." He looked at Nadi Bell. "That is all."

Nadi Bell said, "Thank you," and nothing else, and it was enough.

---

Cliff Marner was in the chair by the window. He had been home four days, forty days since he came up a corridor on a board, and he had said nothing at all until a quarter to ten, when everybody else had stopped.

"You have found out how a machine works," he said. "I have been asking for ten weeks for somebody to write a word about me on a piece of paper.

"**I am not angry either. I have been angry every day since the sixth of September and I have got very good at it, and I have not used it this morning, because this morning I have got a worse thing and I do not know what to do with it.** Somebody somewhere got told, on a form, in a word, that one of us is less of a contradiction than the other one. That is all. That is what your count says, and it says it in the right order, and I would like somebody to put it next to my name on that wall and not explain it, and I know what I have just asked for, and Mr. Lisk can write *no* up there and I will not mind."

---

Petar Lisk wrote it up at the end of it, in his own capitals, small, because there is room for about three more.

> **A HALL THAT AGREES IS SAFER. THAT IS WHAT WAS SAID. I AM NINE AND I DO NOT AGREE AND I AM WRITING IT DOWN ANYWAY. PETAR L.**

He read it out loud twice, because that is what he does, and then he said it did not answer anything on the wall above it, that it had no number in it about anybody, that it was the shortest thing he had ever put on there and the shortest thing on that wall, and that it was not the shape and he was not sure yet what it was.

**Nobody had told him what was found. He wrote it because a man of nineteen stood by the door at ten past eight and said the first half of that sentence out loud to twenty-three people, and a nine-year-old can hear a sentence. He said that part out loud as well, and somebody wrote it down.**

---

Elias Rook went out on the step at half past ten and understood the thing he had got wrong on Friday evening, and it took him four minutes and it was not a relief.

He had thought — and had been *pleased* to think — that an answer with no villain in it cost nothing to hold. He had sat at a table on Friday night and felt the whole of ten weeks come off his shoulders, and there was nobody in the sentence to be responsible for it, and that was exactly the problem.

**An answer everybody can carry is an answer that will be carried by the people the team was trying to protect.**

He had spent ten weeks building a thing out of refusals, and every refusal had a person in it who had said no and had paid for it, and a name on a wall that nobody owned, and a line in a schedule that could not be renumbered, and a woman at a desk forty minutes away who wanted a piece of paper. Every one of those had weight, and the weight was the only reason a nineteen-year-old could say *I do not want that* to a room of twenty-three people and be believed.

And this had no weight. It was a coat that fitted anybody, and by eleven o'clock that morning seven people in this building had been handed it in a corridor, one at a time, out of the mouth of a man who could not put it down either, and what he had instead was a sentence that cost the speaker nothing and could not be impounded, corrected, shown to a person, or argued with by anybody in the world, because there was nobody on the other side of it.

**It was going to be carried by the hall. Not because the hall was stupid and not because anybody was going to order it, but because it was the only thing anybody had, and it was true, and it fit in a coat, and twenty-three frightened people on a Saturday morning in November are going to reach for the thing that is true and light.**

The man behind the wall was seventy days in on this day, inside a tunnel that was sealed and classified in nine words, and nobody had gone near him and nobody was going to, and the eleven meters was a length with a day on it and nothing else.

Elias Rook stood on the step and understood that he had got what he asked for on Friday, in a room with three people in it, and that nobody had paid for it yet, and that the people who were going to pay for it could not afford to, and that the one person in that room who could afford to say it out loud was a man of sixty-four with a job who was not one of the twenty-two.
