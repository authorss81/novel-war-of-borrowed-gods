# Chapter 61 — Four to Six Weeks

Petar Lisk read the board at seven on Monday morning, the forty-fourth day, standing on the same crate, and the second half of the question was still a bracket with nothing under it, and nobody in the room offered him an answer for it.

Elias Rook took the corridor fitting at a quarter past eight in the morning, which is not a light anymore, and put four numbers into a telephone to be put into a queue in another city.

It took nineteen minutes to get to a woman who said her name was not the one he wanted.

"She says it is open," he said, when the woman came back. "And she says there is nothing against it."

"What does that mean."

"It means nothing has come," Elias said. "Can you ask her what she was told to expect and when."

He waited. He could hear her say it twice, the second time slower.

"She was told four to six weeks," the woman said. "Quoted on the fourteenth. So the near end was Saturday. The far end is a week on Saturday. She says she will not chase it because she has a queue and because chasing it is what a queue is for, and she says that out loud, and she said it kindly."

Elias Rook had spent five weeks learning what it was to lose the ability of a building to do something on its own, and he had assumed for most of that time that the loss was equipment. A terminal, two lamps, a repeater, eleven boxes. He got the handset down and stood in the corridor with it against his ear and understood, for about four seconds, that what the hall had lost was a *way of asking*. There had been a box on a screen where you put a case number, and it told you things. Now there was a telephone and a queue and a woman repeating a sentence she had been given.

Thirty days earlier, on the fourteenth, a woman at that desk had been honest with Della Marner about how long a records request takes.

That is the whole of it. Nothing had been refused and nothing had been lost and nothing had gone wrong. The near end of a fair estimate had arrived on a Saturday and there had been nothing on the Saturday, and the only way to find that out had taken nineteen minutes and the ward's one line.

---

Ansel Duarte was asleep on his frame in the corridor at half past nine with a book on his chest, because the dark hours run from midnight to four and the last two of those were added in week four by a woman with a word and a table. He would be awake at nine tonight, and again at three, and nobody could do a single thing about either.

Elias checked the round anyway.

"It is right," Lena Mir said, "and you checked it on Thursday, and it is right today, and I am not going to say it a second time."

---

Inez Sato called the meeting at eleven in the front room with twenty-three people in it and put it the way she put everything, which is that she named the decision before anybody made it.

"Two of us are going to Cordell tomorrow," she said. "Not four. Two. Here is what it costs, all of it, before anybody votes.

"One. There is no physician in this building from tomorrow morning until Thursday night, and there has not been one since the first week, and two of those days are a fact about a nineteen-year-old on a powered frame in this corridor and a bed at the clinic four streets away. Mara is the only person in this county who can look at that frame and say what is happening to the man on it, and she is going to be forty minutes down an ordinary road in a car with two men who cannot write things down properly.

"Two. The tin leaves this building. For six days. And I want everybody in this room to hear the shape of that, because nobody in here has ever sent a copy of anything out of here, and if it is lost it is gone and there is no second one. Three. The hall is one person short of what it was and nobody outside this room is going to find out for nine days."

Nobody said anything for a while. Ru Sabar, who had been in the room since seven, said, "Two."

"Two," Inez said. "Two is my number too. Now somebody tell me who, and do not tell me about anybody's feelings."

---

Elias Rook stood up, and it cost him more than it should have, and the reason was on a piece of paper at a depot on the far side of the estuary with a number on it.

"I am not the person who decides this," he said.

Inez Sato looked at him for a second. "You are not suspended from the hall."

"I am not the person who decides it," Elias said again, and heard how it sounded, and kept going because stopping would have been worse. "Four days ago a Deputy Director asked me a question and I answered it and he wrote it down, and on Friday a man I have known for eight years was suspended from seam assignment in a room with three people in it, and the reason is on paper, and the paper is real, and it names a case number. If I stand in this room and pick the two people who go, then in October there is a Deputy Director with a document in front of him that says the man under the suspension chose the delegation. I would rather be the man who carried the box."

"You have never carried a box in your life," Baros said.

"That is fair," Elias said. "I have carried a lot of other things."

---

Mara Okafor put her hand on the table, which she did instead of raising it, and then took it off again.

"I am staying," she said, "and I am going to say it in the form I said I would say things in, because this is a decision of mine and nobody gets to inherit it.

"Any offer, any boundary, any refusal and any condition somebody is asked to perform before they give anything goes to the people who pay for it, in the words it was said in, before it is acted on. And nobody inherits me.

"The people who pay for this are in this room. I am staying. That is the words and that is the order and I have said it before I have done it. And if one of you thinks I should go, you are not overruling me, because there is nothing to overrule. You are telling me something, and I will hear it, and then I will still be here tomorrow at seven."

Nineteen people said nothing. One said *all right*, which is a thing people say when there is nothing else in the room.

---

Emil Baros, who had asked for a function on Friday and had been promised a vote after Tuesday, came in late and stood at the back and did not ask about it, which was the first thing he had done correctly in five weeks.

Cliff Marner was in the chair by the window with his coat over the blanket. He waited until the room had emptied down to six people, which took a while, because people in that building leave slowly when they are not sure.

"Am I going with you," he said.

"I cannot answer that," Elias said. "I am not the person. It is Mara, and it is not even Mara, it is a doctor who has not been in the room yet."

"You have been in six rooms this week."

"Not one of them had a person in it who has been looking after my chest," Cliff Marner said. He did not raise his voice and he did not need to. "I have been in this building fourteen days and every single person in it has been kind to me and not one of them has let me go anywhere, and I would like somebody to be honest about whether that is because of my chest or because it is easier."

Nobody answered him, which was the correct answer, and Elias carried it to Mara in the corridor at ten past eleven and gave it to her word for word, and she wrote it down in her own book with the time on it, because that is what she does.

---

Emil Baros asked Elias for a pencil at four o'clock, in the front room, with four people in it.

"Twenty-eight lines," Baros said. "I have counted them nine times this month and there are twenty-eight and I have not found one that is wrong, and I want two copies and I want them made by four people and not by one."

"Why four people."

"Because a thing one person copies is a thing one person can be asked about," Baros said. "And because I have watched a woman fill in a field about an afternoon she was not in, and she was accurate, and that is the part I cannot get past. Four hands means four people have to look at a line before it goes in a tin."

---

The copying was done from nine at night and it took until nearly one, and it is a scene and not a sentence because it took four people and a biscuit tin.

Lena Mir did the first eight lines, because she is the only person in that building who reads a list of things and stops at the ones that are wrong on purpose. Emil Baros did the next eight, because he drew the map the list came off and he could not be lied to about a location. Joon Park did the eight after that and did them in his own shorthand with nothing left out. Ru Sabar did the last four and then read back the three she had watched being written because she said out loud that a thing written in a tired hand at one in the morning is a thing that gets read wrong later by somebody who is trying.

Elias held the tin. It was a biscuit tin from a shop on Bell Row and it had held something else, and the lid did not sit flat, so Ru Sabar put a rubber band around it twice and said that was the best it was going to get and that a thing that is tied twice is a thing that somebody has to think about twice before they open it.

Petar Lisk read the copy back.

All twenty-eight lines, in the order they were written, at a volume that twenty-three people in a front room could hear. He read them slowly and he read line fourteen twice, which is the line with Sena Ambrose's name written out in full in a ward nurse's hand against an Office clerk's strike-through with two sets of initials, and the second reading was not for the room.

Then he stopped, and looked at the last four lines, and said, "There is a hole in this and I know where it is and I am not going to fill it."

"Which hole," Ru Sabar said.

"I am not going to say it either," Petar Lisk said. "Somebody in a hall in another city is going to ask us what the site produced, and there is going to be a number, and the number is going to be the wrong shape, and I am not writing the right one down here where a piece of paper is a piece of paper."

Nobody asked him what the right one was.

Lena Mir, from the table, said: "That will do."

---

The car was booked for a quarter past seven on Tuesday morning and it cost forty-one dollars and it goes to a city that is forty minutes away by an ordinary road, and Elias Rook stood in the front room at one in the morning with a biscuit tin in his right hand and a rubber band round it twice and understood something that he had not understood at eleven o'clock that morning.

Nobody had asked him anything. That was the whole of it, and it had taken all day to arrive, and it was the second hardest thing he had done in five weeks.

Being trusted with a decision is not the same as being asked for one. Every person in that room had told him what he was good for, and four of them had put a name against a function, and not one of them had asked him for a decision about anything, because he had told them at eleven o'clock in the morning that he was not the person, and they had believed him, and they were right to.

The tin was on the table with the band round it. The board said two questions and one of them had a bracket under the second half and nothing under that.

If it is lost, it is gone, Inez had said, and there is no second one.

Elias Rook wrote one line in the notebook with his right hand, and it was a list of the four things they were going to be asked for and nothing about what he hoped.

He had written something down at half past one in the morning for the first time in a week.
