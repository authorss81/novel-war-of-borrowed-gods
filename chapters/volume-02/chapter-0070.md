# Chapter 70 — The Date on the Page

The hall had been running for six days without him and it had not once stopped, and the proof of that was that on Wednesday evening, the fifty-third day, Elias Rook came up the ramp at ten past five and four people were doing a job and two of them did not look up.

Lena Mir was on the round. There were four carded draws and they were signed in three different hands, because Ru Sabar had gone to the clinic again on Tuesday and had left her card to a different somebody this time and had told that somebody a different thing about it. The laundry was running. Ansel Duarte was on his frame in the corridor with a book, awake, at five in the evening, which meant he had been awake at three, and nobody was going to say so.

"It is right," Lena Mir said, when he had checked it. "You checked it on Thursday. Sit down or go to another city."

"I have been in another city."

"Then sit down."

---

There was a decision waiting for him, and it was hers, and she had not asked for it and had not told anybody she was going to make it.

She put it at the front-room table after the round, with four people in the room, and it was one sentence and she said it in the voice she used for things that were not up for discussion.

"I want the four dark hours in the residents' file. Midnight to four, every night, since the thirty-seventh day. With a date on it and my name against it and the reason it was done, which is that somebody took a terminal and two lamps and a corridor repeater and I put a word on a table. I want it in the file in my handwriting and not in a summary anybody writes about me, because in two years somebody is going to come in here and tell a woman in a bed at a clinic that her district has a four-hour dark window because of a *power arrangement*, and I would like a page in a building that says a ward nurse said it in her own hand on the thirteenth of October."

Inez Sato did not say no immediately, which is how Elias knew she was going to.

"No," Inez Sato said, after about eleven seconds. "Not the file. **The file is the thing under a demand.** It has a case number on it and no line on it for a resident to refuse on, and I have already put my name against one instrument in that file this month and I am not putting a second name of mine in a document that is going to be read by a Deputy Director's clerk in October.

"And the second reason is Lena's and not mine. There are twenty-eight people in this building and there is one medical authority in it, and if your name is on a page in a file that somebody can demand, then in six months every person in this hall who has ever been frightened of a form is going to know exactly which page to be frightened of, and it will be yours."

"I know that," Lena Mir said. "I said it out loud so that it would be written down that I asked."

Petar Lisk wrote it down.

"I do not need the file," Lena Mir said, and picked the round sheet up off the table. "I have said it out loud in a room with witnesses and a nine-year-old has written it on a wall in this building, and the wall is not a document, and I have been the only night nurse on this street since the second week and nobody has ever put my name on a piece of paper about anything."

Nineteen people were in that hall and the room was quiet and nobody said *fair*, because the word fair is not what anybody in it is able to do this week.

---

There were four frames of a movement book in the records room, on a table, under a lamp, with the date on them.

**The fourth of March, 1998.**

The year was in pencil under the year in ink, in somebody's hand, in two thousand and thirty-nine, on a schedule anybody can hold. And under that, in Elias Rook's own right hand, on a sheet of the hall's paper, the line, the unit, the shelf, and the four words:

**NOT YET. AND NOT BY US.**

Nobody in that building could say the year out loud, and that was not a rule anybody had made.

Elias Rook understood it at about nine o'clock, standing in a doorway with a lamp in his hand, and it took him a long minute to get the shape of it, and the shape was this. A hall that says out loud that it is looking for something has started a search, and a search gets a name, and a name gets a date on it, and a date on it gets somebody's *why*, and a *why* is a conclusion with a person attached to it. In five weeks a hall had worked out how to keep a record that could not be impounded by writing twenty-eight lines on a wall that nobody owns, and the way it had done it was by never once saying out loud in a room that it was looking for a thing.

If they said the year on Ash Street, then in three months there would be a date, and then a request, and then somebody in a records center in another city would open a drawer with a lamp in it and find something, and it would be found by somebody, and the somebody would be a person, and the person would be *looking for* something on behalf of a hall with twenty-eight people and a case number.

**They had a store that consolidates, and a line in a book that nobody can explain, and a year that nobody in that building can say out loud, and it was the first time in six weeks that the thing they were not saying was the thing keeping the hall safe.**

Joon Park said it out loud in the records room at nine o'clock at night, to four people, in the same words as always, and this time he said it in a building where nobody from the Office was present, and nobody was going to quote him, and nobody was going to need him to have meant it.

"I am not writing what anybody thinks it means," he said. "And I want it written down that this is the fourth time and the first time it has not cost me anything, and I have noticed that, and I have not decided yet what to do with having noticed it."

---

Petar Lisk read the board at seven on Wednesday morning, the fifty-third day, and read the four new sheets under the clerk's fourth column, and read the two questions above everything, both halves of the older one and the newer one underneath it, and then he took the pencil and wrote one more line, and he wrote it in his own capitals because it is his wall, and he wrote it under the four sheets.

Then he read it out loud, twice, to twenty-eight people, because that is what he does.

**A BOOK IN ANOTHER CITY HAS A DATE ON IT THAT WE ARE NOT GOING TO SAY OUT LOUD IN HERE. FOUR FRAMES OF IT ARE IN THE RECORDS ROOM AND A SEAL ON A COUNT IS UNDER THE CLERK'S FOURTH COLUMN. PETAR L. — I AM NINE AND I HAVE READ IT TWICE.**

No number about people. No answer to either question.

---

The thing he came back with, on Wednesday evening, at ten past five, up a ramp, with a tin under his arm, was less than he had taken out of the building on Monday morning.

He had gone down an ordinary road with a holding number and come back up it with two certificates, four frames, a count of nine sealed by a stranger, a form with a box on it and two words in it, a store log with a date in it that nobody can explain, a page of the hall's own paper sitting on a shelf in Cordell under a register, a copy of a copy in a county library on a shelf that no Office vehicle can reach, nineteen hands on a wall in nineteen different hands, and a man who had said yes out loud in a records room in Havenport and could not take it back.

He had gone looking for a person and there had not been one, and the not having one was not a failure of the search. **The answer moved one building over, exactly as the Office said it would**, and the second building had given them a form, and a clerk who was honest, and a word printed in about nineteen eighty-odd because somebody had thought about it carefully, and nine people who were still nine and were not going to be anything else, and a woman who had been right in a corridor and a woman who had been right at a desk and a man in a hallway who had been right about a block number, and not one of them had owed him anything, and two of them had cost him something and one of them had cost somebody else more.

The ward was standing.

The round was on the nail. The four draws were signed. The laundry was running. Ansel was on his frame and awake at the wrong hour. A nurse had been refused a page and had taken it. A function had a name against it and a condition in a second ink. A wall had four more lines on it and was nobody's.

Elias Rook had not built any of it, had not maintained any of it, had not voted on it, and was not the reason for one single part of it, and that sentence had been false every day for six weeks and was true on Wednesday evening, and he let it be true, which was the hardest thing he did that week and the only thing he did that cost him nothing.

Then he went to the front room and read the board, because there were two questions on it and the second half of the older one was still a bracket with nothing under it, and somebody nine years old had written a third thing underneath both of them and would have to read it again tomorrow at seven.

Outside, a town that had lost a tunnel in September was doing its ordinary evening in a district where the lights went off at midnight, and nobody in Cordell had come back with a name, and nobody in Havenport had been told, and the seal on the tunnel stood, and the eleven meters was a length with a day on it, and a man behind a wall inside it was fifty-three days in and unwritten at his own request, and the only person in the business who had ever been asked to be a name and had said no was sitting in a chair in another city with ninety dollars a night going out of his own pocket.

And in a records room on Ash Street, a woman of thirty-nine was somewhere in another city finishing a shift, having written two words in a box in the spring of two thousand and thirty-five, correctly, with no idea that it was going to be the whole of what anybody had.

It is written down in five places. It is in a form and it is in a wall and it is on a library shelf in a county and it is in a notebook in a garage, and there is a copy in a tin, and the tin is on a table with a rubber band round it twice.
