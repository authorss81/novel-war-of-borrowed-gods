# Chapter 100 — The Board At Seven

Petar Lisk read the board at seven on Friday morning, the twenty-eighth of November, the eighty-third day, and read it out loud twice, and there were nine people in the front room at seven and it was the eighty-third morning he had done it.

Cliff Marner was fifty-three days out of the ground. Della Marner was eighty-three days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was eighty-three days in, inside a tunnel that was sealed on the thirty-third day and classified in nine words, and nobody had gone to the loading lip in eleven weeks and nobody was going to and the handset line was dead, and the eleven meters was a length with a day on it and nothing else.

He read it from the top, which is the order it is in.

---

At the top of that wall, above everything, in a hand that belongs to nobody in the building, is a fourth column.

The heading is the Office's own printed heading, copied out in chalk by a man in a clean orange jacket who had run out of forms on the thirty-fourth day of September and had written the last column of an impoundment return on the wall of a ward instead. Twenty-eight lines. A date against each one. A figure against each one. **And the heading says ITEMS, because that is what the Office's return says, and the return's fourth column is the column for what was taken out of a building by an order, and in twenty-eight lines there are two rooms, a laundry, eleven boxes of somebody else's evidence, a terminal, four anchor lamps, a corridor repeater, a corridor light, and six access codes, and not one of those is a thing you can carry away in two hands and the ones you can are the ones that matter least.**

Underneath that column are nineteen hands.

They are nineteen people who wrote, on Tuesday night, in chalk, under that column, what happened in the records room at twenty past two in the afternoon, in their own hands, in nineteen different sizes, and none of the nineteen wrote the same sentence twice. There are nineteen hands and twenty-eight lines and the two numbers are not required to agree, and Nadi Bell said that in October about two other numbers in this building and she was right then as well.

Under the nineteen hands are two names.

**CLIFF MARNER**, spelled the way Cliff spells it, in four hands, in chalk, and as of Thursday there is a designation in a file on the Meridian with a form number and a case reference and a Deputy Director's signature on it that says *not a passenger, a person recovered from a designated site under an inquiry reference*, and it is a field that did not exist in this city in 2042.

It is going to stay in chalk. Chalk is what a person wrote about a person. A printer's face is what an institution wrote about a person, and this building has spent eleven weeks learning the difference between those two things, and it is going to keep both.

**PERPETUA OYELARAN**, in Inez Sato's chalk, under a seal, with a time against it, and nobody has crossed it out and nobody is going to, and she is under Ash Street, and there is no designation for her and there is not going to be one, and she is not a rescue and she is not a deadline and she is not a row in anything.

Then the three questions, all of them in the same nine-year-old capitals, and the second half of the oldest one still a bracket with nothing under it, and it is going to stay like that until somebody who is not any of them writes something under it.

And then, above the date, in his own hand and small, because there was room:

> **A HALL THAT AGREES IS SAFER. THAT IS WHAT WAS SAID. I AM NINE AND I DO NOT AGREE AND I AM WRITING IT DOWN ANYWAY. PETAR L.**
>
> **NOBODY OWNS THE ANSWER SO NOBODY CAN GIVE IT AWAY. PETAR L. — I AM NINE AND I HAVE READ IT SIX TIMES.**
>
> **NONE OF THE THREE THINGS ON THIS WALL NEEDED A PERSON TO DECIDE THEM. THAT IS THE SHAPE. I DO NOT KNOW THE MEANING. PETAR L. — I AM NINE AND I HAVE READ IT FOUR TIMES.**
>
> **19 NOVEMBER. — P. L.**

And the date was four days old, because he had decided on the eighteenth of November, on his own account, with nobody having asked him, that a hall which puts two dates on a wall in one week is a hall starting to expect something, and he had been right about that, and about most of it, and about none of the other things.

Petar Lisk read all of that out loud twice, and then he read the last line twice as well, and it was the one he had written at about half past six the previous evening in the corridor while four people watched and none of them said anything, and it is the one this building is going to be described by, in a document, in about four years, in one sentence, correctly and without cruelty:

> **WHO BENEFITS WHEN A BRANCH IS OFFICIALLY DELETED — AND WHY DOES THE FORM CALL IT AN ERROR?**

"There is the answer under it and there is not," he said. "There is an answer to the first half. Mr. Rook has it and I have read it six times and it is in twenty-two hands and I am not one of the twenty-two and I am not going to be given it, because the man who said it asked in writing not to have his name on it and he is right.

"And the second half is why it comes back round to the form, and nobody has asked the form anything, because **you cannot ask a form a question and the form does not answer and that is not a fault in the form. That is what a form is.** So I have written it down, and the wall is going to be full after this one, and somebody has to build another wall, and it is not going to be anybody in this building and it is not going to be me."

He put the pencil in his pocket.

"There is one more thing and I am not writing it up because it is not a fact, it is a feeling." He looked round the nine people. "**Tuesday there was a door in the north wall that is not on any of the drawings, and I was in the dry end, and my card came off the rail, and nobody came and got me. I have been thinking about that for three days and I have got to the bottom of it and the bottom of it is that somebody read my name out loud and wrote *out* against it and then did not come and found me anyway, and that is the whole of the thing and I do not know what to call it and I am not going to write it down because it is mine.**"

---

Elias Rook had been in the room for all of it and had said nothing, and at about twenty past seven Lena Mir told him there was a box in the corridor that had to go into the records room and wanted a fourth pair of hands.

It was about the size of a shoebox and it was not heavy. He had it in his right hand under a minute later and he went down the corridor with it.

There is a step at the end of that corridor, one step, and it is there because a door frame from nineteen fifty-odd was put in when the place was converted, and the ramp is outside by the fire door and the ramp is four meters of gentle slope to a landing that goes all the way round.

Mara Okafor was coming the other way with a cup of something in each hand and stopped and said, "Use the ramp."

"I know," Elias Rook said.

"It is one step."

"**I know it is one step, and I have got a box in one hand, and I am going to use the ramp, and I want it written down somewhere that I said I was going to use the ramp before anybody had to ask me twice.**"

He went out through the fire door and round by the ramp and up the gentle slope to the landing and along it, four meters, and in through the other door, and put a shoebox of somebody's evidence on a table in a records room in a building on Ash Street at about ten past seven on the eighty-third day.

He stood there afterwards with his right hand on the table and did not make anything of it, and it was not a symbol, and his left hand was still gone and it was still gone for the rest of that year.

"**Mr. Rook. You do not have to come on Tuesday.**"

"I know that," Elias Rook said. "The slot is mine. It is on the rota and it is mine and the depot says they will not hold it twice, and that is a fact about a Tuesday and not about me, and I am going to be there at half past six."

Mara Okafor looked at him for a second longer than was necessary, and then said, "Right," and went and did the round.

He did not ask her for anything. Nobody asked him for anything. **In eleven weeks of rooms, not one person in that building had ever asked him for a promise and not one person had ever asked him to make one, and he had been braced for it on about nine separate mornings, and on this one, in particular, he was not going to be the man who asked.**

The envelope to Nell Rook was in the front of his notebook and it was not sent and the reason was four words long and the reason was that the road was sealed. The road was sealed. Eleven meters of a lower service run was a length with a day on it and there was a man in it, alive, eighty-three days, who had asked them once not to write him down and had not been written down, and the tunnel was nine words and a signature, and nothing this morning touched any of that and nothing this morning was going to.

The ward was standing. It had been standing for eighty-three days on batteries, lamps, seam glass, a wooden frame, an analog map, a corridor light, a laundry, a bar on a feeder that any household could cut, and twenty-two resident cards and twenty-eight people, and it had been standing without him for most of those days, and it was going to go on standing without him, and he was not on the lip and he was never going to be on the lip again.

Petar Lisk read the board at seven the next morning as well. There are three questions on it and two names and a column that says *items* and nineteen hands underneath it and a nine-year-old's closing line, and it is still there, and nobody owns it, and that is the only reason it is still there.
