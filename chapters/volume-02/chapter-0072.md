# Chapter 72 — What a Second Trip Costs

He telephoned the depot at ten past six on the Friday morning from the corridor at Ash Street, and R. Dunleavy took the call in the bay.

"Six thirty is the slot."

"Yes."

"So write it down here, while I am on the line, because I am not going to have it be a thing you remember." There was a pen sound. "I have written *not on site 0610, called, meeting on site.* That is the whole of what I am writing and I am not writing *unwell* and I am not writing *personal* because those two words would be a thing you had to explain to a man in an office in March. **You are four hours late before you have started and I would rather know it at six ten than at eleven.**"

---

The meeting was at seven in the morning with four people in the front room, and Inez Sato said why in one sentence.

"Ru Sabar is on the laundrette circuit and her card goes back at ten past six, and by seven in the evening half the people who have to be in this are in a clinic corridor or on a powered frame or asleep, and I would rather have a small meeting at seven than a large one at seven in the evening, and I would rather say that out loud than have four of you go home and say the hall only meets when it is convenient."

Four of them were in the room and one of them was on a frame. Ansel Duarte had come into the front room on his own, which takes eleven minutes, with a blanket over his knees and a book he was not reading, and Lena Mir had said he would be awake at four and he had been, and nobody said so.

Elias Rook put the depot form on the table face up, which is what she had told him to do, and the four of them read it.

---

"A certified copy is two dollars forty," Inez Sato said. "That is the number in Cordell and the number in the county library's letter. A day in a car is forty-one dollars, which is the number we were given in October, and I want it on this table that it has not gone up. Nobody has put a war on a bus fare.

"And we have nothing, because we spent the last eleven hundred dollars on a dry end in the second week of October. The reason there has not been a dollar since is that we are not allowed to have one. **The hall's money is not a budget. The hall's money is a consent card.** Every watt in this building comes on somebody's card with their name on it, and the moment a thing costs money that is not somebody's power it comes out of somebody's power, and the somebody is in this building, and the amount is not forty-one dollars. The amount is a week."

She put her hand flat on the table and then took it off, which she had been told to do by somebody once.

"So the second trip does not cost forty-one dollars. It costs a card for a week. And it will not be mine, because I do not hold one for the hall, and it will not be Mrs. Sabar's, because her condition is *it is not mine after six*, and I am not going to break another person's condition in this building to buy a photograph, and I would like it recorded that I have been asked twice this month to break somebody's condition for a document and the answer is the same both times."

"Who pays, then," Ansel Duarte said.

"That is the second thing. The first thing is the number, and the number is a week, and I have said it out loud in the room, and any person here whose card is the week can stop this now and nobody would say a word about it."

Nobody stopped it. The four of them had all given a card for something this month and knew exactly what a week of that was, and not one of them was willing to be the one who said *mine*.

---

"It was worth it," Ansel Duarte said.

He said it to the table and not to Elias, in a voice built for a room with more people in it.

"Nobody has answered that, so I am going to say it and then answer it, because I am the one in this room who cannot walk to a hospital and the answer changes what my week is. Was it worth it."

The laundry was running. In the corridor past the doorway his frame was where he had left it with a book on the seat.

"I cannot answer that and I am not going to pretend to," Elias Rook said. "I have been in two buildings this month. One of them was a hospital bed in Cordell for four hours and one was a store room, and both of those are things that happened to somebody else."

"That is the first honest thing anybody has said in this room in a fortnight, and it is not an answer."

"No."

"Then it goes on the table that nobody has answered it," Inez Sato said, and wrote three words on the pad, and Petar Lisk is not the only person in this building who takes minutes.

She wrote: **no answer given.**

---

"The hall does not spend a dollar on a second trip."

That was not what anybody had braced for. Tarin Voss, who had come in late and was standing by the door with his coat on, said, "That is not a decision. That is the absence of one."

"It is a decision, it is mine, and I made it at ten past six this morning before any of you were up. **The hall's money is a card, so the hall does not spend money. The hall asks.** And what I am asking is this.

"There is a local-history room in the county seat. It is public, it is in a building the Havenport Continuity Office does not have a building in, and on the last day of October a woman in it put a copy of our papers in a box on a shelf and put a card in her own catalogue saying they are not to be removed without the written consent of the people who wrote them. I have been thinking about that card for four days.

"So I telephoned her yesterday afternoon and told her the answer I wanted. A person from this building could sit in her room for as long as it takes. The hall would pay a bus fare and nothing else. The person would be named in a book in her room the way a donor is named, and the room could refuse to say who sent them. I want that last one in writing from her and not from me, because if the room says it then it is the room's arrangement and not a favor we are asking for."

The room was quiet.

"She said yes," Inez Sato said, "in one sentence, and then she said the second half of it, which was that if a person is going to be in her building then the person is going to be somebody who can be asked what they are doing there, and not somebody carrying a thing for other people who are not in the room."

"Monday," Joon Park said from the corner.

He had stood there for the whole forty minutes with a notebook and had not sat down, because when he stands he can see both doors.

"The room is open Sunday from ten to twelve. That is the store room's public hour, it is the only hour that building gives the public, and it exists because a preservation levy pays for it. Whatever gets looked at gets looked at in two hours on a Sunday, by somebody who can be asked, and not in five hours on a Monday with a form."

---

Then she offered it to him, in front of the four of them, in the form the rule requires: any offer and any condition goes to the people who pay for it, in the words it was said in, before it is acted on.

"Two people can go, and one of them has to be the man who can read a form. I am not going to pretend I do not know who that is, and I am not going to say it in a way that makes it an instruction. **You are the man who holds the tin.** If you go, it goes in the tin and the tin goes on a bus, and the tin has been on a bus before and came back."

Elias Rook looked at the tin on the table, which was on a table nine weeks out of ten.

"No," he said.

"Give a reason."

He gave one. It took about four seconds, and then he gave the real one, which was two sentences and which he had not planned.

"I am the man who holds the tin. If I go, this building has one fewer person who knows where everything is, and everybody here will hear about a shelf in another city before they hear it from me. And the second reason is worse and I am saying it out loud because we have a rule. **I have spent nine weeks being the man everybody decides is the right one, and I have enjoyed it, and I did not know until this morning that I was enjoying it, and a man who enjoys being the right one is a man who cannot say no, and there is going to be a day when that costs somebody a card.** So no."

Nobody argued. Inez Sato wrote something on the pad and read it back before she turned it round.

"It says: *offer refused, reason given aloud, twice, and accepted; the refusal is his to make and not mine to overrule.* Say it if that is wrong."

"That is right," Elias Rook said.

"Then it is Mr. Park, and I am not deciding the transport this morning, because the car is forty-one dollars and the bus is not, and the bus takes forty minutes and the bus does not know whose card it is."

---

The form was still on the table at eight o'clock and all four of them had read it and nobody had picked it up.

Ansel Duarte turned his head to look at it.

"That is your Tuesday," he said.

"It is."

"And your Friday was this morning and you were four hours late for it."

"Ten past six, on the telephone, with the foreman writing it down while I was on the line, because that is the truth and not a nicer version of it."

"Then somebody ought to be told what that sheet says," Ansel said, "because in six weeks there is going to be somebody in a room who says the man from Ash Street did not have a job in October, and there is going to be a piece of paper in front of them that says he had one, and it was on a Tuesday, and he was on it."

---

He got to the far bank at half past ten and R. Dunleavy gave him the last row of the day and did not say one word about the four hours, and worked with him on it for eighty minutes, and when the fog came in off the estuary at eleven and stopped everything, the foreman read the sheet back before he signed it.

"Twelve hoses, twelve passes, one retest on the second because the coupling wanted reseating and that is not the hose's fault. **You were not here for the first two hours and I have written that you were not here and I have written that you telephoned at six ten and I am not going to write it as a lateness, because you telephoned.** That is the same fact and it is a different line, and in four years one of those two lines is what somebody reads.

"Next Friday is gone. The letter said so and I meant it and I would have been the one to say it if you had not beaten me to it. **These will not be held twice.** Tuesday is yours. If you want Friday back, ask me in writing, on the form, with the date on it, and I will answer in four lines, and one of the four will be the truth."

Elias Rook took the sheet and looked at the two entries side by side.

"Thank you," he said.

"I put that in no file," Dunleavy said, "because there is no file, and I am telling you anyway, and if you repeat it back to me I will deny it."

Through the glass he could see the fog coming up off the water and the far bank, and the road to Cordell forty minutes along it, and a bus that ran that road four times a day at two dollars sixty each way, and he knew both of those numbers because he had looked them up on Thursday night and had not told anybody that he had looked them up.
