# Chapter 75 — The Signature

The request went in at nine minutes past ten on Monday morning, the fifty-eighth day, and Rufus Aitken wrote the time on the register and said the eleven days started then, and then he got the volumes himself.

He brought all five out on the trolley, and Wren Okonjo came down from the counter and stood at the end of the table, and nobody opened a volume before she was in the room. She had six years at the counter, could give a credential count in four seconds and nothing else at all, and on that Monday she spent five hours standing still.

Elias Rook asked for the autumn of ninety-eight, and the volume came onto the table, and it was heavier than a book is supposed to be. He held it with his right hand and his forearm and the edge of the table, because his left hand has gone completely and it went in September and is not coming back, and he had asked for a chair with a low back and there was one, and nobody commented on any of it.

---

There were forty lines to a page and a printed grid and four printed headings, *time*, *unit*, *item*, *action*, and the year was a printed column at the left and not a heading.

Line forty-one of a page for the seventeenth of October was signed at four minutes past four in the morning.

The time column had **04:12** in it, and the item column had six words about a relay cabinet, and the unit column had a unit in a block, and the last column had a signature: a woman's name, in a small upright hand that was not a careful hand and was not a fast one.

"Somebody has to say the time," Joon Park said. "**Line forty-one, seventeenth of October, signed at four twelve.** That is the fact and it is the whole of the fact, and I am not saying one word about the hour, because the hour is a fact, and I know what happens in a room when somebody starts being clever about a fact."

---

And then Elias Rook put his thumb on the page above the signature, because there was a rectangle of paper there that was a different color.

The field was gone. Not blank. *Gone.*

About two inches by one, taken out with a blade. The cut was clean on three sides and not quite clean on the fourth, and there was a small tear at the lower corner where the blade had gone the last of the way and the paper had wanted to stay. The binding was intact and nothing had been pulled out. Somebody had laid a blade on a bound working log and taken a rectangle off it, neatly enough that a person could have mistaken it for a printing fault for about nine seconds, and then put the volume back on the shelf.

Underneath there was a strip of the page a line and a half deep, and a partial word on it, and Elias Rook had to turn the book eleven degrees toward the light to read it, and then read it again.

**REQUES…**

Four letters, a stub of a fifth, and then a clean edge.

"It said *requested by*," Joon Park said, "and the heading went with the field, because the field and the name on it are one object and somebody decided that, and nobody decides that by accident."

A person can hide a person, and a person makes a mess, and Elias Rook had spent six weeks learning what a decision looks like from the inside. This was not a decision. This was a Tuesday: a person with a blade and a form in front of them, a field that was not applicable, a reviewer's box, and a form that asks what happened to a *field*.

---

He checked the index, because it was the only thing on that table that could be wrong, and it took four minutes.

Joon Park went down the entries with his finger. Forty was there. Forty-one was there. Forty-two was there. Every line number for October and November of that year, nothing out of sequence and nothing missing.

"Nothing is missing from the index," he said, in the flat voice he uses when he is reading something out loud, "because the index records lines and not fields. There is no column in it for a field. So a field can be taken off a page in a bound volume and the index cannot notice, and it did not notice on the day it was done and it is not going to notice in four years. **That is not a hole in the record. That is what a line is.**"

"Two inches by one," Elias Rook said. "Cut on three sides, torn very slightly on the fourth, and the heading gone with it. Write that as a measurement and not as a feeling. In four years the only way anybody will argue with me about this is with a figure."

Then he refused the other thing, out loud, once.

"And I am refusing the shape. There is a movement book in this building with a line dated in a year that does not match the thing it records, and there is now a working log in the same store with a piece of paper cut out of it, and both are the same year. **If I write that shape down, the annotation in two thousand and eleven stops being a record and starts being a story, and I have watched a document stop being a record twice this month and I am not doing it a third time.**"

---

The annotation was on the flyleaf at the back, in a different ink from anything else on the page. Four lines, beginning with the word *reviewed*, giving a year, giving a unit, saying the field was **superseded** and saying it was **not applicable**, and signed in a block with a unit number in it and no name in it anywhere, because a unit can sign a review and a person cannot be found inside one.

Two words in a box, in a different ink, in two thousand and eleven, about a field, on the correct day, in the correct space, by somebody who was not hiding anything. It is the same shape as the eleventh field on a renumbering form in this same region nine years later, and the same shape as a box printed in about nineteen eighty-odd by somebody who thought very carefully about a field.

Elias Rook said it out loud, once, and did not say it again:

**"A signature is a fact with a date on it and it is not an explanation."**

---

Wren Okonjo found the name in eleven seconds, in a staff index that goes back to the end of the war, on a page nobody uses. She read it out once, because somebody had to, and then shut the book.

Hester Lund. Sixty-eight. A caretaker at a primary school on Sycamore Row, three streets away, eleven years in the post, and the index carries her start date and nothing else about her that anybody has ever needed.

"She is not a villain," Joon said. "If any of us walks out of that door and says that word, it will have been said by us and not by her, and she will not be in the room to be asked."

Then he asked Okonjo for the review file. It was a thin paper folder, and on the covering sheet, in the same small upright hand, was the response of the originating signatory, in the approved form, and the answers were correct.

Joon Park read it, and then copied out every line of it in his own hand — his own words for the headings, the woman's words for the answers — dated it, and read the copy back to the room before he closed the notebook, and added nothing. At the bottom he wrote the number of the answer, because somebody had to keep the count, and the number was two.

"That is the second time she has answered in that form. The file does not give me the first one and I am not going to ask, because it is not in this building and it is not our business. **There is going to be a third time. I have written that down tonight, in a room, so that nobody afterwards can say I did not know — and I am not going to be the man who makes it a fourth.**"

---

And then the whole morning came to the one sentence it had been walking toward for ten days.

To use the thing Hester Lund had signed, they would have to put her name in a proceeding. The line is the document, the document has a signature on it, an inquiry takes evidence, and the moment her name went into an instrument it would be a name in a file with a case number on it, and a case number on a name is a thing that cannot be taken off that name by anybody in this business, including us.

"We are not doing it," Elias Rook said.

"Your sentence first," Joon Park said, "and it is going in the notebook, and the notebook is worse than a minute, because nobody can order a thing out of a notebook."

Then Elias Rook said the price out loud, because nobody else in the room was going to say it, and because a refusal that does not say what it costs is a favor.

"**This is the best document any of us has found in nine weeks and we are not going to use it, and the book is weaker for that, and it will be weaker in a year, and I am not going to be the man who explains to a Deputy Director in October that we had a good thing and did not spend it.** It goes on the schedule as a thing we saw. That is all it is going to be."

---

And the second thing he said in that store room was the one that was wrong, in the mirror of the way he had been wrong about a holding number, and it took him until Wednesday to see it.

He was thinking that the name was not in anything at all, and that therefore the page was safe, and four people in that city had read it, and every one of them was competent, and every one of them would one day have to describe what they saw to somebody who was not us. A page four competent people have looked at is not a page with nothing on it. It is a page on its way into a description, and the description will be accurate, and nobody who reads it will ever know there was a version in which nobody decided anything.

He had learned in October that a record which cannot be taken is a record that cannot be corrected. The other half is worse, and he had it written down by the Wednesday:

**A record that cannot be taken is a record that cannot be corrected by anybody, including the people who were there.**

---

Rusk took the call himself at twenty past three, with no recorder on the line, and neither man said the year and neither man said her name.

"You have not been given a name and there is not going to be one," Elias Rook said. "And the reason is not the one you think. It is not honorific and it is not tact. It is that we are now the body that turned up a document in a file with a case number on it, and the only way that document enters your inquiry is with a person attached to it, and the person attached to it is a woman of sixty-eight who has answered two institutions in a form and has not asked to be asked a third time.

"And I will tell you what that does. It is the best document any of us has found in nine weeks, and it is the best document because of the thing that is wrong with it, and **we cannot use it, and the reason we cannot use it is the reason you will want it for, and I would rather bring you a true thing I could not use than a usable thing with a woman in it.**"

"That is fair," Rusk said. "I am doing something with it and I want you to hear it first. **You have shown me that this office's predecessors edited a book. That is not evidence about what you did. It is evidence about what I have to answer for.**"

Elias Rook stood in a store room with a telephone in his right hand and a bound volume under his forearm and did not say a word for about nine seconds.

"Yes," he said. "That is the fairest thing anybody has said to us in nine weeks, and I would like it minuted, and I am not going to argue with it."

"I am not running a trial. I accept the whole of it into the file and rule on none of it, and I have said that to you twice and I am saying it a third time, and one day somebody will hold me to it and I would rather it was on a recording. Send me the date and the block."

"Tuesday morning. Not today. Today the woman exists as a woman and not as a block."

"That," Rusk said, "is the sixth true thing, and it is worth less than the other five."

---

Rufus Aitken had been at the end of the aisle all day, and at about twenty to four on the Monday afternoon he came and put both hands flat on the trolley.

"I am going to do a thing I have not done since a Wednesday in a different year, and I am telling you before I do it and not after, because the last time I told nobody and it was nine days before anybody found out what I had done.

"Line forty-one, seventeenth of October. Signed at four twelve." He read it in the voice he uses at a counter. "Unit, block. Item, six words. Action, six words. Time, four twelve. Signature, the name of a woman who is sixty-eight, a school caretaker three streets from here, and has never once been asked what it meant."

He stopped.

"Nineteen years ago I read a page out loud to a man in interview room two, because I thought *heard* and *told* were two different men. I was right and it changed nothing, and the file did not change, and I have not done it since, on purpose. **I am doing it now because the person in the line is alive and is not in the building, and that is the difference, and I am not claiming it is enough.** By Friday it will be in four books and it is going to change the file by nothing at all."

Elias Rook put the volume down. His right hand was shaking and he put it flat on the table and left it there, and Joon Park wrote down the time and not the reason.
