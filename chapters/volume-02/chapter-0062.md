# Chapter 62 — Forty Minutes by an Ordinary Road

They got into the car at a quarter past seven on Tuesday morning, the forty-fifth day, and the tin sat on Elias Rook's knees for the whole of the forty minutes with a rubber band round it twice.

Joon Park drove, because the booking needed a name and the name was the hall's records keeper, and the hall's records keeper was not him, and he had said so out loud on Sunday in front of four people and nobody wrote it down.

"One thing before we get there," Joon said, with nine cars in front of them and a river on their left. "I am not going to be the man who says one thing out there and another thing in here. If I write a sentence down in that building, I am writing the same sentence here tonight. There is not going to be a tidier version of today than the one that happened."

"I am not going to promise you what we find."

"You have promised nothing since the fourth week and it has cost you four times," Joon said. "I am not asking."

---

The records center is a long low building with an addition on the north side that everybody in Cordell would tell you was a hospital annex and is not.

The addition is the part that undoes you. Same building material, same decade, same walk, and it is the regional transit family's reception hall, with a different management, a different budget, a different sign over the door, and **no relationship whatever to the records center except a corridor.** Ninety meters. The young woman who wrote Cliff Marner's name on a pad on the ninth day, in a young hand, had been standing that far from a store room with nine credentials in it, for forty-five days. Nobody in either building had a reason to know the other one existed. That was two budgets, not a conspiracy.

---

Rufus Aitken met them at the counter at five past nine, was courteous in a way that cost him something, and did not offer them a chair, because a chair is a thing you offer a person who is going to wait.

"You have a holding number," he said. "Say it and I will look at it. First I am going to ask you one question, and it is not what the box is for."

"That is fair."

"The question is whether you are the person the number was given to."

"I am not," Elias said. "It was read out on a telephone by a woman in a room I work in, off a consolidation schedule dated the eleventh of May two thousand and thirty-nine, and neither of us has been in this building before."

"Then you are a bearer," Aitken said, "and bearers are the ordinary case, and the ordinary case gets the same eleven days as everybody else, and I would like that said out loud in this building once so that nobody afterwards tells you it was special."

"Please say it again where Joon can hear it."

Aitken looked at the man with the notebook, and said it again, in the same voice, at the same temperature.

Then he took the number, did not write it down, and came back with a slip the size of a playing card.

"Regional mapping consolidation, store three, second cart. It went out on Friday for a conservation run and it is not in this building. The second cart comes back at a quarter to four, and after that the item goes to its shelf, and then it is a shelf and not a box, and the shelf number is not the holding number. Do you see the difference between those two facts?"

"Yes," Elias said. "I have been told it by somebody with a queue in another city."

"Then you know more about it than the last four people through that door."

---

He would not be told what the box was for, and he did it without any trouble at all.

"I have a store number, a volume, a box and a date," Elias said. "I am not going to tell you what is in it, because I have not opened it, and because I would be asking you to hold a thing you have not seen on my word."

"I would have refused anyway," Aitken said, "and you are right, and I appreciate the shape of the sentence."

He put them in interview room two, which is where a person goes who will not be left alone in a stack, and said so out loud. Then, at half past ten, with the door shut and no recorder on the table, he said the thing Elias had not known he had come for.

"Nineteen years on the counter, eleven in the store, and I have read a page out loud in that room exactly once, and I did not do it for you this morning, and I am telling you so that you do not spend the day hoping for it. Nineteen years ago a man came in with a missing-person question about his wife, and there was one page, and I read it out loud because I thought a man who has been told *there is no record* and a man who has *heard it read* are two different men. I was right, and it changed nothing. He went away with the page. The file did not change. I have never found out whether it was read, and if I had known I would have been told it was not my business.

"That was the only time. A page is not an act of care. It only feels like one, and I have stopped doing it on purpose."

Elias Rook wrote it down and could not think what it was for, and it turned out to be for the day after.

---

They were in that room five hours and a half and were not allowed into the store or onto the archive floor, and there was nothing in the building they were entitled to look anything up on.

What Elias did instead was read the room, which is what he is actually for.

Everything an institution tells you is on its walls. The notice about the eleven-day public-request period was on the wall, and under it, in smaller type, was the re-dating rule: a request received on a day the building is closed is registered on the next open day, and the waiting period begins then.

Nobody wants a visitor to read that. It is the sentence that turns a location into a state.

"So on Saturday," Elias said. "If a request comes in on Saturday—"

"Saturday we are open till one," Aitken said. "Sunday we are not open at all, except the store room, which has a public hour from ten till twelve because a preservation levy pays for it and nobody uses it. Monday to Friday from half past eight, and the counter shuts at half past four, and if you are not through the door at four it is tomorrow."

"Which is the store," Elias said.

"Which is a shelf," Aitken said. "And that is the question I have been waiting for since the car park. Everybody asks me where the box is. Nobody has ever asked me where the box stops being a box."

"Then we want Wednesday," Elias said. "Not today. We want the day it is a shelf."

Aitken looked at him a moment too long.

"You are the first person in a year who has asked me for a worse day," he said. "Wednesday it is. Ten in the morning, and you will be in the store room and not in here, and you will want a pencil and not a pen, and I will not have anybody say I let you in without a request, because I will have had to write something down myself."

"Write it down," Joon said.

"You are the notes," Aitken said. "I have been in rooms with four of you before and I read the one who is not writing."

---

Around one o'clock, Elias Rook understood the number, and he was not proud of the four minutes it took.

He had believed a holding number was a key that belonged to them. It had been in print since the eleventh of May two thousand and thirty-nine and had gone to every office holding a copy of the schedule, and this archive could not say who was holding it, because **a public records request is anonymous by design**, and a number on a schedule is that arrangement in a different typeface.

Aitken had said the good news and the bad news in one sentence at half past nine and Elias heard the good half. *It works. And it works for anybody who has it.* A broker would work. Rusk handed them a sentence anybody who could read a date could say out loud. He had been describing the building.

Joon Park wrote one line and turned the notebook away. "*The number is not ours. It is an address anybody can hold.*"

---

Wendell Pryce was in a corridor at half past three with a dead signal lamp in a cardboard box under his arm, talking to a young man with a trolley about nothing.

"— I worked the west apron line," Pryce said, without being asked and without lowering his voice. "I did not hold that credential. I never held that credential. I have said so to three people this week and I will say it to a fourth and to a fifth. It is not a favor to anybody. It is a fact about me and I would like it in whatever book you people keep."

Elias Rook, coming the other way, stopped.

"Wendell."

"Elias." The old man shifted the box. "I am not doing this for you. I want that straight, because in about four years somebody is going to write that a team got a signalman to open something. I am doing it because a man in a hut in two thousand and thirty-six kept something on paper and nobody has ever asked him for it, and because I have paid ninety dollars a night in this city for eight years to stand in a ring, and I am not going to be the man who let somebody else's number out of a block."

"You have not told me your number."

"I do not have your number," Pryce said. "I have told you I do not have it and I will keep telling you, and there is a difference between those, and you are smart enough to know what it is."

Then, in a public corridor, in a building where Elias had a claim to stand, Wendell Pryce said the rest of it out loud, to nobody.

"And I will tell you the thing that will waste your morning if you let it, because it wasted three weeks of mine. That number was issued in blocks. Signal huts knew the block, because you had to write it on a sheet to get into a cabinet. There is not one person in this city who could name the man it belonged to. There is a block, a sequence, a printer in Havenport, and a clerk in two thousand and thirty-five. You have come for a person and there is no person in this building. There is a slot, a sheet, and a woman somewhere who filled something in, and if you go at it the other way round you will be here until the roof comes in."

"A block number," Elias said.

"A block number and a sequence, and neither of them is a man," Pryce said. "That door is closed and I am closing it, and I would like it noted that I closed it out loud and to your face and that you asked me nothing first."

He went up the corridor with the lamp. Joon Park wrote all of it down, standing up, and added nothing to it.

---

The second cart came back at twenty to four and a man in a gray apron wheeled it through a door forty meters away without stopping at the counter, and Rufus Aitken came into interview room two and said, "Third box on the bottom shelf is yours by the holding number. You have until four."

At ten to four there was a box on a table. The top label had been replaced at some point in nineteen years with a printed strip in a regional typeface nobody in Havenport had seen.

Elias Rook put his right hand flat on the cardboard and did not open it, because it was not his and he was not to be alone with it.

"What is on top," Joon said.

"Instructions," Elias said. "And a second box printed on the front of it, and the second box is empty, and what is printed over the second box is a sentence about a field, and not one word about anybody."

---

He read it in the car park at ten past five, alone, with the tin in the boot. He had said a sentence in a corridor on the eleventh morning of October, with no wall and no witness: that he had not written one thing down, that he would have to answer *I do not know* if anybody asked him what happens next, and that he had never once said that sentence about a Friday.

Eleven days after Friday the thirty-fourth, standing in a car park in another city with dust on his coat, Elias Rook said it out loud, to nobody and to a car park:

**"I know what happens this morning."**

Then he got the notebook out and wrote down, in his right hand, four things two strangers had told him in one day, the fourth being that a page is not an act of care, and he did not know what any of it was for. He did not know what happens on Thursday either, and he noticed that he had not said so, and he put the date under the four and shut it.

On the road back neither of them put the radio on.
