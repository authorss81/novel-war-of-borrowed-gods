# Chapter 95 — The Vote Is Not Taken Yet

It was ten past ten on Sunday morning, the twenty-third of November, the seventy-eighth day, with nineteen people in the front room and two of them sitting on the floor because there were not enough chairs, which is how that room had been since September.

Cliff Marner was forty-eight days out of the ground and was in the chair by the window, and he was in the room, and he was not on the sheet, and he had asked about that on Friday and had been told, and had said *right*, and had not asked again. Della Marner was seventy-eight days at a desk in Cordell and was not in the room and did not know there was one. The man behind the wall was seventy-eight days in.

Petar Lisk had read the board at seven, out loud, twice, and said the wall was the same.

---

Inez Sato did not stand up and did not sit down, which is how everybody in that building knew she had already done the thinking and the room was going to be told.

"Three things first and all three of them are refusals, and I am saying them before I say anything else, and I would like the pad checked afterwards by anybody who wants to check it.

"**One. This building does not convene itself about a form.** That is a month old and it stands and nothing this morning touches it. **Two. There is no meeting, no form, no line on the pad and no vote about the finding, and the finding is not in this room and is not going to be in this room and nobody is going to be told it.** Those two were decided in October and in November and they are the reason this building is standing and I am not reopening them at eleven o'clock on a Sunday because I have become fond of an idea.

"**Three, and this is the one that is hard, and I am going to say it slowly and I would like nobody to help me.** On Thursday this building refused to do a thing, and it refused a thing it is good at, and it refused it because a document was about to start a process. **And this morning I am going to ask twenty-two people to decide something, and a decision is a process, and if I do this without saying out loud what the difference is then in about four years somebody will write that the hall that refused to convene itself about a form convened itself about a release in the same fortnight, and both of those will be true, and the sentence will have no shape at all.**"

Nobody said anything. It is not a meeting, and there is no minute of it, and the only record that it happened is nineteen people remembering it differently.

"The difference is this. **A form is a document that arrives in a building and starts something whether anybody in the building agrees or not. A decision is not that. A decision is the only thing this building has got that is actually its own, and if the twenty-two of us put a thing out on Wednesday morning then nobody on earth can be asked whether we agreed to it except the twenty-two of us, and the only place that answer exists is in the twenty-two of us.** So we are not convening ourselves about a form. We are going to take a thing that is true and ask the people who have to live in the building after it whether we are allowed to say it out loud. That is not a form. **That is the opposite of a form, and if anybody here thinks it is the same thing then I would rather they said so now than on Wednesday.**"

"Some of us think it is the same thing," Tarin Voss said, from the door.

"Say more."

"**A vote is a column with a heading and a date and a line for each person and a space at the bottom, and a column with a heading and a date and a space at the bottom is a pad, and Mr. Baros refused a column on Tuesday because a receipt says a thing came into this building on a date and it did not.** We are about to rule a column. On Wednesday there will be a sheet of paper with a heading and nineteen or twenty-two names on it and a date, and that is the thing he said no to, and I am the one who said a pad is a file that has not been given a stamp yet, and I am not backing off it in front of nineteen people because I said it in a corridor."

"That is the best thing anybody has said this morning," Inez Sato said, "and it is a reason and not an objection, and here is the answer to it.

"**The rule about the column is a rule about things that come in. This is a thing that goes out.** A receipt for the thing that comes in is a lie and a receipt for the thing that goes out is the only evidence there is that twenty-two people were asked. If we do not write it down, then on Wednesday a stranger stands up in a room and says the hall was never asked, and there is nothing on this earth to put in front of them, because we will have had it in our mouths and not in our hands." She did not turn round. "So we are writing it down, and I have known since Tuesday that we were going to, and I have been not enjoying it."

---

"It has to be read," Joon Park said.

"Read how."

"**One name at a time, and the refusal read out.**" He had the round sheet on his knees. "That is the procedure from the sixth of September. Nineteen said no to the Office's file in October and four said yes and five did not vote, and the four yeses went under the refusal in the same ink at the same size, and that is the only page in this whole business that a stranger can read and understand, and the reason it is that page is the reading.

"**It is the only procedure in this building that has never once been wrong, and I cannot tell you why, and I have been trying for two days.** I think it is because it is slow enough that a person has to hear the name before their own and has time to be frightened and then do it anyway."

"That is not a procedure," Nadi Bell said, "that is a tempo."

"**It is a procedure that happens to have a tempo in it, and I am the record keeper of this building and I have been looking for a better one for eleven weeks and there is not one, and I would like the finding that a tempo is a procedure written down by somebody other than me.**"

"It is going to cost somebody," Lena Mir said, from the back.

"It is going to cost exactly two people, and I know which two, and neither of them is in this room at the moment and I have not asked either of them." Joon Park closed the sheet. "And I am not going to soften it by not saying it, so: **two people are going to vote no on Wednesday, and their names are going to be in the instrument, in the same ink and at the same size as the yeses, and if they voted no and we hid it then the yeses are worth nothing and the release is worth nothing and the eleven weeks are worth nothing.** That is the price of doing this properly and it is not a surprise and it is not going to be sprung on anybody."

---

Petar Lisk had been standing at the back with the pencil he had not been given yet, holding it behind his back, for about half an hour.

"Mr. Lisk."

"Are we going to put it on the wall."

"No," Inez Sato said.

"Can I write it on the wall anyway and you can say it is not official."

"**No, and I am going to tell you why, and it is not because it is not official.** A vote is not a line. A line is one person writing one thing down, and that is what I have been asking you to do since September and it is good and it is the only thing in this building nobody can take. **A vote is twenty-two people, and you cannot put twenty-two people on a line, and if you try then the wall has got a number on it and a number on that wall is the one thing this building has spent eleven weeks refusing to have about anybody.**"

"Right," Petar Lisk said. "Then I will not write it. There is room for about two more lines and the closing one is going to be mine and I am not going to waste it on a vote that is not a line."

Nobody asked him what the closing one was, and he did not say, and Elias Rook noticed that he had been expecting to be asked and that he had been ready with an answer and had been left holding it.

---

"Nine o'clock is wrong," Tarin Voss said, from the door. "Whatever you are doing on Wednesday, do not do it at nine. A hall that votes at seven in the morning is a hall voting so it can go to work, and I want a hall voting because it has decided to."

"Seven," Inez Sato said, "and I am going to answer the rest of it and not the hour, because the hour is the easy half. **You are right that it looks like that from the street, and the only thing I have got against it is that twenty-two people are going to be asked one at a time, and a person cannot go to work and be read to at the same time. In the front room, on the chairs, and the board stays as it is, and the round is done before it and the four dark hours are still the four dark hours and I am not asking a single person in this room to give me an hour of their night for this.** Mr. Park takes the names. Mrs. Mir reads the no's out. I keep the instrument, and it is not in the residents' file and it is not in the tin, it is in the tin's place on the shelf with the band round it twice, because the file is the thing under a demand and the tin is ours and the instrument is going to be produced and I would like it to be a thing a person can hold."

"And us?" Elias Rook said. "The four of us. Are we in the room when it is read."

"You are in the room," Inez Sato said, "because you are going to have to stand in it while twenty-two people do a thing you asked them for, and I am not going to arrange for you to be elsewhere. Mr. Vale is in the room. Dr. Okafor is in the room or is not, at her own decision and with no explanation asked. And the twenty-two of us are the twenty-two of us and there is no observer and there is no second column for the people who came in with us."

---

He came in at half past ten and went up the ramp and stood at the rail for a while, and the thing he had understood in a room of nineteen people at eleven o'clock on a Sunday morning was not about a release.

**He was going to ask twenty-two people to vote on a thing that was true.**

Not a thing he believed. Not a thing he had a reason to believe. A thing that was true, that had been arrived at by other people, in a records room, without him, and that could not be improved and could not be argued with and had no author in it, so that it could be carried by anybody, and it fitted in a coat.

He was going to carry it to twenty-two people on Wednesday morning at seven and ask them to put their names next to it.

"That is the expensive part," he said out loud, to nobody, on a corridor, in a building with a wire rail and twenty-two cards on it. "Not Wednesday. **Asking.**"

Nobody had asked him what it cost. That was the point of it. In eleven weeks nobody in that building had once asked him what a thing cost, because they all knew, because they had all paid something, and the price of a thing you did not pay anything for is that somebody else pays it in the morning in front of you, one name at a time, and you have to stand in the room.

Petar Lisk read the board at seven the next morning, out loud, twice. There were three questions on it and one date and room for about two more lines, and at ten past nine on Monday morning a man of sixty-four was going to come into the front room with the chairs pushed out and put eight pages on a table, and the hall was not going to be asked about that either.
