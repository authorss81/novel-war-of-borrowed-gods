# Chapter 88 — One Question

Kessler Hardware opens at ten on a Sunday and Duane Prewitt is one of the three days it is open, and Joon Park had established that on the Tuesday from the sticker on the door, and had gone at ten, and had asked Elias Rook to come, and Elias Rook had come because the leg is worse on a Sunday and because he was not going to be asked twice.

He said nothing.

That is the whole of what Elias Rook did in that shop for fifty-one minutes. He stood with his right hand on a rack of hose clips and he said nothing, and Joon Park asked one question, and Duane Prewitt answered it, and a man of nineteen on a ladder restocked the top shelf the whole time and did not come down, and the fan heater ticked.

"What did you have in front of you," Joon Park said, "on that night?"

"Paperwork and a man," Duane Prewitt said. "That is the honest order, paperwork first, and I have thought about that for thirty-nine years and it still comes out in that order.

"It was a DS-4. That is what the form was called. It is not a report about a person, it is a return about a site, and it has to go back to the district by eight in the morning or the shift does not close, and that is the only reason there is a clock in any of it.

"It had a row for the site and a row for the time and two printed headings with a box at the end of each. One of those boxes is where the man who was hurt signs. One is where the officer signs. **That is all it had. There was no row on that sheet for a witness at all.** Not a small one. We had a witness. He was standing there. He was asked to sign and he said where does it go, and I said on the form, and he said which bit, and there was no which bit.

"And the man on the floor could not sign. And the officer's box is the officer's.

"So I wrote on the reverse, in the space they leave for remarks, where you put *no further action* or *crew stood down*. And I wrote that I was entering consent on behalf of the man. And I signed it and put the time on it, and it went to the district at twenty to seven.

"And I want to be exact about the words, because it has been thirty-nine years and I have thought about it and I am not going to let you have it wrong. **I did not write *I consent*. I wrote that the officer was entering consent.** That is a different sentence and I chose it in about four seconds and I have never been sure it was the right difference and I have never been sure it was the wrong one either."

He put a box of screws down on the counter.

"Was it the right thing to do. I was on nights. I had a man on the floor and a form that wanted a signature and about nine minutes of shift left, and the choice was to send in a return with a hole in it or a return with a sentence in it.

"A hole in that form said the district was on that site and nobody present could say what happened. **A sentence in the margin said somebody was standing there who could say what happened, and I was him, and I was still standing there, and the ambulance was twenty minutes out.** That is the whole of the reason. I will not stand here and tell you I did it for the law. I did it because I did not want to be the reason the paper had a hole in it.

"And why it was not enough." He set the box down square. "**Because a person can be written in by anybody.**

"That is all of it. There is no way on that form, and there has never been a way on any form I have ever seen, to tell the difference between me writing myself in because I was standing on the floor, and a man in a warm office in this city writing a man's name in because writing a name in is easier than writing a hole. I have had that in my head for about twenty years and I have got nowhere with it. **You can put that on a wall if you want. It is not against anybody. It is about paper.**"

Nobody put it on a wall.

---

He got down off the ladder, came over, and said one more thing that nobody had asked him for.

"You have both been here twice now and neither of you has asked me the thing, and I am going to say it, because I have never said it out loud and it has been sitting in me since about the Wednesday.

"A woman came in on the Monday. Four in the afternoon, maybe twenty past. She asked me was the man all right. I said he was. She said thank you and she went out.

"**That is the only time in thirty-nine years that anybody has come back to that counter about that night.** She gave me her name and I have not got it, and I did not write it down, because at the time I did not know it was going to be the only one."

The shop was quiet for a moment and the heater ticked.

"That is all," Duane Prewitt said. "You did not ask me for it."

---

Joon Park wrote it in the notebook, in his own hand, and then he said the next thing out loud, in the shop, before either of them had to ask him.

"I am going to tell you now that I am not going to use it, so that you are not sitting there waiting to find out.

"One: there is nothing behind it. No letter, no card, no register, no second person. It is one man's memory of one woman's face from thirty-nine years ago, and mine is the second-best version of it in this city and I would not put it in front of a jury and I would not put it in front of a Deputy Director.

"Two: if it goes into a document then it becomes a document about a woman who does not know she is in one. **She never found out that night existed. She came in to ask about a man. You do not get to keep that and also use it.** I have sat in a building with a case number on it since October and I have watched a whole week of people explain that a form with no line for *who is asking* is the reason a woman forty minutes away cannot get a piece of paper, and I am not going to be the man who builds a new one out of a woman in a shop.

"Three: it does not answer the question I came with, and it is not an answer to Mr. Rook's question either, and the only reason to say a thing out loud is that it is going to be used, and this one is not going to be used."

He put the pen down.

"It goes in the notebook. **It does not go on a wall and it does not go in a file, and I want the reason for each of those three next to it, because in about a year somebody is going to read this notebook and ask why the best sentence anybody has said to me in a fortnight is not in the record, and the answer is going to be one line and I would like it to be the right one.** It is true, and there is no document of it, and those two facts are the whole of the reason."

---

Elias Rook said nothing through any of that.

He had a folder in the satchel with a plastic sleeve in it and a copy of a revision letter in the sleeve, and the whole of what he had come here for on a Sunday morning was in that folder, and it was four sheets long, and it was the only document in ten weeks that had been produced by a telephone call to a Deputy Director, and it would have taken about ninety seconds to put it on the counter and about four seconds for a man of seventy-one to look at a form number and a date and work out the rest of his life.

He did not take it out, and it stayed in the satchel, and he has not regretted that, and he is not going to pretend to anybody that it was easy, because his hand went to the strap twice.

Duane Prewitt looked at the satchel, and at him, and did not say anything about either.

"You have been here twice and you have not bought anything either time," he said.

"No."

"Then what is in the bag."

"A piece of paper," Elias Rook said.

"Then leave it in the bag," Duane Prewitt said. "You have been carrying it since the Tuesday and you have not opened it and I have watched you not open it, and I would tell you to leave it there if you were a stranger and I am telling you anyway."

---

They walked back to the bus and Joon Park said, on the pavement, and it was not an answer to a question:

"You were right not to open it and I want to say the whole of the reason out loud before I have thought about it too long and made it tidy.

"**There is nothing to do here. That is what a person is when the team has no standing over him.** It is not a refusal and it is not a favor. A refusal is a door. A favor is a debt. This is a man behind a counter who told the truth to two strangers and asked for nothing and gave them the best sentence in the fortnight and then told them to leave it in the bag.

"That is the third time in a fortnight, and you have had it twice, and you said on the bus coming back from another city that it is the easiest kind of person to be in a room with, and you were right then and you are right now.

"And here is what I have learned in a week, and it took a man in a shop.

"**Knowing what to do with a person is not the same as being able to use one.** Every question in this business has had an action on the end of it, and this one has not, and I have discovered that I do not actually know what to do with an answer that cannot be spent, and I have now had two of them in three days, and one of them is on a wall in chalk with a nine-year-old's name on it, and the other one is in a notebook with no name at all, and neither of them is going to help anybody, and both of them are true, and I have started to think that the two of those facts sitting next to each other in the same book might be the most honest page anybody in this business has ever written."

The man behind the wall was seventy-one days in on that Sunday, inside a tunnel that was sealed and classified in nine words, and nobody went near him and nobody called the handset line. Cliff Marner was forty-one days out and was in a chair by a window in a building on the other side of a river, and a man of fifty-eight had asked on Saturday for somebody to write *home* on a card and put the time on it, and that was all he had asked for and he had not asked for it twice.
