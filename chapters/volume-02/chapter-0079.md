# Chapter 79 — What You Do With It

He had no slot at the depot that Friday. He had been four hours late to the one before it, the foreman had told him a slot would not be held twice, and Elias had said out loud that he would not be the man who argued with that, and so he was not there.

He walked over at ten past six in the morning on his own time with the week's inventory sheet in a folder, because the inventory half of the job is his and a hose is not, and R. Dunleavy took the sheet at the bay door, read it standing up, wrote two figures on it in his own hand, gave it back, and said nothing else at all.

He came up the ramp at Ash Street at half past four and there were twenty-six people in the front room at seven, which was more than anybody had expected for a Friday, and the reason was a single line Inez Sato had written on the pad: *the thing we found, seven o'clock, everybody who wants to be angry about it*.

Nobody wrote the year on the pad. Nobody has written the year in that building in eleven days.

---

Joon Park laid out the options, four of them, in the order he had thought of them, and he did it standing up with his notebook open and he refused to give anybody an opinion.

"**One. We record it.** We write down what was on the page, in a hand, in a book, and we put the book on a shelf in this building. And then in three years somebody comes in here with an order, served on a residents' body that cannot produce a file, and this time there is no wall to put it on, because a wall is a wall and a book on a shelf in a corridor is not a wall, and an order does not care what a book is."

"That loses it," Nadi Bell said.

"Yes."

"**Two. We publish it.**" He looked at Inez Sato and did not finish the sentence.

"No," Inez Sato said.

"I have to say the end of it out loud, because it is the only option any of us wants and it is the one that is not available. We publish it, and it is a description of a page, and the page has a name on it, and a description of a page that carries a name is a document about her, and in three days a Deputy Director's clerk has got a document about a school caretaker in Cordell and there is no line on it for her to refuse on. **That is the whole reason and you all know it and nobody has to say the rest of it.**"

"Somebody say the rest of it."

"Nobody in this room is going to say the rest of it and I am not going to help you," Inez said.

"**Three. We hand it to the Office.**" Joon turned a page. "We put the date and the block in front of a Deputy Director, which we have done, and he has told us what he is going to do with it, and he was straight with us, and what he is going to do is put it in a report as a fact with a date on it. That is a good outcome. It is a fair outcome. **And it becomes a fact in a report with a case number on it, and the case number is about us, and from Friday afternoon a document four people read in another city is a document that exists inside an inquiry about this street, and there is nothing anybody in this room can do about that ever again.**"

"**Four,**" Elias Rook said, "is give it away."

---

Nobody had thought of four. Elias had thought of it at ten past six that morning while a man wrote a figure on a sheet and said nothing, and the reason he had thought of it was a tin on a table and a woman with a rubber band round it twice.

"Give it where."

"To the local-history room. The one with the box and the card that says the papers are not be removed without the written consent of the people who wrote them. They have already got a copy of our copy, and a catalogue card with our name on it and a condition on it. **We do not give them a document. We give them a description of a page, in our hand, with no name on it, and they put it in the box and they put a card on the shelf, and that is the last anybody ever does with it.**"

"And then what," Ru Sabar said.

"And then it is not here."

Ru Sabar put her card down on the table. Her card had been back on the board since ten past six, and the laundrette had been running since a quarter past four, and she had not been in the room for a decision before tonight in her life.

"I want to say the thing I am not saying," she said. "It is not mine after six. Everybody knows that and nobody has ever had to be told, and I am not saying it because of a document. I am saying that I have watched four of you work for nine weeks on the assumption that there is a place in this building where a thing can be safe, and **I have kept that laundrette for six weeks on a condition, and a condition is not a safe place, a condition is a thing you can break, and I break it myself every night at ten past six and that is the only power I have got.** So I am not saying no. I am saying that somebody in this room ought to be honest that a loose thing in a county library is not safer than a loose thing on Ash Street. It is just further away, and further away is a different kind of cold."

"Then what is actually different about it."

In Inez Sato's cooperative language the answer came out in about nine seconds, and it is not in the Office's vocabulary at all, and it uses the Office's word.

"The hall's money is a card," Inez said. "The residents' log, the lamp cards, the consent cards — a card is a thing a person holds and can put down. When we say a hall keeps something loose, we mean we have it, anybody may have it, and nobody can prove we have it. That is the whole of the word, and it is a bad word, and it is ours.

"And in a Deputy Director's vocabulary, in his own file, the same object is called **an uncontrolled document**, because it is a document that is not somewhere his office can reach, and that word is the correct one, and I would like it printed on our line on purpose, so that in five years anybody reading our page knows what kind of thing it is, and so that nobody comes in here in ten years and tells us that what we have is uncontrolled in the way that means *careless*."

"That is a good sentence," Tarin Voss said from the door, and Inez said she knew, and did not say thank you, and Tarin Voss has never in his life been thanked for a sentence in that building.

---

"Then it is done," Joon Park said. "I am going to write it into the schedule, and it has never had a line like it, and I do not know what the line is going to say."

He put the custody schedule on the table. It is nineteen items long. Every one names a building and a holder, and it says *not in this building* four times, and it is the only document anybody in that room will still be able to use in five years.

He got a pen out, and then stopped, and put it down again, and put his left hand flat on the page to hold it open against the table, and turned the schedule so it faced Elias Rook.

"Write this one," he said. "I have got my hand on the book and I cannot write and hold it at the same time. You have got one hand and I have got two, and I am not being romantic about this. It is logistics."

"That is a true thing to say out loud and I am going to write it down," Elias said, "in a book that is not that book, later, when I am not in a room with twenty-six people in it."

He wrote it with his right hand, on his knee, at the bottom of nineteen lines, and it took about ninety seconds, and he read it back before he turned the book round.

> *20. One item. No copy, and none obtainable. Two pages, hand of J. Park, describing a page in a paper working log held in a regional store, a field removed from it, and a review annotation of a later date signed by a unit. No name of any person appears. **Not a record.** Location: local-history room, county seat, in the box, on the shelf, under the written consent of the people who wrote it. Address: none. **Given, not lodged. Not to be recalled. Not to be produced on request by anybody, including us.** Holder: not held. Envelope number 413, entered in that room's own catalogue by somebody who was asked.*

"That is the first line in nineteen with a location and no address," Joon Park said, "and I have written *holder: not held*, which is a thing I have never written in a column with a heading on it, and in five years somebody is going to read that and think it is a mistake, and it is going to be the only true thing on the page, and I am not going to be here to explain it."

"You are going to be here," Elias Rook said.

"I am going to be here," Joon Park agreed, "and I am not going to explain it."

---

He understood at about ten past eight that he had done the most useful thing he had done in nine weeks, and that the useful thing had been writing down somebody else's line, and that nobody in that room was going to make a speech about it, and that a man who had spent six weeks being the one who holds a tin, and who had refused a bus fare on Friday morning because he was the man who holds the tin, had just been, for about ninety seconds, the man who writes.

He did not enjoy it and he was not proud of it, and he wrote the date under it in the notebook at one in the morning, with four of the dark hours gone and Petar Lisk's lamp somewhere ahead of him in the corridor.

---

At half past ten on Friday night the telephone on the shelf that only receives rang, and it was Cordell, and it was Della Marner, and she had rung twice in nine weeks and both times about her husband.

"Did you find something," she said, before he had said a word.

Elias Rook stood in a corridor with a receiver in his right hand and worked for about four seconds.

"We found a page," he said. "It is a good page. It is the best thing anybody has found in nine weeks. It is also a thing I am not allowed to use, and I am not going to use it, and there is a woman in it and that is why, and I am not going to tell you her name and I would tell you her name if you asked me in about a year and she had agreed to it and I doubt she ever will."

"Was it about him."

"No."

"Then I have no opinion about it," Della Marner said, "and I want you to understand that as a woman who has been in a chair in a building for sixty-two days waiting for a piece of paper, and not as a woman who does not care. **If it is not about him then it is a page about somebody else, and somebody else has already had three institutions at her, and I am not going to spend the one thing I have got on it.** I have got a piece of paper. My husband is in a hospital bed four minutes from this telephone and he is going to come home on Tuesday and he has got a name on a wall in your city that you spelled the way he spells it, and that is the whole of what I have been asking anybody for since the sixth of September."

"Yes," Elias said.

"You are not going to tell me his name is not in a system."

"I am not going to tell you anything of the kind."

"Right," said Della Marner. "Then I am going to go to bed, and it is Friday, and you are a man in a corridor, and I have thought about it for two seconds and I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to bed. **Whatever it is, do not put her in it.** She is somebody's mother and I have been somebody's wife for thirty-one years, and I would rather you left the page alone than made it into a thing that turned a stranger's name into a sentence."

The line went down, and he stood in the corridor for a while with the receiver still in his hand.

Then he wrote it in the notebook, in his right hand, in the words she had said it in, and the page has a date on it, and eight pages further back there is a page with a year on it that nobody in that building can say out loud, and both of those pages are in the same book and only one of them is a thing anybody can use.
