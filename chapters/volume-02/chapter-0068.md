# Chapter 68 — Monday, and the Nineteen Years

The center is fully open on a Monday, and on a Monday the copy supervisor comes in at eight, and her name is Constance Ilori and she is fifty-seven and she has been doing it for nineteen years.

Nineteen years. Rufus Aitken had said nineteen years on a counter, and Bettina Hoyle had six, and the woman who could waive nothing had nineteen, and Elias Rook worked out somewhere in a car park on the way home that this building ran entirely on people who had been counting.

She read what they wanted in about four minutes and told them the three things that stood in the way, in the order they stood in the way.

"One. **The movement book does not leave this building.** Not a page and not a facsimile and not a scan with a certificate on it, and the reason is not that I do not like you. It is that the book is the record of a movement and a movement book that has been in two buildings stops being evidence of a movement and starts being a thing somebody has. You can have photographs. I will initial every frame with the page and the date and you will initial it too, and one of you stays in the room.

"Two. **The renumbering form does not get photographed.** That is not a rule about you either, it is that the sheet is a living form, it is still in use, and a photograph of a living form is how you end up with four versions of a form in four buildings and a clerk in nineteen fifty-nine being asked which one is the real one. What you may have is a **certified copy**, made here, on paper with a certificate on it, signed by a clerk and stamped, and that copy may leave, and the original does not.

"Three. **A copy fee of thirty-one dollars**, which is not mine, it is a schedule, and there is no hardship rate, and I have looked, because you did not ask and I looked anyway."

---

The other thing they needed was their own page.

On Sunday afternoon, in a store room, with a pencil, because Aitken had said pencil and not pen, Elias Rook had copied out the printed headings of the 2035 renumbering form and the eleven field labels and the exact wording printed over the second box, and the date and the line out of the movement book, onto a sheet of the hall's own paper, because that is the only copying anybody in that building allows.

That page was now the most useful object they had, and it could not come with them.

"The rule is that a transcription is a derivative copy," Constance Ilori said, "and a derivative copy of a store sheet is registered here and compared against the original and kept here, and it does not travel, because the whole value of it is that somebody in this building has laid it beside the thing and said *yes, that is what it says.* The second it walks out of here it is just a page with our words on it, and in two years there will be a dispute about what the form said and both sides will produce a page, and one of the pages will be ours."

"So it stays," Elias said.

"It stays," Ilori said. "And I want it understood that it is not a favor and it is not a judgment. It is the same for a schoolgirl with a pencil. I have been doing this for nineteen years and I have never let one out, and I have had one heckled about it by a man from a shipping office in nineteen ninety-six and I was right then as well."

He said the sentence that costs a man a thing.

"What do I have to sign."

"The register," Joon said. "The lodge entry, the date, the description, and the fact that the original is not traveling. And it goes on my schedule, because that is where the things go that are in a building I cannot enter."

Joon Park wrote it out on the counter in a copy room, and signed it, and Elias Rook watched him do it and did not offer to sign anything, because the offer would have been a kindness and the wrong kindness.

Elias said the cost out loud, once, so that it would be in the room.

"Two days ago the only copy of that wording in anybody's handwriting was hers, in two thousand and thirty-five. Now it is hers in two thousand and thirty-five, and a clerk's in nineteen eighty-odd on a form, and ours on a page that is going to sit on a shelf in this building for the next working day. If somebody wants to know what the box said, the answer is in three buildings and none of them is a building you can walk into tonight. I would like that written down as the price of the certified copy and not as anything else."

"Write it as the price," Ilori said, "and do not be dramatic about it in my copy room."

---

Nobody in that hall had thirty-one dollars and nobody was going to pretend otherwise.

Joon Park did not ask. He said so in the corridor afterward, in the voice of a man putting a thing down: *I am not going to ask a stranger in another city to spend her own money on my behalf and I would like it noted that I thought about it for eleven minutes and decided no.*

Elias Rook asked.

He asked once, of a woman he had spoken to for nine minutes, in a copy room, at ten past ten on Monday morning, and it was the first time in five weeks he had asked anybody for anything, and it was not a promise and it was not a plan.

"I am not asking you to bend anything. I am asking whether the fee can go, and if the answer is that it cannot, then I am going to go to the bus stop with thirty-one dollars I do not have and we will do this in November like everybody else."

Constance Ilori looked at him for a while.

"No," she said. Then: "I cannot waive the fee. I have not got that and I would not know where to enter it.

"What I have got is a *store attendance*, which is free, and what the schedule says is that a copy made during a store attendance, from the sheet, by a clerk, is certified by the store and not by the center, and it costs two dollars forty, and there is a box on the form that says who the clerk was, and I am the clerk."

She filled it in, and signed it, and stamped it with a stamp that had a wobble in the M.

"That is a thing I have never done in nineteen years," she said, "and I have thought about it for about ninety seconds and I am going to do it anyway, and I would like two things.

"One. Do not thank me, because thank you is for people who have done something for you, and I have not done anything for you. I have done it for the form. If it helps anybody then it helps the form.

"Two. **Send me the copy number when you get it.** Not to me. To the store's day book, on the shelf, on paper, in an envelope, addressed to *store attendance, shelf 3*, and put the copy number on it. There is a shelf in this building where the day books go, and there is a card index at the end, and in nineteen years that card index has four hundred and eleven entries and every one of them was made by somebody who was not asked to make it, and I would like there to be one that was."

---

There was one other thing they asked for, and she said no to it, and Elias Rook took the no.

"A facsimile release of the fourth of March page, nineteen ninety-eight, printed here, on this paper, stamped and dated, and we will pay for it."

"No," Ilori said.

"That is the one that matters to us."

"I know it is, and I am not going to pretend I do not know it, and I am going to tell you the reason anyway because you have behaved well and I would rather you had the reason than the asking. **A facsimile release is a copy.** It has a certificate on it and a stamp and a signature and it is a copy that says it is a copy, and a copy of that page will be read by people who will not have the book in front of them, and they will read the date on it, and they will read it on its own, without the line above it and the line below it, and a date in a box with a certificate on it is a thing that gets put in a document. The photographs are worse, and I know the photographs are worse, and I allowed the photographs because a photograph is a picture of a page and a picture is obviously a picture. A facsimile is not obviously anything. That is what it is for."

"Then we take the photographs."

"You take the photographs and you put them somewhere nobody can serve an order on," Ilori said, "and if you are honest with me you are going to have to think about where that is before you think about how."

---

They were on the road at ten past two, and the tin was in the boot with two certificates in it, a certified count of nine, four frames of a movement book, and a store's certified copy of a form with a certificate signed by a woman who had never done it before, and the price paid was a sheet of the hall's own paper that was going to sit on a shelf in Cordell for one more working day and then go into a register and be there for nineteen years.

They came off the estuary at four, and Elias Rook telephoned the hall from the payphone in a station waiting room, and Mara Okafor answered out of the corridor, because the records room had a door and the door was shut, and Joon Park was standing beside her with his notebook open.

Tomas Vale was not on the line, because the rule is that the thing goes to the people who pay for it and the form was addressed to a man and not to a hall. So Elias Rook said, "There is a question on a form from the Office in a room in another building, and it has been on his desk since the day they put a name in an envelope, and nobody has asked him about it out loud in a room with witnesses, and I am asking now, and Joon is going to hear it."

"You have about forty seconds," Mara said.

It took about nineteen.

"**Yes**," Tomas Vale said. "I will be available to give evidence at any future inquiry. I will make myself available, I will come, and I will not send anybody. And I want it written down in the words I have just used and not in the words on the form, because the words on the form are *do you intend to be available*, which is a question about my intentions, and my intentions are not the point."

"That is a yes," Mara said, "and it is the first time in five weeks that you have said a thing out loud that you did not have to say, and I want you to know what I think about it and I am not going to say what I think about it, because it is not my business and I am not going to manage you from a corridor."

"I know," Tomas said.

"I have got one thing to say and it is the only thing and then I am going to put the handset down. **You believe the truth is better for you now.** That is the most dangerous belief a man can hold in an inquiry, and there is no form anywhere in this business with a box for it, and I am telling you that I noticed it and I am not going to tell you what to do about it, and I am putting the handset down now."

Joon Park read the entry back across a telephone line, in a station, on a Wednesday's worth of paper he did not have, in his own hand, twice, the first time as it was said and the second time with the time on it, and Elias Rook listened to a man in another city record a sentence that could not be taken back, and understood that he was going to hear that sentence read out in a room in October by somebody he had never met, in a voice he could not predict, and that this was the correct outcome and that it had cost a man in another city more than it had cost anybody in this one.

He did not mention the notebook, and neither did Joon, and neither of them said the word *notebook* on a telephone in a station.

---

They got in at a quarter past seven and Lena Mir was on the ramp with a lamp and the fourth carded draw signed in three hands, and the hall was dark from midnight to four, and somebody had put a new bulb in the corridor fitting and nobody knew who.

Elias Rook put the tin on the front-room table, and unbuckled the rubber band, and rolled the band up and put it in his pocket, because Ru Sabar had said a thing tied twice was a thing somebody had to think about twice before they opened it, and he had thought about it twice, and it had been true both times.
