# Chapter 93 — What Is In The Box

He was on the far bank at twenty-five past six on Friday morning, the twenty-first of November, the seventy-sixth day, with an inventory sheet in a folder under his right arm, because the Friday hose slot had gone back in a letter four lines long and the inventory half was his and had been since October.

R. Dunleavy took the sheet at the bay door, read it standing up, wrote two figures on it in his own hand, gave it back, and said nothing at all, which was the third Friday and had stopped being an event for either of them.

"Heading south," Elias Rook said, because he had started saying it, and because the man wrote down times and a heading was a fact.

"I did not ask."

"You did not ask and I said it."

The bridge was cold and the estuary was doing nothing in particular. On a chair by a window in a building on the other side, Cliff Marner was forty-six days out of the ground. In Cordell, forty minutes and an ordinary road from where Elias stood, Della Marner was seventy-six days at a family-reception desk and had told nobody there the date of anything. The man behind the wall was seventy-six days in, inside a tunnel that was sealed and classified in nine words, and the eleven meters was a length with a day on it and nothing else, and nobody had gone near it in seventy-six days.

Elias Rook got to Ash Street at a quarter past four and went straight up, which he had not done in about a fortnight, because there was nothing in the building he was needed for at a quarter past four and he had begun to understand that as a fact about himself rather than about the building.

---

They were in the records room because that is where things go. Joon Park had the custody schedule open and the tin beside it. Lena Mir was at the table with the round sheet for the week and a card reader she had not brought out. Nadi Bell was in the doorway, which is where Nadi Bell is when a conversation is going to be about how a conversation is going.

"He is going to do it," Elias Rook said.

Nobody asked which him. He had said the name and everybody had let it go by, which was a courtesy he had not expected and did not deserve.

"**I am not going to ask you to tell me what is in it,"** Joon Park said.

"You have a rule for everything."

"I have four questions I ask a document. What is it, where is it, who holds it, and who put this name on it. **Every document since the sixth of September has gone through those four in a corridor where the room could hear me, and every one of them has an answer, and two of them have had an answer that made me stop.**" He turned a page. "This one has not got one. A box in a hallway under a chair is not a document. It has no location anybody can look up, it has no holder, and the man who packed it is the man it is about, and I cannot ask him who put the name on it without asking him the whole thing, and I am not going to."

"So what is your procedure."

"**The procedure is to leave it alone.**" Joon Park said it without any weight at all, which was how he said the things he meant most. "That is a real procedure. It is the only one I have got and it is the one I have used since the third week of September, and I would like it minuted that the reason it is my only one is that I have never had to write down what to do about a thing a person has chosen to carry."

"Then you have not got a procedure and you have got a habit."

"I have got a habit that has held for eleven weeks," Joon Park said, "and I would like somebody to explain to me the difference, because I have not been able to."

---

Lena Mir had not said anything for about four minutes, which was longer than her and about nine minutes shorter than Tarin Voss, who was not there.

"You have both got it wrong and it is worth saying so before Saturday," she said. "You keep talking about that box as though the interesting thing is what is in it."

"What is the interesting thing."

"**The interesting thing is that he has had it since the third week of September and has not read it out.**" She put the round sheet down. "You have both been sitting on your own rules about him for eleven weeks. His rule is one sentence and it is older than yours and he has never broken it: *a held plan is one I have not told the room.* He is not hiding a confession. A man who has got a confession in a box under a chair takes it out within a week, because a man who believes the truth is better for him cannot sit on a truth. He is sixty-four and he has told a Deputy Director to his face that he believes the truth is better for him, and a Deputy Director has already told him what that belief is worth in an inquiry. **So whatever that is, it is not the thing he would use against us. It is the thing he would use against himself, and he is not going to use it either.**"

Nadi Bell said, from the doorway: "That is a very good sentence and it is not a fact, Mrs. Mir, and you have just used about four of them in a row."

"I have. I am a nurse and I have been wrong in rooms for twenty-six years and I would like it minuted that I know the shape of the thing I have just done."

"It is not minuted," Joon Park said. "It is refused. **I do not have a procedure for a nurse being right.**"

---

Elias Rook took the whole of it out to the corridor at about half past five and stood at the rail with his right hand on it, and worked out what he actually thought, which was the thing he had come up the ramp to find out and had not found.

Tomas Vale was in the dry end at that moment, out of sight, doing whatever it is a man of sixty-four does in a dry end of a building in November for four hours a day so that he is not sitting down.

**He could go in there in ninety seconds.** That was the whole of it. It was the only fact about the last eleven weeks that had not moved, and it had not moved because nobody had pushed on it, and the reason nobody had pushed on it was not the wall Inez Sato had put up on Thursday about a form, and it was not Joon Park's four questions, and it was not the rule about disclosures. It was that Mr. Vale had told them, in a records room on the seventeenth, standing up, in eleven sentences, and had been thanked for it and told not to thank him, and every one of those eleven sentences had been about not saying a thing in a room.

If Elias Rook walked into the dry end now and said *I know you have had a box since September and I am not asking what is in it and I am not here about the inquiry*, then a man who has spent thirty-one years being the one who stops other people would have to decide, in about two seconds, whether Elias Rook was managing him. And he would get it right, and he would decide, and **Elias would never in his life know which of the two things he had done.**

He had been on the wrong side of a wall four times in a month. Three of them were a woman's decision and had a date and a reason and a way of being appealed to on paper. **This one was a man's pride, and there was no form on which to appeal, and there was no version of this week in which asking him was the kind thing and also the right thing.**

So he did not ask him, and it did not feel like restraint. It felt like the fourth wall, and the fourth wall had no door in it either.

---

"What are we going to do if he does not bring it out."

That was Nadi Bell, from the doorway, and she had been standing there long enough to have heard the last part of it.

"**The release goes forward without it.**" Elias Rook said it out loud because saying it out loud was the only way to find out whether he believed it, and he found out. "We do not wait. We do not go and ask. We do not build a schedule around a day when a man of sixty-four gets round to it, because that is a schedule with a man in it and we have four of those already and every one of them has cost somebody.

"We put what we have in front of a room on Wednesday morning. The four lines about a form. The four fields. The blank one. The two words. The two thousand and three revision and the fact that it has no name on it. The two thousand and eleven annotation with a unit number and no person. And if he has eight pages of his own, and if he chooses to put them on that table with the rest of it, then they go on the table and nobody in this building reads them first.

"And if he does not, then on Wednesday morning there is a release and there is no eight pages, and we do not say a word about it, and that is the end of it and it is the correct outcome and it is not a defeat."

Nobody improved on that either.

"You have just decided something about a man's document without the man," Nadi Bell said.

"I have just decided something about **Wednesday**, and Mr. Vale can do whatever he likes on Wednesday, and one of the things he can do is nothing." Elias Rook turned round. "**That is the first decision I have made in a fortnight that I would be willing to have read out, and I have made it about a day and not about him, and I would like somebody to notice that and not say anything about it.**"

Nadi Bell said nothing about it, which is how he knew.

---

He went down at ten past eight and Inez Sato was in the corridor by the card rail, doing the thing she does with the rail on a Friday, and he asked her, and she let him ask, and he said it in one sentence because she had taught him in October that one sentence is the whole of it.

"I need a room on Monday morning with three people in it and nothing on the pad and no form and no vote, and I am not going to tell you what for, and I would like it to be a room with a door that shuts."

Inez Sato looked at him for about four seconds, which was longer than she usually looked at anybody, and then she went on with the rail.

"**The dry end is not a room you can shut a door on and it is the only one with a table in it that is not the front room.** The records room shuts and it has the schedule in it and I would rather the man who writes those down was not in the room. Take the front room at nine and put the chairs out of it and leave the board alone, and the pad stays clean, and I will not ask, and if anybody asks me afterwards I will say I did not know and it will be true because I will not have been told."

"Nine on Monday," Elias Rook said.

"Nine on Monday," Inez Sato said, to the rail. "**And Mr. Rook, you have been on the wrong side of that man's wall since the third week of September and you have just spent an hour deciding not to knock on it, and I want you to know that the second one took courage and the first one took a wall, and you are going to be asked in about four years which of those two things you were doing, and I would like it on a wall that is not this one.**"

"I do not have anything to write on it."

"**Then you have somebody else's problem, and it is the only problem in this building I have ever been glad to hand on.**"

He came in at half past ten and Petar Lisk read the board at seven the next morning, out loud, twice, and there were three questions on it and one date and room for about two more lines, and the second half of the oldest question was still a bracket with nothing under it, and nobody took any of them down.
