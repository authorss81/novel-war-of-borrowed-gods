# Chapter 84 — The Reporting Word

The Office answers a question with a person, and on Wednesday morning the twelfth of November, the sixty-seventh day, the person who answered it was Counsel Renata Solano, forty-seven, and she said so in the first minute, in a ground-floor room on the Meridian with four chairs in it and a window onto a car park, because she had been in this building for twenty-six years and she had an office two floors up and had chosen not to use it.

"You have written to the Deputy Director," she said, "and you have asked a question about a word, and Mr. Rook has been suspended from seam assignment and Mr. Park is a dispatcher and neither of them is going to get a straight answer out of that office on a question about a word, because that office does not write the words. I write the words. I am the person in this building whose job is what a sentence in a report has to be able to mean in four years, and I have been doing it since before your Mr. Rook was in the service, and I have twenty minutes, and I am not going to waste them, so ask."

"Twenty minutes," Joon Park said, and wrote the time down.

---

The question he asked was one sentence and it had been in four drafts on the hall's own paper since Sunday, and it did not contain the word *who* and it did not contain the word *why*, and both of those had been removed from it on Sunday night by a nine-year-old's logic, which is that a question with a *why* in it is a question about a person and this one was not about a person.

"The Office's covering letter of the thirty-sixth day reports a discrepancy between two records," Joon Park said, "between a service roster and an equipment list for the same morning. It reports it in two words. I am not going to say them out loud in this room because they are in your letter and I do not repeat an institution's words back to it.

"What I want to know is what the last word of those two is *for*. Not what it means. What it is for."

Solano looked at him for about four seconds.

"You have written a question about a word," she said, "and you have written it well, and I am going to answer it and I am going to tell you the whole of the answer and not the useful half, and then I am going to stop, and you are not getting a second answer today and I am not being cold, I have four hundred pages to write before the nineteenth."

"Go on."

"It is a **reporting** word." She turned her chair about ten degrees. "Understand what that means inside an institution. Every word in a report is either a reporting word or a finding word, and they are not the same and they cannot be swapped, and a report that uses them wrongly is not a badly written report, it is an unusable one.

"A finding word decides something. *Violation. Failure. Cause. Misconduct.* If you put a finding word in, somebody has to be the author of it, and if the author of it is a person, then that person can be spoken to. That is the whole difference between a report and a proceeding, and there are a great many people in this building who make that difference on purpose.

"A reporting word does not decide anything. **It reports that a thing happened which is a thing that happened, and that it is going to be put right in the ordinary course, and it does not say who did it, because an error does not have an author.** Not because the Office is hiding one. Because an error is *a thing that happened*. A car runs over a cat. That is an error. There is no man in the sentence. If you want a man in the sentence you are not writing about a car and a cat, you are writing about conduct, and then you are in a proceeding, and then there is a finding, and a finding needs an author, and an author has to be somebody who can be asked what they meant."

She stopped, and looked at the ceiling for a second, and then went on.

"That is why the last word of those four is the one it is. **A thing which is a thing that happened and gets put right in the ordinary course is exactly what a person is not.** The word is not a euphemism. It is a grammar. It puts the event in the subject position and leaves the space where the person would go empty, and the space is empty on purpose, and it was emptied by somebody in a forms office who was doing a job, and the job was to give a clerk in a district office a way to close a discrepancy between two pieces of paper without the clerk having to have an opinion about anybody."

Elias Rook said, "The form is not lying about the error."

"The form is not lying about the error," Counsel Solano said. "**That is the part I want you to hold on to.** In our case the discrepancy is real. The service roster says a man clocked on at four minutes to six in the morning on the sixth of September. The equipment list for that crew does not have him on it. That is not a filing quirk. That is a real difference between two true documents, and it happened, and it is not anybody's invention, and reporting it as an error is accurate. Reporting it as a *cause* would have been a lie. Reporting it as *misconduct* would have been a lie and a libel.

"**The form is not lying.** It cannot lie. It can only carry what is printed on it, and that is what makes it useless to you and useful to me, and I have watched four good men get that wrong in the same week and one of them was me."

---

There was a silence of about four seconds, and then Counsel Solano said, and she said it in a different voice, not a lower one, a flatter one, and she said it looking at the window.

"I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to stop, and I would like it understood that I have thought about whether to say it and I have decided to say it, and that it is not a kindness and it is not a courtesy and it is not me being fair to you.

"**There is nobody in this building you can be angry at, and I would like you to stop looking for one on my account, because you are going to find that searching is now the most expensive thing you do.**

"I have written findings about people for sixteen years. Some of those people were negligent and some of them were frightened and a few of them were cruel, and I could tell you in the first ten minutes of any morning what the difference looks like on a page. In the last four years I have written about a box, a field, a unit, a form and a word, and I could not tell you in ten minutes or ten years which of those five a person is, because not one of them is one. **The absence of a villain is not a mercy and it is not a reprieve. It is the finding, and it is the finding I am going to have to write, and if your Mr. Rook wants to know what it costs to be unable to blame anybody, he should go and stand in a corridor at Ash Street for an afternoon, because the people in that corridor are the ones it is costing.**"

Then she said, "Your nineteen minutes are up," and stood up.

---

Joon Park wrote all of it down. He read it back to her, all of it, and she stopped him at the fourth sentence of the second part and said "skip that, it is the same sentence twice," and he skipped it, and then he asked the question he had not asked yet, which he had been carrying since a Tuesday in October in another city, and which was the one the whole of this business had been walking in a circle for ten weeks.

"Then what does the system gain."

Solano did not pretend not to understand.

"That is a different question," she said. "**And it is not mine.** I write the words. I know what each of them can be made to carry and I know what happens to a report that uses one where the other belongs. What the network gains by having fewer contradictions in it is an arithmetic question and it belongs to somebody who does arithmetic on a network, and in this building that is a man in a basement with a title I could not tell you and a budget, and I have never been in his corridor and he has never been in mine, and I am not going to guess at his answer in a room with a recorder that is not running but might be.

"**If you want the answer to that, you are asking the wrong office, and I have been saying the correct sentence for sixteen years and it never once gets me out of the conversation.**"

---

They walked out into a car park at ten past eleven and the sun came out about as far as it comes in this city in November, which is not far, and Elias Rook stood on the curb and understood something that he had been carrying since the sixth of September and had not been able to put down.

He had wanted a motive.

Nine weeks. A corridor, a board at twenty past eight, a blade, a rectangle of paper two inches by one, a woman of sixty-eight with a mop, a woman of sixty-two at a desk, a man behind a wall, nine credentials with no names, a box on a cart, a form with a printed box, four lines in a unit's block, and a Deputy Director with a real case and a report to write in October.

He had wanted somebody to have wanted it. Somebody competent who had sat in a room and read a form and understood and typed a line into it anyway, and done it on purpose, for a reason that was theirs, in a year he could say out loud.

He was not going to get that. He had never been going to get that, and Rusk had told him so on a telephone in October and Aitken had told him so in a store room in November and Lena Mir had told him so on a wall in chalk, and every one of them had been telling him the same thing in the same room and he had been waiting for a different answer with the same patience he brought to everything.

What he was going to get instead was a shape.

And a shape was harder to argue with than a motive, because a motive can be resisted — a motive can be called a motive by a person who has one, and a person who has one can be shown to be afraid, or greedy, or obedient, or any of the four, and then the sentence about what they did goes into a file with the adjective in front of it and it looks like work. A shape has no adjective. **A shape simply does not have anybody in it, and there is no adjective in the world for a form that was designed well and used correctly by a person who was in a hurry on a Tuesday in a building on the Meridian.**

He could not call Rusk afraid or obedient. He could not call the Office greedy. He could not even call the thing a design, because design implies somebody sat down and chose it, and this had been chosen by about nine people over thirty-nine years in a row, each of whom added a box for a case that had actually happened, and not one of them ever sat down and decided what the boxes added up to.

"There is nobody in it," he said, out loud, to Joon Park, on a curb in a car park.

"There is nobody in it," Joon Park agreed, "and I have now written down four separate things that say so in four different hands, in four different rooms, in three different cities, and I am not going to write the fifth one down today, because a man who keeps writing the same sentence in more places is not being thorough, he is looking for a version of it that somebody will accept."
