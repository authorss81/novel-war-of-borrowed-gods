# Chapter 90 — An Answer With No Author

Lena Mir was on the round at five on Tuesday evening, the eighteenth of November, the seventy-third day, and there were four carded draws signed in four hands, one of them Ru Sabar's with the time on it, and she was reading the sheet as she walked, which is a thing she does and is not a comment on anybody.

The four dark hours were five hours away. Ansel Duarte was on his frame in the corridor at seven in the evening, awake, which meant he had been awake at three, and nobody was going to say so.

Cliff Marner was in the chair by the window, forty-three days out of the ground, going to a clinic in a week and not knowing it, because the driver was Ru Sabar and the swap was a night and the date had not been said in the room he was sitting in.

In Cordell, forty minutes away, Della Marner had been at a family-reception desk since the Saturday this all started. It was seventy-three days. She had a piece of paper in a plastic wallet with her husband's name on it in two hands, one of them a stranger's, and she had told nobody there the date of anything.

The man behind the wall was seventy-three days in, inside a tunnel that was sealed and classified in nine words, and nobody was going near him and nobody was going to call the line, and the eleven meters was a length with a day on it and nothing else.

---

There was no meeting.

That had been decided on Thursday, and Emil Baros checked it himself at about half past five, standing in front of the pad with his hands behind his back, and the pad had nothing on it, and he said "good" out loud, alone, to nobody.

"There is going to be a discussion at seven," Inez Sato said, "and it is not a meeting, and there is no form, and there is nothing to sign. I am saying all four in this order because two people in this building would otherwise do something admirable and ruin it."

It was in the front room with fourteen people in it and lasted fifty minutes, and it was about one thing: **an answer with no author in it cannot be given to anybody, because there is nobody to give it to.**


---

"It has to go somewhere," Lena Mir said. "Everything gets put somewhere. The form goes in the tin, the page went to the county library. **You cannot have a true thing in this building that is not in a book, a wall, a file or a hand, because in six weeks somebody will come in here and not remember it, and that is how things get lost that were not lost.**"

"So where does it go."

Nobody answered for about a minute, and the answer came from a man with a pencil at the end of the table with the hall's copy of the clerk's fourth column in front of him and his own beside it, doing what he does every Tuesday.

"No column," Emil Baros said.

"Emil."

"**No column, and I am the man who has to say it, because that column is the only thing on that wall with a heading on it and I have put my name to six weeks of keeping it, and if this goes in a column then it is a receipt, and a receipt says a thing came into this building on a date, and it did not.**

"It came out of a man's mouth on Friday evening and no paper crossed a threshold, and there is no document in the world that says any of it, and I have spent twenty minutes trying to find the line it goes on and there is not one, and I am not going to invent a column at half past seven on a Tuesday because the man with the pencil has run out of lines."

He turned the two sheets round so the room could see them side by side, put his own finger on the fourth column, and then on nothing at all, which was a piece of table about nine inches away.

"**Our copy says *items, 2*. Theirs says *item, 2*, one letter, no comma. That is the only difference in twenty-eight lines and I found it with a pencil and a Tuesday.** And the reason that letter matters is not the letter. It is that on the day somebody writes in a document, this hall has a copy of its own and the Office has one of its own, and the two are not the same object, and that holds up in a room.

"And that does not hold up for Friday. **Friday is not a document. Friday is four people in a room and a sentence and no paper, and there was never an original, so there is nothing in this hall that is a copy of.** So it does not go in a book with a date, because a book with a date is a copy, and a copy means somebody wrote it down and the somebody is missing. And it does not go on the wall, because a wall is a place people are, and this is not about anybody in this room now or in nine years."

"Then it is lost," Ru Sabar said. "**Lost is not a thing somebody hides. It is a thing that was never on anybody's list.**"

"No," Joon Park said. "**Every one of the twenty lines on that schedule is a thing somebody is holding, with a building and a holder and a sentence about who may ask and who may not, and that is not bureaucracy, it is a promise: somebody has it, and if you come and ask, a person has to answer you.** Friday is not that. **There is no holder and no building and there is not going to be a person in it who has to answer you, because there is not a person in it.**"

"Then it is not a record," Inez Sato said.

"Then it is not a record," Joon Park agreed, "and I am going to write it down as one and read it back and not improve it."

He wrote it in the notebook, and on a sheet, and read it back.

> **21. One item. No copy, none obtainable, and no original. A statement about the design of a network and about the word an institution uses to report a discrepancy between two records. It was said once, in a room, on the evening of Friday the fourteenth of November, and it is not attributed here, because the moment a true thing about a design has a speaker it stops being a fact about the design and becomes an opinion somebody holds. It is true. It is not evidence of anything anybody did. No holder, no location, no address; no column on the wall for it, no file in any building for it, and nobody to whom it can be given, because an answer with no author in it has nobody to give it to. Not to be recalled. Not to be produced on request by anybody, including us. Not a record.**

"That is the first line in twenty-one with no location and no holder on it," he said, "and on the twentieth I wrote *holder: not held* because somebody had put it down and I could not find who. On this one there was never anybody, and in four years somebody is going to read line twenty-one and think it is a mistake. It is the only true thing on the page."

---

They put it in the notebook, the notebook went in the tin, the band went round the tin twice, and the tin went on the shelf where it has been since October.

And then Lena Mir said the thing the room had been circling for fifty minutes, in the plainest words anybody had used on that street in ten weeks.

"Nobody decided anything," she said. "That is the finding. It took two days and a half and a form with a line on it and a man in a shop, and it is *nobody decided anything*.

"And the other half, said once before in this room and said again because we have a new fact and the old sentence has got bigger: **a design that is good does not need anybody to be a villain, and that is the same shape I have been pointing at since Tuesday, and so is Petar's, and so is the box somebody printed in about nineteen eighty while thinking hard about a field, and the line somebody added in two thousand and three because a night in January did not fit on the paper.**

"I am not going to be sentimental about that. I have spent ten weeks waiting for somebody to have done this to us and there is not going to be one, and I am not going to be sad about it in front of you, because that would be asking you to comfort me about a thing that has a number in it."

"Then what is left," Petar Lisk said.

---

"It is going on the wall," Lena Mir said, "because Mr. Lisk has decided it and he is nine and he gets to decide that, and I am glad, because I have watched two more lines go up this week and I am tired. There is room for about two after this one, and then somebody has to build another wall, and nobody in this building is going to be the one who decides that."

He had to write it small, because there was not much space left.

> **NOBODY OWNS THE ANSWER SO NOBODY CAN GIVE IT AWAY. PETAR L. — I AM NINE AND I HAVE READ IT SIX TIMES.**

He read it out loud twice, because that is what he does.

It contains no number about anybody. It does not answer any of the three questions, all three still on that wall in the same hand above it, and the second half of the oldest is still a bracket with nothing under it, and it will stay like that until somebody who is not any of us writes something under it.

Nobody asked who, and one person had already asked not to be asked.

Tomas Vale was not in the room. He had gone down the corridor at twenty past six on Friday and said he was not going to say it again, and on Saturday morning he sent a line up the stairs on a piece of hall paper: *do not put my name on it. A finding about how a machine works is not mine and it is not anybody in that room. If it has a name on it a man can be asked whether he believes it, and then it is an opinion, and I have spent thirty-one years telling people a corridor wants fewer decisions and I am not spending the last of them being quoted at.*

"That is his to refuse and he has refused it," Inez Sato said, "and it costs him nothing and it costs him everything, and both of those are true, and I am not going to discuss it."

Then Petar Lisk took the pencil to the bottom of the board, where the nineteenth of November was already written in his own hand with a dash and a P. L., stood there with it for about eleven seconds, and did not write anything.

"There is no date for tomorrow," he said. "**The nineteenth is already on there. A hall that puts two dates on a wall in one week is a hall that is starting to expect something, and then it is a hall that will be disappointed on Wednesday.** So there is one date, with a dash and my name on it, and tomorrow is not on there, and I have decided that on purpose and I would like somebody to write down that I decided it on purpose."

Somebody did.

---

Elias Rook came up the ramp at about twenty past ten and the building was doing its ordinary evening, and it was standing, and it had been standing for ten weeks without him, and he had two answers in ten weeks and could not spend either.

He noticed on the stair that he had stopped noticing the first of those two things as a loss, then noticed that he had noticed, and let it go, which he has now done twice.

What he had learned in four days, and told nobody, is that an answer with no author in it cannot be given away either: not to the county library, because it is nobody's, and not to a Deputy Director, because it will be accepted into a file and ruled on none of it.

**There is nowhere to put it. It is not a burden and not a shield. It is true, it fits in a coat, it weighs nothing, and everybody who hears it will carry it without knowing they are carrying it.** And it started on Saturday morning, when a woman walked into a room with twenty-three people in it and said eleven sentences and left. Four days later this building has a rule about it, and a rule is not the same as it not having happened.

There is a hall on Ash Street where people argued about a laundry every Tuesday for ten weeks and kept their own alive on a schedule, and in about nine days it is going to be described, correctly and without cruelty, in one sentence, as the hall that kept things other people had decided were finished. That is a true sentence.

And a man came up a corridor on a board on the thirtieth day at fifty-one pounds, and on Tuesday night he was in a chair by a window in that building, and he has not been told about the appointment in the front room, and neither of those two men is going to be in a system this week.

Petar Lisk read the board at seven in the morning, out loud, twice, the way he does. The three questions were still on it and the date was still on it, and there was room for about two more lines, and then somebody had to build another wall, and nobody in that building was going to be the one who decided it.
