# Chapter 80 — Saturday, and Three Questions

The hall had been running without him most weeks since the collapse and it said so, and it said it in the first thirty seconds, and it did not say it unkindly.

Elias Rook came up the ramp at ten past five on the Saturday, the sixty-third day, and four people were doing a job in the front room and two of them did not look up, and Petar Lisk, who is nine and who reads the board at seven every morning, walked past him with a hand lamp under his arm and said, without stopping, "You were in Cordell on Sunday and Monday. That is two days out of the last ten, and I counted, and I am not counting Saturday because Mr. Park went and you did not, and that is a different thing, and I have written the two things separately."

"Who counted?"

"I counted," Petar Lisk said, and went to do the corridor.

Lena Mir was on the round. There were four carded draws and they were signed in four different hands, and one of them was Ru Sabar's with her time on it, and the laundry was running, and Ansel Duarte was on his frame in the corridor with a book, awake, at five in the evening, which meant he had been awake at three, and nobody was going to say so.

"Stop standing there," Lena Mir said. "Sit down or go to another city."

"I have been to another city."

"Then sit down," she said, and then, about a minute later, without turning round: "Comfort came home on Friday and she is on the ward circuit from tonight and it is batteries and the clinic has charged us forty-one dollars for two batteries, and I paid it out of the medical tin, and nobody is to tell her I paid it out of the medical tin."

---

There was a meeting at seven because Inez Sato had written a line on the pad, and the line said *the thing we found, what we are doing with it, and the record, seven o'clock*.

Nineteen people came. Elias Rook stood at the back with his back to the wall, and the tin was on the table with the band round it twice, and the wall behind him had the clerk's fourth column, and nineteen hands' worth of sheets underneath it, and Nadi Bell's sentence about two different kinds of safe, and three lines of things with no answers on them, and Petar's line about a book in another city.

"The record first," Inez Sato said, "and then the thing nobody has ever done."

Joon Park put his hand on the notebook and did not open it.

"I want to say something and I want it said out loud and I want it to be cheap this time, and I am not going to know for about a week whether it is cheap. **I am not writing what anybody thinks it means.** That is the fifth time I have said that in nine weeks, and the second time it has cost me nothing, and the first time it cost me nothing I noticed it and I did not decide what to do about having noticed it, and I have now noticed twice and I am telling the room, because a refusal that has stopped costing anything is not a refusal any more, it is a habit, and a habit is the thing where a practice goes to die without anybody voting on it."

Nobody said anything, and he said the other half of it.

"The other half is that I do not want to stop. So I have written down that I want to stop, and the date, and that is on the page with my name on it and somebody else can look at it in a year and tell me I said so. That is the whole of it and it is nineteen seconds long and I would like it minuted."

"Minuted," Inez Sato said, and wrote it.

---

"**The 2011 annotation does not go in as a finding.**"

Elias Rook said it and Joon Park wrote it, and nobody argued, and the reason nobody argued is that they had all worked out during the week that a finding is a thing that decides something and there was nothing here that anybody could decide.

"So it goes in a book," Elias Rook said, "in a hand, with a date on it. Inez's book, not the file, and Joon's hand and not mine, and it is going to say what is on the page and not one word about what the page means, and the date on it is Monday the third of November, which is the day four people read it and the day we decided, and the two are the same day and I am putting both of them on it."

"Read it out," Inez Sato said.

Joon Park read it out. It is nineteen lines long and it is a description and not a conclusion, and the last line of it is *no name of any person appears on the page or in this account*, and when he had finished, Lena Mir said, "That is well done, Mr. Park," and he said thank you, and meant it, and she has never said that to anybody in that building before and she has not said it since.

---

"And now the part," Inez Sato said, "that I do not have anything for, so I am going to let the room have it."

It took about eleven minutes and Lena Mir said the sentence that everybody else had been circling for nine weeks, and she said it the way she says things, which is once.

"Two boxes," she said. "One printed in about nineteen eighty by somebody who thought very hard about a field, and filled in correctly in the spring of two thousand and thirty-five by a woman who told us that being competent at a form is a skill and not a virtue. And one printed in some year I do not know and filled in in two thousand and eleven by a unit, also correctly, in a box, about a field.

"And in between them there is a piece of paper two inches by one that somebody took out of a bound book with a blade, in a year I am not going to say in this room, on a night when a woman who had asked for none of it was doing the thing that made her a name in a form. **And there is not one villain in any of it.** Not one. Twenty-six years I have been a nurse and I have watched three people do damage they would not have called damage, and I have never once watched a person do it who thought of himself as the villain of the story, and the difference between those two sentences is the whole of what has happened to this street since the sixth of September, and it is not a comfort, and I am not going to pretend it is a comfort, and I would like somebody to write it on the wall."

"That is a form doing its job," Nadi Bell said. "Both of them. They are not covers and they are not lies and they are not anybody's fault. **A form doing its job is the only thing in this whole business that has never needed anybody to be a villain.**"

"Then write it on the wall," Inez Sato said, "and both of you, and nobody else, because I want two hands on it and I do not want a committee."

---

Then Petar Lisk read the board at seven, as he does every morning and has done every morning since the second week, and he read the two questions and the third thing he had written under them, and the newest thing under that, and then he said that the wall was full.

"There is room for about four more of these," he said, "and then somebody has to build another wall, and nobody in this building is going to be the one who decides that, so it is going to be a thing that happens to us, and I have decided that I am not going to be the one who decides it either."

He took the pencil off the string.

"Somebody asked me on Wednesday what the first question means," he said, "and I said I do not know, and then I said I know the *shape* of it. And Lena has got the shape. So I am going to write the shape and not the meaning, because that is what is on this wall, and if I write the meaning I will be wrong by Friday."

And he wrote it at the bottom, in his own capitals, and he had to write small, because there was not a great deal of space left.

> **NONE OF THE THREE THINGS ON THIS WALL NEEDED A PERSON TO DECIDE THEM. THAT IS THE SHAPE. I DO NOT KNOW THE MEANING. PETAR L. — I AM NINE AND I HAVE READ IT FOUR TIMES.**

Then he read it out loud, twice, to nineteen people, because that is what he does.

It contains no number about people. It does not answer any of the three questions. All three of them are still on that wall in the same hand, above it, and the second half of the oldest one is still a bracket with nothing under it.

---

And then he wrote one more thing, small, at the very bottom, in a hand nobody else in the building has, because Nadi Bell gave him the pencil and the paper and stepped back.

> **19 NOVEMBER. — P. L.**

"Inez Sato said on Wednesday that the Deputy Director has fixed the third sitting for the nineteenth and that it is the same day the answer to our request is due, and that he chose it on purpose, and that he said he chose it so that nobody afterwards could say it was a trick," Petar Lisk said. "I have written the date because we are waiting on the date, and a hall that names a date is not a hall that is looking for a thing. That is all that is. I do not know what is going to happen on the nineteenth and I am not going to guess on a wall."

Nobody asked him to take it down. Lena Mir read the round sheet twice and said it was right, and said it the way she says it.

---

Outside, a town that had lost a tunnel in September was doing its ordinary evening in a district where the lights went off at midnight, and Elias Rook had been to two cities in a fortnight and had come back with less than he took out on every single occasion, and the less was not nothing: it was two certificates, four frames, a count of nine sealed by a stranger, a form with a printed box, a movement book with a date nobody can explain, a rectangle of paper two inches by one, four lines in a unit's block in a different ink, a schedule with twenty items on it and no address on the twentieth, a tin with a rubber band round it twice, and the fact that a woman in a school had locked two vans.

The ward was standing. The round was on the nail. Four draws were signed in four hands. A nurse had got a woman home on a Friday and paid for two batteries out of a tin and told nobody. A function had a pencil and a Tuesday in it. A wall had two more hands on it and a date at the bottom of it and room for about four more.

Elias Rook had not built any of it and was not the reason for any part of it, and that had been true since the Wednesday of the fifty-third day, and about nine days ago he noticed that he no longer found it a loss, and then on Saturday morning he noticed that he had stopped noticing, and he understood that this was the last thing the week was going to pay him and that it was a very small thing and that he was going to be all right.

Then he went and read the board, because there were three questions on it and the second half of the oldest one was still a bracket with nothing under it, and somebody nine years old had written the shape of all three at the bottom of a wall in a hall where nobody could say a year out loud, and it was going to be read again at seven in the morning, by somebody, out loud, twice.
