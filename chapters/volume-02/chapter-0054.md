# Chapter 54 — The Inventory

They came at eight on Monday morning, the thirty-seventh day, with a cart.

That was the part Elias Rook had not been ready for. He had spent Friday and the weekend preparing for a person with a clipboard, and what came up the ramp at two minutes to eight was two clerks and a supervisor and a handcart with four flat shelves and a man behind them carrying a box of forms in a plastic tub.

Nobody knocked. Nobody asked whether anybody was working. The supervisor said, "Order of Impoundment, property of a residents' body, served on the thirty-fourth," and then said, "Are you the person who holds it," and Elias said that he was one of the people who held it and that the rest of it was in a garage on a street with a name, and the supervisor wrote *address provided* in a box and did not ask a follow-up question, because the form had no line for one.

It took ninety minutes.

The terminal went first, which meant the Civic Spine in the records room went dark at fourteen minutes past eight and the four-C line that had come back degraded on the Wednesday went with it, and nobody in that building said anything about that at the time and Joon Park wrote down the time and initialed it himself for once.

Then two anchor lamps. Then the corridor repeater, and the corridor light, and that was the moment Inez Sato put her hand flat on the table and said one word, and everybody heard it, and then she did not say anything else about it for two hours.

"Because that is the whole of the corridor," she said. "That is the corridor light, and it is a card, and the card is one of the four carded draws, and the draw is this hall from midnight to six." She turned the sheet around so the room could see the column. "So the dark hours stop being two and start being four. From midnight until four instead of two until four, and I have written it on the board, and it is not a complaint, it is a fact with a time on it, and I am going to go and be angry at it for about a quarter of an hour and then I am going to start asking two households whether they are willing to be asked."

She went out into the corridor and came back in and put two more names on the fourth column of her power sheet, and nobody followed her, because in four weeks this building has learned that Inez Sato's quarter of an hour is not available.

Ansel Duarte's frame is on the hall's own draw and the frame does not stop, and nobody said that sentence out loud either.

Then the access codes for the sealed node, which is a building the team cannot walk into, and the codes went on the cart, and the codes were the only thing in that whole ninety minutes that Elias could not replace in a week with a piece of paper and a person who knew how to use it.

---

The garage was four streets away and eleven boxes and a handcart got them into the back of an Office vehicle at twenty past ten in the morning.

Elias did not obstruct anything. He has thought about that for a long time afterward, and the thing he keeps coming back to is not that he obeyed and not that he fought, but that he wrote.

Joon Park built the inventory in the doorway of the garage in the wind, from memory, out loud, item by item, and the clerk wrote it down, and Joon read it back before the clerk wrote the last line. Two copies of every measurement, including the eleven meters and the day it changed and a man's own account of finding out with a cart. Every patient name in Lena Mir's handwriting. Every survey frame, including three that were never printed. Both of Tomas Vale's nine-line documents. The refusal log, complete. And a sheet with eleven words on it, a relay stamp seen twice, and a line saying the meaning is not written down.

"Read that last one back to me," the supervisor said.

"It is eleven words and a stamp and a line saying the meaning is not written down," Joon said. "I have read it back. I am not going to interpret it in a doorway."

"Noted," the supervisor said, in the voice of a person who writes noted, and it turned out later that *noted* and *read back and not interpreted* are two different things in a receipt and that the difference is worth four hours of somebody's life in an October, and that neither of them knew it at the time.

And then the supervisor asked the question, in the doorway, in the ordinary way.

"Is there another copy of this?"

There is a copy of this in another city.

Elias Rook said, "Yes."

That is the whole of what he said, and he said it in about a quarter of a second, and it is in no file in four buildings, and Petar Lisk was not there and there is no record anywhere on earth of a man in a doorway deciding anything.

Joon said, "The parallel record went out of Havenport on a bus on the Sunday morning of the twenty-ninth, by an ordinary road, and it is in a building that neither this office nor this hall can name, and the person carrying it is not in this city, and the burden of proving what is in it is now on that person and not on us. Write that on the receipt, please, with a receipt number and not a name, because I do not know the name and because a name is not required."

"It is not required," the supervisor agreed, and wrote *second copy, out of jurisdiction, holder unnamed* and a number in the top box, and the number is the only way anybody in this story will ever get back to that doorway.

And then the supervisor turned to the last line of the schedule of property, which is the line with the most words on it.

"The file of the residents' body."

"It exists," Elias said.

"Then I write *claimed* against it, and I hand you a second copy of the order, and I am going to tell you something you will not enjoy, because you have had a long morning." The supervisor did not look up. "I cannot take it off you. I have a cart and a schedule and I have taken the property on the schedule. That line is not a seizure. It is a demand, and a demand goes to whoever the residents say has the authority to answer it, in writing, and somebody has to write the answer down. There is no box on this for a refusal either. I have looked. I have been looking since Friday. I am telling you now so that you do not plan a speech."

"Who has the authority to answer it," Elias said.

"That is not my question," the supervisor said. "It is the one you are going to have to spend a day on. I am in Cordell on Wednesday and I would rather not hear afterwards that I buried the paragraph."

---

The last clerk ran out of forms at twenty past two in the afternoon.

She had used eleven, and the twelfth one was for the sealed node and the eleventh had been for the corridor repeater, and there was a column on the form headed for the property of a residents' body and about three inches left of it, and the items on it were in three columns and there was a fourth column on the table which was not a column at all.

It was a wall.

A wall with twenty-eight people in the building, a board on it, a sheet of paper with Lena Mir's round on it, a list of nineteen functions with a name against each one, four signed promises, a case number, two questions, and eleven names.

It was the only surface in that room with a column on it.

"Well," the clerk said, and stopped.

"Write it on the wall," the supervisor said, and then said, "No. Do not — that is not a form. That is not a form, and if it goes on a wall it is not on a form."

"I know it is not on a form," the clerk said. "That is the whole reason it is the only thing in the room I can write on."

She wrote it in chalk, in her own hand, which was a small neat upright print that nobody in that building had seen before and would know again for the rest of their lives, in a fourth column to the right of Petar Lisk's capitals, and she numbered the lines, and she went down the cart's list the way she had been going down it all day, in her own order.

Nadi Bell read it back to her.

She did it line by line, out loud, in the order the clerk had written it, and she did it the way she reads a movement record, and she did not stop her to hurry and she did not skip to the parts she had an opinion about. It took six minutes. Twenty-eight people heard it.

Line fourteen was wrong.

It read: *items, 2 — oxygen cylinder, residential.*

"That one is not two items," Lena Mir said.

"It is a cylinder," the clerk said. "It came off the medical inventory, it is a residential item, and there were two, one of them on the machine shop draw and one of them in the dry end —"

"The patient's name is written on the tape on the top of it," Lena Mir said. "It is in my hand. It says Sena Ambrose, and she is eleven, and she is in the bed at the end of the row, and a cylinder with a person's name on it is not two items, it is one person's oxygen."

The clerk stood with the chalk in her hand and did not write.

They said afterward that this was the four minutes that made the wall the thing it became, and nobody at the time knew it, and the reason it took four minutes is that the clerk asked Lena Mir to say the whole of the name and the whole of what was on the tape, and wrote it in full in the fourth column of a wall, and then struck the number two through with a single line and put the tape copy beside it, and then asked Lena Mir whether she wanted her name against the correction, and Lena Mir said yes, and wrote it herself in her own hand, and it is the only place in the history of the Cinder Quarter Electrical Cooperative where a correction by an Office clerk and a ward nurse are in the same column in the same ink.

Then they read the whole column back again, all twenty-eight lines, and the supervisor wrote it on a form, and the clerk copied it into the residents' file and initialled it, and Lena Mir initialled it, and the vehicle went at ten to three.

And the wall kept the fourth column, and nobody in that building has ever been able to explain to anybody from the Office why that particular surface is not a document.

---

Elias Rook wrote the receipt copy in his own hand at the table in the records room at half past three, all twenty-eight lines and the correction and both sets of initials, because his hand is the only one in the building that is not attached to anything that can be taken, and he wrote it in about twenty minutes and got it right the first time, which is what he is actually good at and what nobody sent him to do.

Then he put the pen down and found that he was not tired, and that this was the worst of the day, because he had spent the entire day doing the one thing he still knows how to do, accurately, on the list of everything that had been taken out of a building he lives in, and there is no version of that sentence that is not a sentence about defeat, and he had known for a month that he was going to be tested on something other than his hands.

He had thought it would be a wall. It was a signature line.

At the board, at seven, Petar Lisk read the new column out in his capitals, and he read the clerk's number out loud as well, four times, because it was the only number on that wall that belonged to a stranger, and then he read the thing underneath it that nobody had thought to put there.

**TWENTY-EIGHT LINES AND ONE OF THEM WAS WRONG AND THE WOMAN WHO WROTE IT FIXED IT HERSELF IN FOUR MINUTES AND WROTE HER OWN NAME NEXT TO THE FIX.**
