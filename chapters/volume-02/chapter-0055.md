# Chapter 55 — Della Marner

The call came at twenty past seven on Tuesday night, the thirty-eighth day, to the corridor light that was not a light anymore but a bulb in a fitting on a card, and it was a bad line from a long way off, and Elias Rook took it in the records room because the front room had twenty-eight people in it and this was not for twenty-eight people.

Mara Okafor and Joon Park came in with him and neither of them was asked, and Elias understood about four minutes in that they were there to stop him, and he understood it about four minutes after that they were there because they were not going to let him do it on his own.

She said her name at the second ring.

"Mrs. Marner."

"That is correct."

"I am going to tell you how I am going to do this, because I have had thirty-eight days to work it out and you are the fourth person I have tried." Her voice was level and it was not a pleasant voice, because it had been used for this. "I am going to tell you what I know. Then I am going to tell you what I have been told. Then I am going to ask you one question, and it is going to be the only one, and if you answer it kindly I am going to hang up and I am going to know what kind of man you are. Are you ready?"

"Yes."

"I filed a missing-person notice on the Saturday morning. The first Saturday. Nine in the morning, at a desk at the Cordell end of the regional line, because that is where they put people when they do not know where else to put them. My husband is a station attendant at that end. He is fifty-eight. He has a service number and it opens four doors and he has never once in twenty-two years used it on anything that was not a door." She was reading from something. "I have a case number and I have a date and I have a piece of paper that says the transit authority has referred the question to the Continuity Office in Havenport, and that is the only paper anybody in two cities has ever given me."

"What did the Office tell you," Elias said.

"They did not tell me anything. That is what I am calling about." He could hear her put something down. "On the Monday I was told — kindly, and I want to say that, kindly, by a woman who had the queue in front of her and did not have to help me — that if my husband was on the Kestrel service on the Saturday, then he would be on the response list. That is what a response list is. It is a list of who was on the service."

"And there is no response list," Elias said.

"No," she said. "There is no response list, because there was no incident with people on it. The Office's finding is that it was a structural failure in legacy equipment. I have read the finding. I have read it nine or ten times, because I have a great deal of time and because it is four sentences, and it is correct. The equipment failed. The equipment did fail."

Elias put his hand flat on the table, and Mara Okafor said, from the other side of it, "Elias."

"I have not said anything," he said.

"You were about to. You were about to say *I know*, and you were going to mean it kindly, and I have watched you do that to four people this month and I have watched it help nobody." She did not raise her voice. "Say the arithmetic to her."

"Right," he said. "Mrs. Marner, the classification for that site names nine words, and the nine words are *catastrophic progressive structural failure attributable to legacy installation condition*, and an equipment failure does not carry passengers. There is no passenger list, and there has never been one, and the reason there is not one is that the document that would hold a person's name next to a site does not get made for a site with no event in it. The event is the thing. Without an event there is nowhere for a person to be written, and that is not a decision anybody made about you. It is a form that was designed the other way round, and it was designed that way by people who were not thinking about you."

"Thank you," Della Marner said. "That is the first sentence anybody has said to me in thirty-eight days that has a thing in it that I did not already know."

---

"The second thing I am going to tell you is worse," she said, "and I am telling you because you have a person in your building who came out of that ground and I have worked that out from his name appearing in a record somebody read out on the radio, and I am not going to make you say it, so I am going to say it.

"On the fourteenth I was told a records request takes four to six weeks. That is a real answer and the woman who gave it was not hiding from me, she was telling me the truth about a queue. So I have written to you because you are the only person I have found who has been in the building, and I am not angry with you, and I want that said at the start, because I know what this is. I know what this is. It is an inquiry, isn't it. I read the notice — they put one on a board at a shelter and a woman with a clipboard read it out loud for the people who could not read it. It is an inquiry into the response, and I am a member of the public, and you are a person in it, and I am not going to insult you by pretending I do not know that."

"Yes," Elias said. "It is an inquiry, and I am a subject in it, and I have not been able to read the file, and I have been in a building for five weeks where nobody can produce their own records on demand."

"Which brings me to the part where I ask," she said. "I am not going to ask you to apologize. I want to be very clear about that, because I think people assume. **Apologies are for people who did something.** I want his name on a piece of paper I can hold, and I want somebody to have to write it down."

Mara Okafor sat down on the edge of the table. She did not say anything, and she did not have to, because the sentence had just been said in a room with two other people in it and none of them could improve on it.

"That is a strange thing to want," Elias said, "given what I have just told you."

"It is the only thing I have wanted since the Saturday," Della Marner said. "Not a body. I know what a body is, I have had one, and I am not nineteen, and I will not sit here and be comforted about that. I do not want to know about a body. I want a document. I want to hold a piece of paper that says he was in that tunnel, that he was there, that somebody wrote it down and then had to sign it, and I do not care in the slightest what your inquiry does about it."

"Somebody wrote it down," Elias said. "In chalk. On a wall."

"Then I would like it on paper," she said. "And here is why that is a real sentence and not a figure of speech, and it is the thing I have learned in thirty-eight days at a desk. On the third week somebody in Havenport put a word on the paperwork about my husband, and the word was *error*. An error in a return. The word *error* means nobody has to find out who wrote the thing that left him off, because an error is an accident, and an accident has no author." She was not reading any more. "**A classification is not a mistake, so calling it a mistake means nobody has to find out who wrote it.** That is all I have learned. I have been sitting at a desk for thirty-eight days and that is the entire content of what I have learned, and I would like somebody to write it down, and I have been told four times by four different people that that is not the sort of thing anybody writes down."

Joon Park said, "I have written it down. I am the person who writes things down in that building. I have written down the word and the date and who said it and you have said it and I have said I have written it down where four people can hear me, because a thing that is only in a woman's head on a telephone in another city is a thing that dies in about a year."

Della Marner said, "Thank you."

"It is the correct thing to do and it is not a comfort," Joon said.

---

"Two more things and then my question," she said, and then there was about eight seconds of nothing at all, and then she came back. "A woman at that desk told me in the second week that it was not her department. She said it kindly. I wrote it down on the same pad I have been writing everything on, because I said to myself, I will hand this in one day, and there will be something to hand it in about. So I have thirty-eight days of a woman at a desk in my bag and a piece of paper from a transit authority and a case number, and I am going to walk into the first room in this city that has my husband's name in it and I am going to put all three of those things on the table."

"Mrs. Marner—"

"You have thirty seconds and I am going to use them." Her voice changed and it was not harder, it was closer. "My question is not *did you save him* and it is not *is he alive*. I have a man telling me every Friday that he is alive and I believe him and I would like to believe him. My question is this. **Is there a piece of paper.**"

Elias Rook put both hands on the table, and one of them did nothing, and he looked at the working one for a second and then he answered her.

"There is a piece of paper and I am going to tell you all of it, including the parts where we look bad and the part where I look worst, because you have been at a desk for thirty-eight days and you are owed a straight answer or you are owed nothing."

Then he did. It took eleven minutes.

He told her that a man had come up a corridor on a board on the thirtieth day at twenty past eight in the evening and had said his own name twice, and that the hall wrote it down twice because Lena Mir does not trust a name once, and that the page with it on it belongs to the team, and that the team has just lost a copy of its records to an order of impoundment served on Monday morning and a second copy that went out of the city on a bus on the twenty-ninth day and is in a building nobody can enter, and that on Thursday she should ask for the case number and a man called Joon Park and a date, and that the answer will take four to six weeks, which he is sorry about and cannot fix.

He told her that the notebook is in the same custody, and that the notebook has been demanded by name by an inquiry he is the subject of, and that he therefore cannot promise her it will exist on any particular day.

He told her that he can prove to her, sitting at a table, on an ordinary evening, that her husband was inside that ground for thirty days, and that he cannot prove it to her institution, and that this is the exact shape of the thing that has been eating his life since the fourth week, and that he is not going to soften it by telling her a story about a machine that found him, because the machine is not what got him out and he was not the machine's work.

"That's the worst one," he said. "You should have that last. I have got about six hours of people telling me I have got him out right and you are going to go home with the part that is mine."

"Thank you," Della Marner said. "I would like to do one more thing and then I am going to hang up, because it is bad and I have to sit with it."

"Go on."

"The two words," she said. "You said it twice, at the beginning, the two words you wrote in your own book with your own pen. Not a case number. The other thing. I have been trying for a fortnight to get a piece of paper in this city with anything on it that belongs to him."

Elias Rook did not move for about four seconds.

Then he said them.

He said them slowly, twice, and he spelled the second one, and he heard her write.

And then a second voice came on, in that building, forty minutes away by an ordinary road — a young woman, brisk, in a corridor with a queue in it, saying that she had been asked to write something down and did not mind doing it but wanted to know whether the lady wanted the surname spelled a particular way.

"No," Della Marner said. "Write it the way he said it."

"All right," the young woman said. "That is the second one with two r's? I have got it as I have heard it. I am writing it as I heard it."

The line went, and came back, and Della Marner said, "It is on a pad in a building in Cordell. It is not a document. It is a pad and a pen and a young woman who did not mind. And it is the first thing I have been able to put my hand on since the sixth of September, and I would like you to understand that it is not the paper, and that I know it is not the paper, and that I am going to keep it anyway."

"I understand that," Elias said.

"Two minutes," she said. "Hang on. I have got to write down that I asked you for it and you said it and the time, because I told you I would hand it in one day and I have got a date and a case number and a pad with a man's name on it and a young woman's handwriting, and that is four things, and next week it is going to be five."

---

He put the handset down at two minutes to eight and stood in the records room with a dead line in his hand.

He did not feel better. He did not feel worse. He felt **fast.**

That was the alarming part, and he noticed it on his own, standing there, about a minute and a half after the line went, without anybody having to point at it, and he did not say anything to anybody about it and he wrote one line in the notebook and underlined it, and the line was: *Faster. I have felt that before and I know exactly where, and it is not a good sign and it is not a sign of anything at all.*

It is the feeling he had in the records room on the Tuesday of the fourth week, when a voice had offered him a road that went fast enough to get him his sister, and he had been thirty-five, and he had four things on a wall and had not yet broken any of them.

In the front room, Cliff Marner was asleep in the chair with his coat over him, alive, eight days out of the ground, at fifty-six pounds and going up.

Forty minutes away, by an ordinary road, in a building with a queue in it, a woman was putting four things into a bag.
