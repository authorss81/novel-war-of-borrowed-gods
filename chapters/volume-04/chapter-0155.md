# Chapter 155 — The Hose Is On The Thursday Now

Cliff Marner was one hundred and eight days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and thirty-eight days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and thirty-eight days in, and Elias Rook was on the far bank at ten past six on the morning of Thursday the twenty-second of January, which was the first Thursday he had ever been in that yard, and it was there because a letter of the ninth of January had moved the Friday hose certification onto the Thursday, and the letter had said the slots would not be held twice and had said nothing whatever about the days.

R. Dunleavy had the gauge out and the book open before the man was through the gate, and the book was open at the right page, and the date was written at the top of it in pencil, and it had not been written at the top of it on any Tuesday or any Friday since the end of October.

"You wrote the date in."

"I wrote the date in," Dunleavy said. "I have never done that for a Tuesday and I am not going to start for a Tuesday. You are on a Thursday because a letter moved a hose and the letter did not ask me anything and it did not ask you anything either, and there was nobody in this yard on Friday a fortnight ago who could have told you where the hose had gone, and there was no line on any notice that said it, and I am not a department and I do not get told things. So I wrote the date in. If you had come in on Tuesday I would have had to look it up."

The needle went up and stayed up for about four minutes and came down and was the same as it had been on every other morning, and Dunleavy wrote two figures in the book in his own hand and turned it round.

"Same as December," he said. "Go on."

"That is the whole of what you have got to say to me."

"That is the whole of what I have got to say to anybody," Dunleavy said, and shut the book.

He stood at the gate for a minute with his coat on because the next bay had a man of about sixty in it who was doing the same thing he was doing and had been doing it since the collapse, and neither of them said anything, and then he said the thing he had come to say, which was that he now had two early mornings a week instead of one, and that the Friday half he had not had since the twenty-first of November was an inventory half, and that an inventory half is a half shift and nobody ever had a name for it.

"Since the twenty-second of November," Dunleavy said, before he was asked, reading the back of the book. "Every Friday since then, the book has a line for the hose and nobody's name against the second half, and I have never once put a line in it saying a man did not come, because he was never late and there was never anything wrong, and a yard book with that many empty lines in it over a winter is not a complaint against anybody. It is just a yard book."

"You have counted them."

"I count the empties," Dunleavy said. "It is a Thursday and there is a hose and I have got twenty minutes before the next bay wants the water, and you have got a bus in about eleven minutes and you are going to be in Tidegate before half past nine, and I have not got anything else to say to you and I never have."

---

Tidegate at about half past ten on the Thursday, and the yard had two lorries in it and the rest of the booking on the day work, and twelve working days to the fifth of February.

Nerys Hobbs had the sheet with the four ruled lines on it in a drawer in the survey office, because every line on it was signed and there was no fifth one to sign, and there was a different piece of paper on the trestle, which was a works' day sheet, and it was for the Friday, and it was for a street that had not been on any of the four nights.

"Sixteen to eighteen Murrow," she said. "Make safe, internal, second floor. Day work, daylight, five men, and it is the only thing in this yard this month that is not a clearance, and I have found that I do not much enjoy it."

"Anything wrong with it."

"Nothing is wrong with it," Hobbs said. "That is what I have found. In January I have had four nights and a tower and eleven people hurt and a night in December that was not a night and was on no sheet with a date on it, and this week I have got a street in daylight with five men on it and a ladder, and I have signed nothing about it because there is nothing to sign, and it is the first work since the eighth of January that I have not had to stop, and I have found out that I do not like being good at this."

---

The man on the pavement outside the newsagent at about twenty past two on the Thursday was about fifty-five and had a dog, and the dog was a gray thing with a bad back and it was standing with its back legs to the shop window and its front end out in the road.

He was not on the sheet and everybody in the shop knew why, because he lived at number thirteen Rennard and thirteen was not in any of the four blocks and had not been in any of the four blocks since the notice went up, and a foreman had printed a street on a gate and a street runs in odd numbers on one side and even on the other and a range is a street and a street is not a house.

"Five days," the man said, to Elias Rook, and did not say good morning. "On Wednesday lunchtime in that shop there were nine people in there and two of them knew me and one of them said my name out loud, and a week ago I was a man in a house at number thirteen Rennard, and now I am the man who is not on it."

"Your address is on it. Somebody else wrote it."

"I know somebody else wrote it," the man said. "That is what I have been standing on this pavement about for two days. My name is not on it and my address is on it and I did not write it, and there is a woman on this row who has been saying for eleven years that a door is not a household, and she is right about that, and it turns out she is right about that from both directions, and I would like somebody to explain to me how that works with me living in it."

Nobody on the pavement explained it to him.

"You have been in this district since the twenty-ninth of November," the man said, turning round, and he was not hostile, and that was the worst part of it. "That is not a thing this row has to guess at and everybody on it has worked it out in about four days. And every single person who has come up to me since Tuesday with an opinion about that wall has asked me how I feel about it, and you are the first one who has not, and I would like to know whether that is a decision or an accident."

"It is a decision," Elias Rook said, "and I will tell you what the decision is, and then you can decide what you think of it. There is nobody in this district whose job it is to ask you. That is not a way of putting it. A foreman has a job and his job is a street and a card for every door on it. A woman at a clinic has a job and her job is people who come to her. A woman who runs this post office has had her counter open for twenty-nine years and she is not anybody's district officer. There is no one. I have been in this district eight weeks and I am a fifth man on a road, and there is no part of me that is the person who comes to number thirteen Rennard with a form, and I would rather say that to you at two o'clock on a Thursday than have you find it out in a month and think I had been managing you."

The dog sat down on the pavement in the slush and did not go in.

"So you will not ask me."

"I will not ask you," Elias Rook said. "And that is a worse thing to say to a man than asking him, and you are entitled to be angry about it, and I am not going to soften it by telling you that I would ask if I could."

---

Winifred Alagoa came out of the shop at about half past two with her coat on, which she had not done for anything in three weeks, and she did not ask him anything either.

She asked him two things, and both of them were about the paper, and she asked them in the street in front of about six people, and she said before she asked that she was going to ask them in the street and not in the shop because she was not going to have it be a thing that happened at a counter.

"Is thirteen right."

"Thirteen is right."

"Do you want your name off it, or your address, or neither."

The man looked at the dog for a while.

"Address comes off," he said. "Nobody writes my name on it, so there is no sense in my house being on a wall in a district that empties houses. Name stays, because the day somebody decides a name column is a name column, I am going to be in it, and I would rather be in it by accident now than be argued into it later by a man with a good coat."

Nobody wrote either of those things down.

At about ten past four Peart heard about it in a yard and went out on foot at half past four, alone, in his own coat, and was gone twenty-two minutes and came back and said that the man at thirteen had a dog with a bad back and no way of getting it out of the way of a lorry, and had not asked the works for anything, and that the works had a card for the door because the works has a card for every door, and that no crew was going near it in daylight or dark until the man said.

"That cost me twenty-two minutes," Peart said. "I want it noted that it cost me twenty-two minutes of a working afternoon that nobody is going to pay me for and that is not going in a book, and that is the whole of what a foreman is for, and in about four years somebody is going to ask me what my job is and I am going to say it was that."

---

By Friday morning the strip of brown paper along the bottom of that sheet had three lines on it and two of them were in the third column, and nobody in the shop had counted them, and the address at number thirteen Rennard had not come off, and the man had said so himself on a pavement, and nobody had written it down, and it was still on the wall at a foot and a half above a counter that a woman of sixty-two had told them on Saturday was a foot and a half further than she would like.
