# Chapter 165 — The Same Shed

Cliff Marner was one hundred and eighteen days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and forty-eight days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and forty-eight days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at half past ten on Sunday morning, the first of February 2043, and paid the fare, and the counter at number six was shut behind a locked door, and the counter at number two shut at one, and the works' yard was shut because a yard shuts on Sundays, and there were four working days to the fifth of February.

The corner at half past ten had nobody on it, and nobody on this row has ever expected anybody to be on it on a Sunday, because the woman who reads doors on that corner has not read a door on a Sunday since 1931 and says so about once a decade.

So he went and stood by the wall at the top of the row for an hour and a half, which is what he does.

---

The office at the boatyard is a shed, forty feet by twenty, with a bench down one side, a table in the middle, a heater that has not worked since 2039, and a window that looks at the north slip.

Delia Marsh is fifty-four and she has had that yard since her father died in 2038, and there were three men on the books at ten past eleven on the first of February 2043, and there had been three men on the books every Friday since the twenty-second of November.

Emlyn Parry was forty-nine and could do steel and was the only man in that shed who could do steel. Meredith Loy was thirty-one and had been in the trade four years and had a van of his own on the days he was not there. Gwilym Sands was twenty-four, had come out of a college course in the autumn, and had a bicycle.

She had told the three of them on the twenty-third that she was going to be honest with them in the same shed on the first of February and would not know what she was going to say until she was saying it, and she had put a note on the door of the shed on Friday evening with the day and the hour on it and nothing else, and there had been eleven men at the meeting on the Friday afternoon and there were three men in the shed on the Sunday morning.

She did not have it written down. That is the first thing, and it is the thing that decides the whole of the morning.

"You are going to want a bit of paper," Delia Marsh said, "because that is what people want, and I am not going to give you one, and I want to say that I have thought about it and I am not being brave. If I write it down then it is a thing a yard decided, and a yard is a thing that decides things about money, and I have not got the authority to decide this and there is no form in this shed that says I have, and I have looked for one on Friday and there is not one, and this is a boatyard and not a council office."

"Then tell us," Sands said.

"The Cut has been down since the second week of November," Delia Marsh said. "The north slip has been dry since the twenty-second. It is the first of February. A yard that cannot put a boat in in February does not get that boat out in April, and I have said that in this shed before and you have all heard me say it and none of you have argued with it, and it is still true and it is still the whole of what I know. I am not going to stand here and tell you I think it is coming back, because I have got no way of knowing that and neither have you and neither has anybody in this town who would sell you the idea."

Nobody said anything.

"I have money for one man until the middle of March," she said. "That is the honest sentence and I have had it in a drawer since about Christmas and I have not said it out loud in this shed because every time I have opened my mouth about money in here I have said something that was not it. One man. Six weeks. And I am not going to pretend I can make that two, or that I have been trying to since December, because I have not, and because the men who would have been on those two would have found out in a fortnight and it would have been worse."

---

"Who," said Emlyn Parry.

He had not said anything for a while. He was forty-nine and he was the only man in that shed who could do the steel and he had been in that trade since he was twenty-one, and he had a daughter in her second year at the college in the city who had told him in December that she was thinking of not coming back.

"That is what I have been sitting here for two days working out how to say," Delia Marsh said, "and I am not going to say it, and I want to say the reason out loud, because you are all three of you standing in a shed and one of you is going to say in about a minute that I have not asked, and I would rather have said it first.

"There is no form in this shed that says a woman of fifty-four chooses which two of three men stop being paid, and I have looked, and I have looked in the drawer as well and not only on the wall, and there is not one in either. And I have thought about it as a person and not as a form, and if I choose then I am a woman in a shed picking which two men her father built a slip for stop coming to work, and I will do it and I will be right to do it and I will not be able to live in this town after it, and you three will not be able to look at me, and that is not a reason. That is just what it is."

---

Emlyn Parry put his hands on the edge of the table and left them there for about a minute and a half, not on anything in particular, and then took them off.

"My dad had that slip," he said. "Not the yard, the slip. He laid it in 'ninety-four. I have got a bit of his trowel in the back of the office and I have never once in my life thought about what a form was for and I have just spent a minute and a half thinking about it, so you are not the only one who has had a morning."

"I know that," Delia Marsh said. "I know. I am not asking anybody to have had a morning."

"I am not going to be the one who stays," Parry said. "I have had the offer on the table since the middle of January from a yard at Netherfield and it is not more money and it is worse work and it is eight miles, and I am going to take it, and I am telling the two of them in here before either of them says anything about standing on a slip for the yard, because I would not have wanted to be the one who found out afterwards that somebody had stayed out of loyalty to a dead man's trowel."

"That is your business," Delia Marsh said, and meant it, and could not make her voice do anything else.

"It is my business," Parry said. "That is exactly what I am telling you."

---

Nobody chose anything and the shed was warm enough by eleven o'clock and cold again by half past twelve, and at about one Delia Marsh said that she would know by Friday and that she was not going to spend a Friday deciding it, and that on the Monday she was going to write to the three of them the same letter, and that all three of them were going to get the same letter, and that she had decided that bit and she was not going to be talked out of it.

Meredith Loy said, at about ten to one, that he had a brother-in-law at the fish market who needed a man on the night, and then said that he was not asking for anything and was only saying it out loud so that there were two of them in a shed and not one.

Gwilym Sands, who was twenty-four, said nothing at all for the whole of the morning, and at the door he said that he had a bicycle and that his father would be very pleased and that he was not going to be one of the men who says his father would be very pleased.

That is the whole of what was said in that shed on the first of February 2043, and there is no minute of it and there is no letter yet and the letter is going to be the same letter three times, and none of it is on a nail anywhere and none of it is in a system and there is no form in this city that says a yard has to be honest with three men in a shed on a Sunday morning or in any other morning.

---

The three of them came out of the shed door at about ten past one into flat white light, and they stood about four feet apart in the way three men stand who have been in a room for two hours, and none of them looked at the wall, and the man on the wall with the brush had looked at all three and had said one word, which was a woman's name, and then had gone on with the door.

"She is going to write to us," Sands said, to nobody, about ten feet away, in the voice of a man of twenty-four who has not yet worked out how to carry a thing.

"I would not," said Loy, and got on his bicycle, and went.

Elias Rook was thirty feet from three men coming out of a shed, with a lamp in his coat he did not take out and his right shoulder going, and he had about nine questions and every one of them was a question he had no standing to ask, and he asked none of them, and the reason was not that he did not want to know. It was that the man on the wall had a van and a bed in a house in Weaver Street and no house in this district, and that Delia Marsh had a yard on the far bank, and that neither of them was in Tidegate, and that in eleven weeks the only people in Tidegate with any business in that shed were a woman of sixty-three who read doors, a woman of sixty-two who ran a counter, and a foreman whose booking ended on Thursday.

He had not asked the man on the wall either, and he had been talking to that man on a wall for eight weeks.

"What did she say," he did not ask.

The man on the wall went on with the door for about four minutes and then said, without turning round, "She said the money and she said what the money is. That is two sentences and I was stood at that door for two hours of it and I did not go in because it was not my shed and I have not got a shed, and I have not got anything in this town except a wall and a can of paint that somebody's brother-in-law gave me in November."

"Do you know what they are going to do."

"No," the man said, "and neither do they, and that is the correct answer from everybody on this street this morning, and in this town it lasts about eleven weeks and then somebody comes and tells one of them what they are going to do and it is a person with a letter, and the letter is the same letter three times or it is three letters and that is worse."

He put the brush down and looked at the door he had been painting for the fourth time since November, for the price of the paint.

"There is one thing I will take," he said, "and I said it in January and I have not had an offer since, so I will say it again. If that woman upstairs ever wants that door done properly, I will do it, for the paint and not for anything else, and I will not want to be thanked, and I will not be able to stop her thanking me, and that is the whole of what I have got, and it is not enough to keep a yard open, and I am not going to pretend it is."

He got the 11 at about ten past two and sat on the far side of the cut for an hour with nothing in his hands and nothing in his coat except a lamp, and a booking that had four working days left in it, and a corner that was going to have about twenty people on it again in twenty-eight days because a woman had put two lines in pencil at the top of her own sheet and had not slept, and a slip at the north end of a yard that had been dry since the twenty-second of November, which had nothing to do with anything anybody had said in a room in this district and which nobody in this district was ever going to be told about by anybody in this district.
