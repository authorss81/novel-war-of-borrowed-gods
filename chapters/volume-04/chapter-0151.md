# Chapter 151 — Three Of Four, And Something In The Third Column

Cliff Marner was one hundred and four days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and thirty-four days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and thirty-four days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Sunday morning, the eighteenth of January, and paid the fare, which was a fare and not a day of a run, because the run had stopped on the Wednesday before Christmas and he was not starting it again in a month in which nothing in that district had got any easier.

The works' yard shuts on a Sunday. The office inside it was locked, and had been unlocked at half past six that morning by the only person in this district who had ever been given a key to it.

He telephoned the shop at number six from the far side of the cut and Joon Park answered it.

"Three of four," Joon Park said. "She was in there at half past six and she signed it in there with the door open and she locked it behind her and she has gone home. That is all of it. I am not going to tell you anything else about it, and you are not going to ask me a third question on a Sunday morning."

That was the whole of the call. It took about ninety seconds.

---

The notice was still in its frame behind the glass on the yard gate, four rows and a bent drawing pin and a line at the foot saying it was printed by the yard and was not a program document, and the fourth row was the one they were working on tonight, and nobody had taken it down and nobody was going to.

There was nothing else on that gate. For nineteen years there had been something on that gate on a Sunday morning, and there was not one sheet on it now, and that was not a decision anybody had made.

Nev Peart was on the other side of a locked gate at about ten past ten and he came out to the wire rather than open it.

"Five till the fifth of February, Sundays off, and the board has to come back for money after that, and in my experience it does not come back in the same year it has already spent. So there is a Tuesday in February on which there is going to be no sheet on this gate at all, and I have not got anything to put on it, and that is the first time in nineteen years I have been able to say that."

"Are you going to take the four rows off."

"I am not going to take the four rows off," Peart said. "They were true when they went up and they are true now, and in about four years somebody is going to want to know what this yard was told to do in January of 'forty-three and there is going to be one piece of paper in the world that says it. And there is a nail on the other side of that gate with nothing on it since Christmas, and if a true thing comes along it goes on that nail, and if nothing comes along then the nail stays empty and nobody has to invent something for it."

---

By half past ten the east side of Cutbank Row was dark from number fourteen up to the top of the row, and there was a skip on the pavement outside the shop that sells door furniture and a lorry on the double yellows that had not come from the works, because the works was shut, and four men in a private firm's overalls were carrying a chair down a garden path at number sixteen and being told by somebody inside number sixteen that they could not have the chair.

The woman of seventy-nine who had come out of number thirty-one on Saturday morning to look at a nail and had gone back in was not on the row. Her light was off. It had not been dark at half past ten on the Saturday, and nobody on that row had said one word about it to anybody.

He went up at about eleven because the door at number twenty-two was open eight inches.

---

The boiler was going. There was a bag by the door with four things in it and a folded blanket on top of the bag, and a school exercise book face down on a table with a cloth on it, and a chair on the table-side with a blanket over the back of it that was not for visitors and had never been for visitors.

"Sit down," Winifred Alagoa said. "You are going to say a number at me and I want you to have said it before you have decided what your face is going to do."

He said it.

"Two to twelve Cutbank Row is on the fourth row of the notice," Elias Rook said. "That is tomorrow night. That is a Monday and a crew of four and it has been on that gate since the thirty-first of December."

The boiler went through about a third of what it had to do.

"You have been in this district since the twenty-ninth of November," Alagoa said, "and you have just told me my own street's numbers off a nail, and I want you to hear yourself do it, because I have not asked you for a single number since the twenty-ninth of November and I have got a sheet of my own with a thousand and something doors on it and I read it out on a Saturday for nothing."

"You are not in that block."

"I know I am not in that block," Alagoa said. "Fourteen to twenty-two went on Saturday night and I have been out of it since ten o'clock on Saturday morning, and I am going to be out of the lower half of this side of the row tomorrow night as well, so it is two nights, and I have the back room of this shop for the Sunday and the Monday, and on the Tuesday night I am going to a woman I have not seen since 'thirty-nine, and I will be in this shop on the Wednesday at one o'clock as usual. That is the whole of my arrangements and you can have all of them and there is nothing else in there, and I am telling you them because you have just given me a number and it is only fair that somebody in this row knows where the two of us are."

He did not ask where the woman was. He had not asked and he was not going to, and the not-asking was the hardest thing he did in that kitchen, and it was not hard because of the stairs.

"Now say why you are telling me," Alagoa said.

"I do not have a clean reason."

"You have a reason," she said. "You have had one since the moment you got off that bus and you have been carrying it up my stairs and it is about four foot long and it weighs about a pound, and I would like to hear it, and then I am going to go and do my packing and you are going to go and sit down somewhere with a cup of tea in it."

He thought about it for longer than the boiler took.

"I have had four things off a man in a coat since November," Elias Rook said, "and every one of them was me deciding somebody should know something. That is the whole of what I have been good at in this district and it has not once been what anybody wanted. So I have read a number off a nail that a foreman printed himself and brought it up a set of stairs to a woman who did not ask, and that is what I have come to say, and you are the first person on this row getting it as a gift and not as a bill, and I have not got a better sentence for it than that one."

"That is nearly it," Alagoa said, "and it is not quite it, and I am not going to finish it for you at eleven o'clock on a Sunday. There is a cup on the table already because I put it out at about half past ten without deciding to, and that is the most embarrassing thing I have done this month and I have told you about it on purpose."

He sat down. The tea was cold and she made it again.

Then she said, with her back to him and the bag open on the floor: "Fourteen went on Saturday and the lower half goes on Monday and there will be nobody on this side of the row from the second to the twenty-second, and the shop on the corner has had no trade in it since the twenty-second of November because the Cut has been down since the second week of November and the slip at the boatyard has been dry since the twenty-second, and a closed boatyard is where they send a man, and that is all true, and I am not going to say one word to you about how any of those four things are connected."

"I am not going to write it down."

"No," Alagoa said. "I know."

---

He got the 11 at ten past twelve and came back at half past one, because there was nothing to do in Tidegate and nothing whatever to do on a Sunday, and the newsagent at number two was open and Mrs Postridge was behind her counter and was not interested in any of it.

The sheet was on the nail above the counter, four feet of unruled paper, four columns in pencil, more than two hundred lines, and it had been there three days.

Mrs Postridge had a pencil behind her ear and she did not have the sheet in her hands and she was not going to.

"Third column," Elias Rook said, from about eight feet away.

"I have not read it."

"There is one line in it."

"There is a line in it," Mrs Postridge said, "and it was not there on Friday and it was not there on Saturday morning when I opened, and it was there at about two this afternoon, and I have been looking at it for a quarter of an hour and I have not read it, because it is a foot and a half above my counter and I am sixty-two and I am not getting on a chair."

He read it from where he was standing.

```
19 RENNARD          17th        knocked 21:40 — no answer
```

The fourth column on that line was empty. Every other line on that sheet with anything at all in the third column had a name in the fourth column, because the fourth column had a heading over it in capitals underlined twice and the heading was a name.

"That is a Saturday date and it says somebody knocked at nineteen Rennard at twenty to ten on Saturday night and nobody answered, and nobody has put a name on it."

"Then whoever it is did not want a name on it," Mrs Postridge said, "and there is a difference between that and being somebody nobody can ask, and I have run a shop on this row for twenty-nine years and I know the difference at about four o'clock in the afternoon on a Sunday without reading a word of it."

Mrs Postridge put the pencil back behind her ear.

---

Winifred Alagoa came in at about ten past three for a stamp and read it from about four feet away, which is what everybody in this district does, and read it again.

"Nineteen Rennard," she said. "That is inside tomorrow's block, and tomorrow night is a crew of four and a ladder, and the woman who wrote that line is going to be in that house when they come."

"Yes."

"Then somebody was in it on Saturday night and did not come down, and has written down that they did not come down, and has not written their name, and is not going to be asked, and I would like you to not be the one who asks either, and you are going to tell me you are not, and I am going to believe you and we are both going to know what it costs."

"I am not going to ask her."

"That is a Monday night's work on the fourth row of a notice a foreman printed himself with no addresses on it," Alagoa said, "and the works does not work off that sheet and it never will, and Hobbs would tell you a door is not a household and be right, and there is a card in a drawer in that yard with a floor and a stair on it and not a street. And there is now a line of paper on a nail above a counter that says a woman was in number nineteen and did not answer a door, and there is nothing in the world anybody can do with that. It is a thing that is known and not a thing that is used, and I would like you to go and sit down."

He got the 11 at about half past four, and the fare was the fare, and the thread had been on under the length of Cutbank Row for the whole of the afternoon and had not given him anything, which is what it had done every time since the first of January.

Tomorrow night there were fifteen to twenty-one Rennard and two to twelve Cutbank Row and a crew of four and a ladder, and a woman of about eighty-four on Rennard who opened her door to anybody who knocked, and a woman at number nineteen who had been written about by somebody who had said, in a shop, that nobody was going to be asked.
