# Chapter 164 — The Sentence At The Top Of It

Cliff Marner was one hundred and seventeen days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and forty-seven days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and forty-seven days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Saturday morning, the thirty-first of January 2043, and paid the fare, and walked down to the bollard at half past ten, and it was two degrees and there were about twenty people and there would be more by eleven, and the counter at number six was shut behind a locked door because it is shut on Saturdays, and there were four working days to the fifth of February.

The east side of Cutbank Row was standing empty from the second to the twenty-second and there was one light on in it, on the third floor, over the shop that sells door furniture, and it was on at half past ten in the morning, and it had been on at half past ten on the two Saturdays before this one, and nobody in the district had ever said one word about it to anybody.

---

She read the four nights off the notice the way she has read that notice four times every Saturday since it went up, and then she did Rennard, and then Murrow in a flatter voice, and then Marlow Street, and then Sallow Court last, and it took her about twenty-two minutes and nobody interrupted her.

Then she read the sheet, and she did not read it out.

She read the first column off it, address by address, for about eleven minutes, at the same speed she reads her own round, and about thirteen of the twenty people knew their own address being said and about seven did not, and nobody said anything at all.

---

The sheet was rolled under her arm with the top folded back, which is how she has carried it since about 2031, and it came open in the third minute of the first column, and a man of about fifty-five with a gray dog, standing about four feet back with his hands in his pockets, said, "What is that at the top of it."

It is two lines in a shopkeeper's pencil above the first address and under the heading.

SECOND SATURDAY · 31 JAN · THE CORNER · HALF PAST TEN

nobody in this district is answerable for it. say so at the corner.

"Who wrote that," the man said.

"I did," Alagoa said, "on Tuesday morning at about half past eleven, in pencil, at the top of my own sheet, and I put it there because on Saturday the twenty-fourth I stood on this corner with about nineteen people on it and asked for one thing to be written down and nobody wrote it down. That is the whole of it and you have read it."

Nobody said anything. The man with the dog looked at the two lines for about four seconds and then at the top of the first address, and about six people near him read the two lines at the same moment, which is what happens on this row, and then everybody looked at the ground, and the dog sat down in the slush.

"It is not a decision," somebody said.

"No," Alagoa said. "It is a notice that says nobody is answerable for it, and I have made myself answerable for it by writing it, and I said that out loud in a shop on Tuesday to a man in a coat and he is the only person in this district who heard me say it, and I have not slept since Tuesday, and I would like it left alone now that it is out."

---

Then somebody said it.

Not the whole thing. The second half, in a flat voice, the way a person reads a sign out loud to check whether it is true.

"nobody in this district is answerable for it."

And about four other people said it with him, in the same flat voice, at about the same time, the way a crowd does a thing, and then a woman of about forty said it again properly, all of it, both lines, including the first one, and it went round the corner the way a thing goes round a corner, and Elias Rook was standing in it and it happened on both sides of him.

Nobody had decided to do it. He had been in this district for nine weeks and he had watched about two hundred and forty people learn four nights and four streets from a nail nine inches above a counter, and he had watched a woman read a door column out loud to a pavement for eleven years, and he had watched a man in a works jacket point at a gate and say that a person was on it up there, and he had watched a hall of two hundred people believe a competent man for an hour, and not one of those things had ever once turned into a sentence that twenty people said out loud together in a street.

There is no form for it. There is no rota for it. Nobody can be found in four years who arranged it.

---

The man from number nine Rennard asked the question at about a quarter to eleven, with about twenty people standing about, and he had been at this corner every Saturday for a month and had not been thanked for any of it.

"Is that a decision."

"No," Alagoa said. "It is a sentence and a date and a corner and a time, and I wrote it, and I am not a chair and I am not an office and I have not taken a vote and I am not going to, because a vote makes a meeting and a meeting has to have a chair."

"Then what is it," the man said, "and I am asking because I have wanted that paper off that wall for a fortnight and if that is what this is then it is going to be there in February and in March and I am going to be standing on this corner in April being told the same thing."

"It is worse than a decision," Alagoa said, "and I will not soften it. A decision you can vote against on a Saturday and win. That is a sentence that twenty people said out loud at ten to eleven this morning because somebody was cold, and it will still be true in April, and there is no way in this city to vote against a thing that got said by accident."

---

Roy Pellow came up to the counter at about ten past eleven and asked for the shopkeeper's pencil, and gave it back inside of a minute.

He did not ask anybody to help him and he did not explain himself and he did not wait for a gap in the conversation.

He took the sheet off the counter, and held it flat against the edge of the counter with his right hand, and put a single line through thirteen Rennard in the first column with a pencil that had been sharpened at some point in the last forty years, and then wrote in the fourth column, in the same hand, in capitals.

"You told me on the twenty-second of January that there was no form," he said, without looking up, "and that nobody wrote either of those two things down, and that the address was still on the wall. I have thought about that for nine days. I said address comes off and name stays, and I said I would rather be in that column by accident than be argued into it later by a man with a good coat, and I have been standing here for nine days waiting for somebody to be wrong about that, and nobody has been, and so I have come and done it myself, and it is not an accident, and I have known that since about a quarter past eleven this morning."

He put the sheet back up.

Elias Rook did not thank him and did not say it was the right thing to do, because a man who has spent nine days being talked at by about nine people in a district does not need a seventh voice, and because what Roy Pellow had actually done was hand a piece of paper back to it, in a pencil, in a shop, and there was nobody in this district qualified to tell him whether that was a good idea.

---

At about half past eleven the door of the shop opened and the man from the door shop next door came in and stood about four feet back, which is where everybody in this district stands to read anything on this row.

He had been on that row forty-two years and he had said in a shop on the twenty-first of January that he had watched a woman read those doors out loud every Saturday since about 2031 and had never once gone out and stood next to her, and he had said that out loud in a room he was in on purpose.

Ivy Nancarrow was at the counter with her glasses on and her coat open, and the fourth column was nine inches above the counter and the strip of brown paper's fourth column was on the plasterboard to the right, and there were fourteen names on the plasterboard in biro.

The man from the door shop read the fourth column to her from the beginning to the end, at the speed a man reads a list out loud, and he did not hurry any of it, and when he had finished he read her the fourteen on the wall as well, and told her they were not under anything and did not know which lines they belonged to.

"Thank you," Ivy Nancarrow said.

"I am not doing anything," the man said. "I am standing in a shop on a Saturday and I have got nothing else to do, and I would rather you did not thank me for it, because I have read that sheet from about four feet away for eleven years without once coming in here, and a person in this row who has never come in here has not earned it."

---

The thread came on under the length of that row at about eleven o'clock with about twenty people standing on it in two degrees, and it stayed on for about fifty minutes, and it gave him nothing at all, which is what it had done every time since the first of January, and which was by then the only fact about it he was entitled to.

There was a woman in the flat over the shop that sells door furniture and he did not know that and nobody told him and he was not going to find out, and there was a woman of eighty-four standing in a cold shop being read a column out loud by a man from next door, and there were about twenty people on a corner saying a sentence nobody had arranged, and a man had struck his own address out of a wall with a shopkeeper's pencil in his own hand, and the thread was on under all of it and had nothing to say about any of it, and there was a lamp in his coat he did not take out and his right shoulder had gone on the thirteenth of January and was no better at eleven o'clock in the morning on a Saturday than it had been at eight o'clock on a Tuesday.

---

He got the 11 at about ten past twelve and sat on the far side of the cut for an hour and a half, which was the longest he had sat on that wall since he came to this district.

Twenty people had said a sentence out loud in a street in this district on a Saturday morning, and no form in this city asked whether they had the right, and in about four years there would be a document in a file that said the second Saturday of January happened, and it would not have one word in it about the sentence, and the sentence would be the only part of the whole of January that anybody could repeat from memory, and there is no index for that and no column for it and nobody's name goes against it.

A man of fifty-five had given a pencil his own name to a piece of paper, which was the thing nobody had done for nine days, and a woman of forty-two had stood in a shop and read a wall to a woman of eighty-four because he had been in a shop on the twenty-first of January and had said out loud that he had never once stood next to her, and neither of them had been asked to by anybody, and one of them had given up the only thing he had said he wanted, which was to be in the column by accident, and the other one had given up forty-two years of standing four feet back.

In twenty-eight days it would be the fourth Saturday, and Alagoa had said out loud in a shop on Tuesday that on the fourth Saturday somebody in that row was going to stand in the cold and say it was not fair, and that they would be right, and that after that it would be a notice with a woman's shopkeeper's pencil at the top of it and a man's coat on the wall, and after that it would be a thing with an owner, and there was no form in this city for giving one back.
