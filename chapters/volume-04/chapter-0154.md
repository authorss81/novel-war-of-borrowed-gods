# Chapter 154 — There Is Nowhere On It To Say Why

Cliff Marner was one hundred and seven days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and thirty-seven days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and thirty-seven days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Wednesday morning, the twenty-first of January, and walked down to Cutbank Row at about ten because the weather was flat and there was nothing to be in Tidegate for before ten.

Nev Peart had the shift book open on the bench with the band round the corner of it and had said nothing about it for four days, and on the Wednesday morning he said it.

"I am going to hand this in in February," he said. "Not to the region. To the board's engineer, in a room, with a date on it, and he is welcome to read it, and there is no version of it where anybody reads all of it, and I have known that since I was thirty and I am not going to pretend to be surprised about it in front of you."

"Then I am not going to ask you for a line of it."

"That is the correct thing and it is not why I said it," Peart said, "and I said it to you and not to Hobbs because I have known Hobbs nineteen years and you have known me eight weeks, and a man hands a book in to somebody he has known a long time and not to somebody he has not, and I would like that written on the wall of whatever room he takes it into, in my own words, and I have just said them in my own words, so that is the wall."

He put the band back over the corner of it and did not say anything else about it.

The man at the boatyard had stopped painting the door. The door was a door and it had been a door and it was going to be a door in February, and he was on the wall doing nothing with his hands, which is the thing a man looks like when a yard has been shut for a fortnight and a second one.

The third column had two lines in it.

17 RENNARD     17th    knocked about ten — opened

The second line had a name against it, which was a name anybody on two streets could have put to a house, and the first had nothing at all in the fourth column, and the fourth column had a heading over it in capitals underlined twice and the heading was a name.

Mrs Postridge had not read either of them and said so, and had not been asked, and the pencil was behind her ear.

---

The woman of about sixty from number nineteen was in the queue at ten past eleven for a packet of stamps and she knew him and he knew that she knew him, and there were three people behind her.

"Good morning," Elias Rook said.

"I wrote it Saturday night," she said, before he had said anything else. "And on Monday night, when your man was at my door and it was gone ten, I stood in this shop with the shopkeeper's pencil in my hand for about ten minutes and I did not write it a second time, because one of them is a fact and two of them is a woman making sure somebody believes her, and I have decided which of those I am."

He did not ask her which line was hers, because he knew, and because there were three people behind her in a shop at ten past eleven.

"You do not have to put it anywhere," he said.

"I know I do not," she said. "That is what I have been sitting with since Monday night. You have got an address, and a date, and whether anybody came, and a name, and there is nowhere on that paper to write the rest of it, and the rest of it is that I was on the stairs in the dark with a lamp that had gone out about ten minutes before and I could hear somebody on the landing and I did not want to wake him because he has been awake since Christmas with his chest, and I did not come down, and I am sixty-one and I can manage a stair in the day and I could not have got that coat on in the time it would have taken me to answer a door, and that is the whole of it and none of it fits in a line."

"Then it does not go in a line."

"It does not go in a line," she said. "It goes in the wall of my front room and it goes in my own head and it will go nowhere else, and that is the arrangement, and I have lived in this street for nine years and I would like you to go and ask somebody about a column and see what happens."

She paid for the stamps and went out into the rain and did not look back at the counter, and he did not follow her, and the queue went forward by one person.

---

At about half past twelve the shop filled up and about nine of them stayed.

The man who spoke first was about forty-one and he lived at number nine Rennard and he had been in the shop once before this week, buying tobacco, and everybody in the queue knew his face.

"That comes down," he said. "I want that off the wall today and I have thought about it for four days and I am saying it now with nine people behind me."

Mrs Postridge put the counter down flat on its bracket, which she does.

"You are going to tell me why," she said.

"A woman came up my stairs on the seventh of January with a printed card and a photograph of a woman on it who is not her," the man said, "and she told me verification protects me. And I have looked at that sheet every day since Saturday, because I walk in here every day, and my name is on it, and my street is on it, and there is a column on it for a name, and I have not asked anybody to put me on it and I did not put me on it, and somebody has, and now there are two hundred and forty lines on that wall with two hundred and forty households on them and not one of us asked to be on it. That is not a complaint about a program. That is a complaint about a nail."

Nobody in that shop said anything for about four seconds, which on a Wednesday lunchtime in Cutbank Row is a long time.

"You are right about the card," Elias Rook said. "She came up the stairs at this end of the row on the seventh and she was let as far as the step of this one and refused, and I watched her go down those steps, and the reason she was refused is on a piece of paper in a kitchen upstairs and it is not about your office and it is not about a program either."

"Then what is it about."

"It is about her street," Elias Rook said, "and I have got about nine words for why and I am not going to give them to nine people in a shop, and if I do, they will be a sentence in this room by lunchtime tomorrow, and they will be a sentence in this district by Friday, and then somebody will use them at a hearing and it will be a thing a woman said in her own doorway about her own road."

A man from the door shop next door had come in for a stamp and had been standing about four feet away with his hands in a tray of brush handles, and he said, "I have been on that row forty-two years and I have watched a woman read those doors out loud every Saturday since about 2031 and I have never once gone out and stood next to her, and I am not going to now, and I would like everybody in this shop to notice that I have just said that out loud in a room I am in."

Winifred Alagoa came in at about ten past one for a stamp, which she did not need, and stood at the counter and heard the whole of the end of it, and did not say a word in front of nine people.

She said it outside, on the pavement, with her back to the door, and there were about six of them out there by then.

"It stays up because I put it up," she said. "It does not stay up because anybody voted on it and it does not stay up because the works or the board or a program or a man in a coat decided it should, and if you want it to come down you are going to have to come and ask me, and I will say no, and you are allowed to be angry with me and I am not going to pretend you are not."

"You have got a rule for that."

"I have got a mouth and a door and a row I have been on for thirty-eight years," Alagoa said, "and there is no rule and there is no form and nobody has ever asked me for a rule and nobody is going to be given one this week."

"And the rest of it," the man said. "The two hundred and forty. That is not you."

"That is not me," Alagoa said, "and it is not you, and it is the second week of January, and I have got about four things I could say on that pavement and every single one of them is about a different person who is not in this conversation, and I am not going to say any of them here and get somebody else in trouble for it before I have had a cup of tea."

---

The third line had been on the sheet since about eleven o'clock that morning and it was in Winifred Alagoa's hand, and it said that nobody came, and it was the only line on four foot of paper and a strip of shelf that was written by somebody who had walked the length of it reading it.

He had noticed it at about a quarter to twelve from about eight feet away. He had said nothing about it at half past twelve when nine people were arguing about the nail, and nothing about it at ten past one when its author was standing six feet away from him on a pavement, and everybody who came into that shop on the Wednesday said nothing about it, and Mrs Postridge was in it too without knowing she was in it, and none of the three of them looked at either of the others while it was happening.

It was true. She had been out of that flat since ten o'clock on Saturday morning with a bag and a folded blanket, and she had been out of it when a crew of four came down that side of the row at about nine on the Saturday night, and nobody had knocked, because there was nobody there, and there is no version of that door where anybody was standing behind it at nine o'clock.

---

The fourth column of that sheet had a heading over it in capitals, underlined twice, and the heading was a name, and a hundred and ninety-one names had been counted into it on a Friday night in January by a woman with a shopkeeper's pencil who had said it would be wrong by nine by Tuesday, and on the Wednesday morning there were more, and nobody was counting.

There were two lines on that sheet with something in the third column and nothing at all in the fourth, and they were at nineteen Rennard and at twenty-two Cutbank Row, and they were in two different hands, and both of them were written by a person who was not behind that door on the seventeenth of January — one because she was on a stair in the dark with a lamp that had gone, and one because she was not in the house at all.

Neither of them was going to be asked. There is no form in this city that asks a person whether they were in their own house on a particular night, and there is no form in this city that asks a person why they did not answer a door, and in about three weeks the whole east side of that row is going to be standing empty from the second to the twenty-second, and every one of those addresses is going to be a line on that paper with a name under a heading, and in the middle of February there is going to be somebody in an office who asks who stood in those houses on the seventeenth of January, and the paper is going to be the only thing in this district that knows anything about it, and it is not going to be able to say, because there is nowhere on it to say.
