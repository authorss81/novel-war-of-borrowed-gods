# Chapter 161 — Then I Shall Have To Stand In The Shop

Cliff Marner was one hundred and fourteen days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and forty-four days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and forty-four days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Wednesday morning, the twenty-eighth of January 2043, and paid the fare, which was a fare and not a day of a run, because the run had stopped on the Wednesday before Christmas and was not being restarted in a district with seven working days left in it.

Nine days earlier Nerys Hobbs had carried four signed ruled lines out of a locked drawer in a yard and put them on a table at the counter at number six, and in those nine days nothing at all had happened to them, and the reason nothing had happened is that the counter at number six is open Monday to Friday from nine till half past four and this was a Wednesday, and about fourteen people had walked in and looked at the four lines and about nine of them had asked the woman behind the counter what the table was for.

That is the whole of what a piece of paper does in public in a district that has run out of everything else. Somebody asks what it is for.

---

Wendy Tallow had the counter log open at the left-hand end of the counter and the district's own arrival book open at the right-hand end, and the four lines were on the table between them, and nobody signed for the four lines and nobody took them away.

"The log wants a line," Tallow said. "Anything that comes onto this counter and stays on this counter, I write a line. That is what the log is. If I do not, then in a year somebody is going to ask me where a piece of paper on my counter came from, and I am going to have to say that a woman carried it out of a yard in a coat."

Hobbs was there at about ten with the terminal off and her coat still on, and said, "Then write the line."

"The log has three columns," Tallow said. "Name, date, and what was asked. If I write what-was-asked for those four lines, then what was asked for is a street, and there is no column on this book for a street. And if I write not-applicable in that column about nine times a day, then the column stops meaning anything by the middle of February, and after that it is not a column, it is a habit. I have got two of those in this building already and I am fond of both."

"Write the line and leave the third column empty," Elias Rook said. "Nobody said the third column had to be filled in."

"It has never been empty," Tallow said. "Not once in four years. A date every day, a name every day, something asked every day. If I start leaving a gap in it in January, then somebody in four years will be looking at the gap instead of at the lines, and they will think somebody found something in it."

Nobody said anything about that for about four seconds, which on a Wednesday morning on Cutbank Row is a long time.

Hobbs picked the pen up and put it down again without writing anything.

"Then it does not go in the log," she said. "I am not going to have the only piece of paper in this district that anybody wrote down afterwards about four nights, sitting on the same table as the only book in this district that has ever put a person into it without asking that person anything. And I am not going to have a clerk in this building deciding in January which of those two is more true. It is on the table. That is the end of it."

The arrival book is the district's own. It had been on a shelf on the board's third floor for twenty-six years and it had come down to Cutbank Row for the first time in its life on Friday the eighth of January, because a district being emptied on a schedule with no column on it for anything a person goes in has acquired a public counter. Nobody had indexed it, nobody had ever been asked to write in it except the women who have worked that counter over the years, and it records a person, a day, and what they came about, and it has never once had a street in it.

It was open at January, because it is open at whatever month it is.

---

The woman of eighty-four came in at about eleven and gave her address before anybody had asked her for it, which is what everybody on this row has been doing since November, and which nobody has ever said out loud.

"Seventeen Rennard," the woman said. "I have been in this district sixty-one years and I have not been down that street since the Monday, because there is nothing in my house any more and I have got a bag packed that I have not unpacked. Somebody on a corner on Saturday told me there is a table with four lines on it at number six."

There was no queue, which was the only reason she had come in at all.

The four lines are four ruled lines with a date, an hour, four men, street walked, barriers down, and Hobbs's name at the foot of all four of them, and the figures in the middle of them are small, and the paper is lying on a table at the height of a table.

The woman of eighty-four had her glasses on and could not get them.

Tallow read them to her, all four, without hurrying any of them, and she read the hours out in a voice that made it plain she had done arithmetic on them before, and when she had finished the woman said, "Is my street on it."

"There are no streets on it," Tallow said. "The notice on the gate has four block ranges and not one door number in it, and that is a foreman's decision, and he will tell anybody who asks him. Those four lines are about the mornings."

"Then it is not about me," the woman said, in the tone of a person confirming a delivery. "Right."

Then she said, "And when does that yard finish."

"The fifth of February," Elias Rook said. "That is the last day of the booking."

"And after the fifth."

"After the fifth the board has to come back for money," Elias Rook said, "and the foreman has said out loud in his own yard that in his experience it does not come back in the same year it has already spent it. I am not going to improve on that for you."

"I have been in a hostel since the Monday week," she said, "and I have got until the eighth, and that is not a complaint. I am telling you the date because you are on a bus in this district every weekday, and I would like somebody in it to know before it happens and not after."

Nobody asked her a second question, and not out of politeness. There is no form in this city that asks a woman of eighty-four what her arrangements are.

Nobody asked her a second question, and not out of politeness. There is no form in this city that asks a woman of eighty-four what her arrangements are.

Then he walked her down to number two, because that is what you do on this row, and because the other thing she had come for was on a nail in that shop and not in this one.

---

Mrs Postridge had the counter down on its bracket, which she does, and the sheet on the nail above her, four foot and a strip of shelf, the columns not lining up, the heading in capitals sitting up at the top where it had always sat, and it was nine inches lower than it had been a fortnight ago.

The woman stood about four feet back, which is where everybody in this district stands to read anything on this row, and read the first column for about a minute and a half, going down it, and stopped at nothing in particular.

"Ivy," Mrs Postridge said. "That is Ivy Nancarrow. She has come to this counter since 1982, she buys two stamps a fortnight, and she has never once asked me for a bag."

"I want the fourth column," the woman said.

Elias Rook looked at it from eight feet away, which is the only distance from which anybody in this district reads it.

The fourth column has a heading over it in capitals, underlined twice, and the heading is a name. There are more than two hundred names in it. It reads for a person who is under seventy and standing up with her glasses on. It does not read for a woman of eighty-four looking up at an angle in January with the light behind her.

"That is no good to you," the woman said, before anybody had said anything, and Elias Rook said, "No. That is no good to you."

"Then I shall have to come and stand in the shop," the woman said, "and somebody will have to read it."

Nobody in that shop offered.

He had it in front of him and it took him about four seconds. A rota needs somebody to own it. Nobody in this district owns anything. Four people have already said no in four buildings in ten days to four requests of exactly this shape, and he has personally said no twice to a one-page sheet with a second name on it. The cost of all of that is that a woman of eighty-four is going to have to stand in a cold shop on a Saturday morning and be spoken to, and the only reason it is arranged is that she said so out loud in front of two shopkeepers and a man in a coat.

"I am in this district every weekday on a bus," Elias Rook said. "I am not going to be the person who reads that column to you, and I would rather say that now than let you find it out in March, and I am not going to soften it by telling you I would do it if somebody asked me properly. Nobody is going to ask me properly, and you have not asked me, and I would rather you did not have to."

"Then who reads it."

"I do not know," Elias Rook said. "That is the honest answer and I have got very good at it this winter. Mrs Postridge has told me herself that she is nobody's district officer, and that woman has run this counter for twenty-nine years and has never once in my hearing offered to be anybody's anything. And Winifred Alagoa will stand in this shop on a Saturday morning and look at anybody who comes round the corner, and she has said out loud that she is not a chair and not an office. Neither of those is a rota. They are two women who have each said a thing out loud once and have not written it down, and you have just asked me for the thing this district has not got, four days before somebody stood on a corner and said it out loud anyway."

Winifred Alagoa had been in the shop for the whole of it and had not said a word, and now she said one.

"Four days," she said. "You have come in here on a Wednesday morning and asked me whether I will read a wall to a woman of eighty-four every Saturday until one of us is dead, and I am not answering that in a shop on a Wednesday. If I answer it on a Wednesday it is a rule, and I have never made one in this district in my life."

"You do not have to say yes."

"I have not said no," Alagoa said. "That is a different thing and you have known it is a different thing since about the twentieth of January, and you have never once tried to make a no into a yes so that you could go away with something. There are about twenty people on that corner on a Saturday and I read the first column out loud to all of them for eleven years. If somebody stands in this shop at ten past ten and reads the fourth column to whoever wants it, then it is a corner and not a service and not a form, and it will stop the day nobody stands there, and nobody in this district has it as a job."

"And if nobody stands there."

"Then she stands in the shop and nobody reads it to her," Alagoa said, "and I would rather that than a list."

---

He got the 11 at about ten past three and sat on the far side of the water for an hour with his right forearm across the page.

In seven days a booking that had started on the fifth of January would be finished. A foreman would take eleven names off his own gate with a piece of string, because he had decided in a yard on a Friday that a piece of paper about eleven injuries is not a leaflet. On the Tuesday after that a vehicle would come out of a depot and go down an alignment with the street shut at twenty to six, and a man would sign a check box with a date and an hour on it. The only other record in the world that any of it had happened would be four ruled lines on a table between two books, and a woman of eighty-four under a nail in a cold shop, asking a man in a coat to read her a column out loud, for as long as somebody kept standing there.
