# Chapter 158 — I Was Not In This District And I Cannot Say

Cliff Marner was one hundred and eleven days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and forty-one days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and forty-one days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at about ten past five on Sunday evening, the twenty-fifth of January, and paid the fare, and the two miles and eleven minutes were the first cost every day since the twenty-ninth of November and the sleep had been the second and the money the third and it had never once come in the other order.

He had been asked twice since the ninth of January to put a second name under his own on a piece of paper about this building, and he had said no twice, and the second time had cost him the only good hour of that week.

The paper is a one-page thing put round the flat doors on the ninth of January by a woman who runs a night rota for eleven households in two streets, and it asks for a second name against a first one for the four nights a week between December and March, and the reason it wants a second name is that in November a rota of eleven households found out that every one of them was out on the same evening, and a man who can be named in a room is a man who can be phoned.

"I put my name," the woman said, on the second Saturday, on the landing, with the paper in her hand. "I put it on the ninth and I did not tell you I had put it, and I have had to take it off twice since because there is nobody to put under it, and a rota with one name on it is not a rota."

"Then put two people on it who are not me."

"Two people who are not you are two people who cannot be on a road in Tidegate at seven in the evening and cannot carry anything and cannot hold a lamp, and I have got eleven households and about four people in this building who are on a Sunday," the woman said. "I have been at this since September. I have watched you go out of that door twice a week since the sixth of January and come back at eleven o'clock with your arm against your chest, and I am not asking you to stop, and I am asking you to be on a piece of paper, and you have said no twice and I have accepted it twice, and I would like it noted that I asked twice."

"It is noted," Elias Rook said.

"Then stop coming on Sundays," she said, and went in, and he did not stop, and there was nothing in that exchange available to anybody in any room, and it cost him a pound and a half and four hours of sleep.

Mara Okafor was in the front room at about a quarter past ten and did not say one word about the letter, and said one thing to him instead, quietly, by the kettle.

"That shoulder went on the thirteenth and you have been carrying things on it ever since, and there is nothing in this building that needs you to carry anything on a Sunday."

"I will get it looked at."

"You will not," Mara Okafor said. "You will get it looked at in March and you will tell me you have and I will know that you have not, and I am not going to chase you about it, and that is me being tired and it is not a treatment."

There is a float and a lock-box and a kettle and a shelf of things people bring back, and on the shelf there is a lamp that the building voted for on the fourth of January for eleven pounds fifty, and in Petar Lisk's book on a page after the twenty-second of November are four minutes and a resolution, and neither of them is on the board, and the board has three questions on it and no room left.

Inez Sato had a letter, and it had come on the Saturday, and she had read it twice and had not answered it, and she read it out in the front room at about half past ten at night because the room was awake anyway and there were twenty-eight people in it and the four dark hours were two hours away.

"A determination nine days old, three weeks of a schedule and a date on a letter," Inez Sato said, "and they have not asked the works, who printed it, and they have not asked the board, which drew the band the schedule came out of, and they have asked a hall on the other side of the water with a lock-box and a float of about nine pounds in it. So I am going to read their three points and then I am going to say the answer, and I have not told anybody what the answer was going to be before I read it, and that is not modesty, that is because I did not know until about four o'clock this afternoon."

She put the sheet down on the table on its face.

"One is true and it is the works' document and it says what they say it says. Two, I cannot do, and the reason is not a rule and there is no form for it and I am not writing one. I was not in that district. I have not set foot in that district since the sixth of September, and that was three weeks before there was anything in it to be in. Nobody in this room has been in that district except a man with a bad shoulder, and he is not the cooperative and I could not put this building's name to a sentence about a block range and I am not going to try.

"And the other half of two is the half that matters. The answer is that about two hundred and forty people in that district now know four nights and four streets, because a foreman typed four rows on one sheet and pinned it on his own gate on the thirty-first of December and a woman in a shop sold six copies of it, and that is true, and it is the only truth in this letter, and it is not the truth that anybody asked the question about, and if I write *residents were informed* on a piece of paper with this building's name at the top, then in about four years there is a document in a file that says this building stood behind that district in January, and we did not stand behind it, we stood two miles and eleven minutes away with our own light on."

Nobody in that room said she had done a brave thing and nobody said she had done a small thing either, and a man at the back said, "Then what do we send them," and she said, "Nothing, for two days, and then one page, and the one page is going to say that the cooperative has no knowledge of a district it does not hold a record of, and that it is glad to be told, and that is a lie, and I am going to send it anyway and I am going to sleep about four hours first."

---

Joon Park had the custody schedule in his coat and did not take it out, and said one thing at about a quarter to eleven, which was that twenty-two items had not become twenty-three in twenty weeks and that he had checked on Friday and again on Saturday, and that a letter in a building did not renumber anything.

Petar Lisk asked about the fare.

"How much is it," Petar said, "to go there and back."

"Two pounds seventy-five each way. Eleven minutes each way. Five pounds fifty a day."

Petar did that on his fingers for longer than it deserved and got it right on the third attempt, and then asked the next question, which is the one a nine-year-old actually asks.

"How many days."

"Twenty-four days," Elias Rook said. "Last riding day the Wednesday before Christmas. That is a hundred and thirty-two pounds and it finished in December and I have not started it again and I am not going to."

"Then why do you keep going."

Elias Rook sat on the arm of a chair with his right hand on his knee and his left hand in his coat, and had about eleven answers available, of which nine were about a district, one was about a woman in a room on the sixth of December, and one was true, and the true one was the one he did not say.

"Because the band is the only instrument in that district that has never once been wrong about the ground," he said, "and there is no one standing on it but the program, and a program is a thing that is paid."

Petar Lisk thought about that and found a hole in it, in the way that a nine-year-old finds a hole in it.

"You are not paid by it either," he said, and went to bed.

---

At about ten past midnight the four dark hours came and the woman of thirty-one who has the right to cut the power off a bar in the corridor had checked it and had not cut it, because it was not required tonight, and he went up with the notebook in his coat and the lamp in the other pocket that is his and not the building's, and the envelope to Nell in the front of the notebook, unsent, the reason four words long.

The letter wanted an answer by the twenty-eighth of January, which was four days after the district's four nights were over and a week before the works' booking ran out, and after that the board would have to come back for money, and the field office wanted a sentence about a district from a building on the other side of the water that had a float of about nine pounds in a lock-box.

There was one document in this city about those four nights that had been written by the people who lived in them, and it was four foot long and a strip of a shelf and it was on a nail nine inches above a counter in a shop on Cutbank Row, in about two hundred and forty hands, with a column headed for a name, and in three days somebody in an office was going to want a piece of it.

He was not going to ask for it. He was not going to suggest it, and he was not going to be the one who found a way to describe it in a room and get somebody to write the number down, and the reason was not a rule and had never been a rule, and it was a woman in a hall on the other side of the water who had said in December that the districts stay separate and that a hall does not become a second headquarters, and a woman in a kitchen in January who had refused a card on her own doorstep and had not wanted to be thanked for it.

There was a second Saturday in six days and nobody had written it down.
