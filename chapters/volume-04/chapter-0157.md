# Chapter 157 — The Only Lit Window On That Side Of The Row

Cliff Marner was one hundred and ten days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and forty days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and forty days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Saturday morning, the twenty-fourth of January, and paid the fare, and walked down to the bollard at half past ten, and it was four degrees and there were about fifteen people and there would be more by eleven.

The east side of Cutbank Row was standing empty from the second to the twenty-second and there was one light on in it, on the third floor, over the shop that sells door furniture, and it was on at half past ten in the morning.

The yard was open on a Saturday and had two lorries in it and nine working days to the fifth of February, and Nev Peart had said on the Friday that a Saturday with something on the gate was a Saturday he could get through and a Saturday with nothing on it was a Saturday he could not, and there were two documents and four biro words on that gate and one of the documents was going to come down on the fifth.

She read the four nights off the notice, the way she has read that notice four times every Saturday since it went up, and then she did Rennard, and then Murrow in a flatter voice, and then Marlow Street, and then Sallow Court last, and it took her about twenty-two minutes and nobody interrupted her and a man of about fifty held a child's hand for the whole of it.

Then she read the sheet.

She did not read it out. She read the first column off it, address by address, for about eleven minutes, at the same speed she read her own round, which is slower than you would think, and about nine of the fifteen people knew their own address being said and about six of them did not, and nobody said anything at all.

---

The woman of seventy-nine came at about eleven o'clock and had been on a bus, and had been on a bus at seventy-nine years of age on a Saturday in January because there was nobody in that house who could have brought her, and she was the woman who had come out of number thirty-one last Saturday to look at a nail from about four feet away and had gone back in, and her light had been off on the Sunday and on the Monday and on the Tuesday.

She had a piece of paper in her coat and she did not have it out on the corner and she did not have it out in the shop and she had it out on the pavement at about ten past eleven, at half a shop's distance, the way everybody in this district reads anything on that row.

"I want my name on it," she said, "and I want it spelled the way my husband spelled it, and I have not got anybody left who would know how to check."

Winifred Alagoa had a shopkeeper's pencil behind her ear and she took it out and looked at the woman of seventy-nine for about four seconds without saying anything at all.

"How is it spelled."

That is the whole of it, and it took about eleven seconds, and about four people on that pavement heard it and one of them said afterwards that he had been on that corner every Saturday for two months and had never once heard anybody ask anybody how their name was spelled.

"It is a long one," the woman said, "and it has a capital in the middle of it and the capital is wrong, and it has been wrong on every bit of paper about me since about 1988, and my husband spelled it the other way and he was not wrong, and there is nobody left."

"Then it goes in the way you say it and I am not going to argue with you about a capital in the middle of a word on a piece of paper in the cold," Alagoa said. "Say it once and I will write it and I will not say it out loud again, because there are nine people standing here and about a third of them are going to get it wrong when they repeat it, and it is yours."

She wrote it. She did not read it back and she did not count it and she put the pencil behind her ear again, and the woman of seventy-nine looked at it from about a foot away, and said, "That is the first one that has been right since about 1988," and went down the row to get her bus.

---

Alagoa did not count the fourth column on the Saturday and she said so before anybody asked her to, out loud, at the corner, with about nineteen people there.

"I am not counting it," she said. "I counted it on the Friday night a week ago and it was a hundred and ninety-one, and I told a pavement on the Saturday that it would be wrong by nine by Tuesday, and on the Tuesday it was wrong by more than nine by Tuesday, and I did not tell anybody by how much, and I have been asked twice this week whether I have counted it again and the answer is no, and it is no from now on."

Somebody at the back said, "Why not."

"Because a number is a thing to be right about," Alagoa said, "and I have been right about that number once and wrong about it once and there is no third thing it is for, and I am tired of it, and in three weeks I am going to be a woman standing in an empty row on a Saturday morning with a sheet of paper that has got two hundred and forty-one lines on it and not one of them mine."

---

The man from number nine Rennard was there. He had come at about half past ten and had said nothing for twenty minutes and then asked, at about eleven, with about nineteen people standing about, whether anybody thought the paper was going to be up in February.

The man from number thirteen Rennard was there too, with the dog, and had not said anything to anybody at all, and about half an hour later he asked a different question, which was whether the woman who had read the address off the sheet on Wednesday was the one who had written it, and about nine people looked at the pavement.

Nobody answered him for a while.

"I am not taking a vote on it," Alagoa said, "and I want that said first, before anybody starts counting hands, because I read doors on this corner and that is what I do. I do not take a vote. If I take a vote on it then this is a meeting, and a meeting has got to have a chair, and I am not going to be a chair on a Saturday morning in the cold, and the last thing that corner needs is a chair."

"You are the only one who can put it down."

"I can put it down any time I like, and I am not going to, and I have said that to nine people in a shop on Wednesday and I am saying it to nineteen on a corner, and I am not saying anything different today because there are more of you," Alagoa said. "So it stays up. And the man at nine Rennard is going to be at this corner next Saturday, and the woman at thirty-one is going to be at this corner next Saturday, and the four of us are going to say the same thing we said today, and it is going to be exactly as much of a decision as it was this morning, and nobody is going to be any better informed about it than they are now."

"What I would like," Alagoa said, at about a quarter to twelve, "is for somebody to write down the second Saturday. That is all. Not the arguments. Just the second Saturday. A notice that says *second Saturday, same time* is not a decision and it is not a form and it is not anybody's business, and if it is on a wall in seven days then it will still be on a wall on the fourth Saturday, and that is the whole of what I have got and I would like it now while I am asking."

Nobody wrote it down.

She rolled the sheet and put it under her arm and it was four foot long and a strip of shelf and it was going to be back on the nail above the counter at number two by one o'clock and it was a foot and a half above that counter, and on Thursday the fifth of February, if the fifth had gone the way the fifths go, there would be a man in a yard in this district with a piece of string in his hand.

At about ten past one, in the shop, Mrs Postridge took a hammer out from under the counter and a piece of batten about the length of her forearm and put the batten across the plasterboard and pulled the nail out of it and put the nail nine inches lower and hammered it back in through the batten.

Nobody had asked her to. There were two people in the shop.

"That is a foot and a half," Mrs Postridge said, "and I said on Saturday a fortnight ago that it was a foot and a half further than I would like, and I have thought about it every day since, and I have done it in a quiet minute on a Saturday on my own, and I am not going to be thanked for it, and if any of you say thank you I will put the price up."

"The bottom nine lines," Alagoa said, "are at a height where a person of seventy-nine has to take her glasses off and hold them at the end of her nose and go along it twice."

"Then it wanted doing and I have done it and it is nine inches and not twelve, because at twelve it is over the football coupons and I am not having that," Mrs Postridge said. "The fourth column is going to be readable by a person in this district who is under seventy and stands up, and it is not going to be readable by a person in a wheelchair, and I have known that since about 1998 and I have not got a batten for it, and I would like it noticed that I have said that out loud instead of doing the twelve inches and being pleased with myself."

Alagoa put the sheet back on the nail at about a quarter past one and it went up nine inches lower and the columns did not line up and the heading in capitals stayed at the top where it was, and two hundred and forty-one lines and a strip of shelf hung over a post office counter in Cutbank Row at a height a woman of sixty-two had chosen for herself in a quiet minute on a Saturday, and everybody in that shop knew that a nail nine inches lower is not a nail at knee height, and that it was still not reachable by a person who cannot stand up, and that was the other half of it and she had said so out loud about ten minutes earlier and nobody had disagreed.


He got the 11 at about half past two and sat on the far side of the cut for about an hour, and on the wall by the boatyard the man with the brush had nothing in his hand and had not had anything in his hand since Friday, and across the row from him there was one light on in an empty block on the third floor, and in twelve days the booking would be at an end and the board would have to come back for money, and in eight days the boatyard's owner was going to tell three men in a shed what she was going to do about a slip that had been dry since the twenty-second of November, and neither of those things was going to be on a nail in a shop, and both of them were going to happen.
