# Chapter 159 — Who Is The Man In This District In February

Cliff Marner was one hundred and twelve days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and forty-two days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and forty-two days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Monday morning, the twenty-sixth of January, and paid the fare, and there were eight working days to the fifth of February.

The yard had one lorry in it and the rest of the day work on twenty-two to thirty-two Rennard, internal, first floor, which is five days late and has been five days late since the eighth of January, and Nerys Hobbs was on Rennard at about ten with a clipboard she had not written on.

"I am going to start signing the day work," she said. "Not that sheet. A new one, with the date and the crew and the hour, and I am going to write the word *day* at the top of it in capitals so that nobody in February can look at it and think it was a night. That is the whole of what I have decided this week and it has taken me three weeks."

"You do not have to sign anything that has not got a night on it."

"No," Hobbs said. "That is why I have taken three weeks to decide to do it and it is going to be the last thing I change in this district and I would like it in a room while I am in it, because in a month somebody is going to find a piece of paper in this yard with the twenty-seventh of January on it and no night on it, and they are going to ask what it was, and the answer is going to be that I signed a day because nobody had ever signed one, and there was no form for that either and I did not look for one."

---

Nev Peart had the shift book on the bench with the band round the corner of it and did not open it, and had the accident form's string in his pocket where it has been since the eighth, and put it on the table at about half past eleven and then took it off the table again and put it back in his pocket.

"I am going to ask you a question and I want you to have had four days to have an answer and I do not think you have," Peart said, "and if you have got one I would rather you said it in a yard than in a room in the Meridian with a table between you."

"I have not got one."

"Then I am going to ask it anyway," Peart said, "because I am fifty-one and I have been in this yard nineteen years and in February I am going to have nothing in my hands, and the thing I am going to be is a man with nothing in his hands, and that is a thing a man in a coat from the other side of the water can either help with or make worse."

"Say the question."

"Five nights," Peart said, "you have been on this road for eight weeks and you have been on four of them and you have carried things for nine hours once and held a door frame for two hours once and counted door numbers for four minutes once, and not one of it is on a sheet of mine and that is my decision and I have never regretted it. In February, when this booking is over and the board either comes back or does not, somebody is going to ask a question in a room, and it is going to be the same question they asked in December and in January, and it is going to be *who is the man who speaks for this district.* And I want to know what I say."

Elias Rook looked at the bench for a while.

"I do not know," he said.

"No," Peart said. "I did not think you did, and I have had four days and you have had four days, and I want to tell you what I am going to say before you think about it any more, because you think about things."

"Tell me."

"I am going to say that this district has a foreman with a booking that ends on the fifth and a works' day sheet and four lines in a woman's hand, and that after the fifth the district has a woman on a corner with a sheet of paper and a shop with a nail in it, and that I have never in nineteen years been able to answer that question and neither has anybody in this building, and I am going to say that out loud in a room, and I am going to be asked who the district's person is, and I am going to say *there is not one, and there has not been one since the twenty-ninth of November.*"

He put the kettle on.

"A ladder wants an anchor," he said. "That is a trade word and I have used it about four thousand times and it means something at the bottom of a stair, and a wall gives you one for nothing and a floor does not. And a person is not a wall. I have watched men try to be a wall to a man in a coat and it does not hold and it is not honest work, and I would rather say a sentence in a room that I know is true than have somebody else in that room be able to say a truer one."

---

The woman at the counter at number six had the door open from twelve to four on a Monday and there was no queue, and Joon Park was at the end of the counter with the custody schedule in his coat and did not take it out, and said that a regional office had telephoned on the Friday about something on the twenty-eighth and that he had said the counter was open on Mondays and Fridays and that was all the information he had given them.

"That is the whole of the telephone call."

"That is the whole of the telephone call," Joon Park said, "and it took about two minutes, and about four seconds of it was them telling me the date and about a minute and a half of it was me saying that the counter is open on Mondays and Fridays, and neither of those two things is going to be in a record and I would like it written down here, in this room, by somebody, that I gave a counter's opening times to a regional office on a telephone on a Friday afternoon and did not give them anything else, and that twenty-two items have not become twenty-three in twenty weeks and I have checked on Friday and again this morning."

Nobody wrote it down, and he had not expected them to, and that was the arrangement.

---

There are three surfaces in this district where a piece of paper can go and not one of them will take this, and he walked all three in about an hour and a half on the Monday afternoon with a piece of paper in his coat that said *second Saturday · same time · the corner* in his own handwriting on the back of an envelope.

The gate of the works' yard will take a works' notice and the foreman who prints it. The nail above the counter at number two is for leaflets and the woman who owns it said so out loud on the Saturday, in front of about nine people, and she meant it. And the board's notice board in Civic buildings will take a submission and has a form and a closing time and takes about eleven working days, which puts the closing time on the ninth of February, which is nine days after the Saturday it would be about.

The trestle by the door of the church hall off Marden Row is the fourth one and it is not in this district and it belongs to a parents' group.

It was in the hall, folded against the wall where it lives, and it had had a four-foot sheet of unruled paper on it on the sixth of January at a parents' meeting and again on the fifteenth at seven minutes past seven in the evening, and it had not had anything on it since.

Marged Prosser was in the hall on the Monday at about half past three because the hall is cleaned on Mondays, and she is a chair of a parents' group and has the hall hire in a ledger at the back of it and has never once had a question asked of her about anything except the hire.

"Yes," Marged Prosser said. "The trestle goes to the group, and anything on the trestle is something the group put there, and I have been thinking about that since the fifteenth and I have got it down to a sentence and the sentence is this: the group will not put up a notice that it has not been asked to put up by the person who is answerable for it, and if nobody in this district is answerable for a Saturday morning, then the trestle is a table and not a noticeboard, and I am not going to have a thing going on a trestle that nobody will stand behind in a month."

"Who would be answerable for it."

"That is your question and I have thought about it for eleven days," Marged Prosser said. "It is not me, because I chair a parents' group and I have a hall hire ledger and no reason to be answerable for a street in January. It is not the works, and it is not a program, and I do not know the name of the woman with the sheet and I am not going to guess at it in a hall. And it is not a man in a coat, and I have said that out loud to you in a room and I would say it again, and there were two hundred people in here on the fifteenth who believed a man who was not wrong, and I sat beside a table for an hour and said nothing at all, and I have not slept properly since."

The trestle was folded against the wall and there was nothing on it.

---

He got the 11 at about ten past five and sat on the far side of the cut for about an hour and did not get on it, and worked out on the back of an envelope that there were three things he had come to this district for since the twenty-ninth of November and that he had got one of them.

In ten days the booking would be at an end. In ten days the accident form would come off that gate, because a foreman of fifty-one had decided on a Friday afternoon that a piece of paper about eleven injuries is not a leaflet and had said so out loud to a locked gate. In five days it would be the second Saturday, and about nineteen people had stood on a corner in the cold and been told the argument would be back the following week, and not one of them had written it down, because there is no form in this city for a Saturday morning, and because a notice that says *second Saturday, same time* is not a works' notice and a foreman cannot put it on his own gate, and a woman who sells post and newspapers has one nail above her counter and it is for leaflets and she has said so.

So there was a Saturday in six days that was going to happen, and about two hundred and forty lines on a nail that only said *second Saturday, same time* would have no surface anywhere in this district to be on, and a district that had spent four nights in January and a month of evenings in a church hall and a woman reading her own doors out loud in the cold had run out of walls, and had done it on purpose, and a woman in a hall on the other side of the water had said in a room in December, with five other people in it, that there was not going to be a second wall and that she would take the chalk off it herself, and he had been in that room and had not argued with her about it, and nobody has ever thanked him for agreeing with her, and he would not have wanted it to be a thing he was thanked for.
