# Chapter 156 — Eleven Names Read Out Loud In A Yard

Cliff Marner was one hundred and nine days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and thirty-nine days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and thirty-nine days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Friday morning, the twenty-third of January, and paid the fare, and the Friday at the depot was not his and had not been since the twenty-first of November, and R. Dunleavy had said the words *since the twenty-first of November* out loud to him at ten past six the previous morning, and had been right to say them in that order and no other.

The yard gate had three things on it. The night schedule of the thirty-first of December with four rows and four biro words under it. The works' accident form of the eighth of January with a piece of string through the corner and eleven names on it and a line under those in a different pen. And nothing on the other nail, which had been the other nail since Christmas.

There were nine working days to the fifth of February.

---

The boatyard had a meeting at two in the afternoon on the Friday and it was in the office that is really a shed, and eleven men were in it, and the owner was a woman of about fifty-four who had had the yard since her father died in 2038, and nobody in this district had ever heard of her and she had never been in a room with anybody from the works or the board or the program.

She said out loud, with ten men behind her, that the Cut had been down since the second week of November and that the slip at the north end had been dry since the twenty-second, and that a yard that cannot put a boat in in February cannot get a boat out in April, and that she had three men on the books on Friday and had had three men on the books on Friday since the twenty-second of November, and that on the first of February she was going to be honest with them in the same shed and would not know what she was going to say.

There is no version of that meeting in which anybody in this district is told about it by anybody in this district, and the boatyard man on the wall who has been painting doors since the twenty-second of November was not in it, and was not asked, and did not want to be, and said so.

"You held a chair up on Saturday," Elias Rook said, on the wall, at about half past ten. "For a man in a boatyard who has had no work since November."

"I held a chair because there were two people on that pavement who could not reach a nail and a man of nearly seventy could," the man said, and went on painting. "I am not going to be one of the two hundred and forty people on that wall. I have not got a house in this district. I have got a van and a bed in a house in Weaver Street and a yard that is shut, and I held a chair, and I would like it left at that, and you are the fifth person this week who has wanted to give me something for it."

"They are going to say it on Monday."

"They have been saying it since Saturday," the man said. "I get two or three a day and I have had about nine this week and not one of them has offered me a day's work, and I have not asked any of them for anything, and I would like you to understand that this is what a closed yard looks like on a street, and it looks exactly like a man who is being given something."

He put the brush down and looked at the door for a while.

"There is one that I will take," he said. "If that woman upstairs ever wants the door doing properly, and not three times with a tin of whatever the shop has left her, she has a man who will do it for the price of the paint and not for anything else, and I will not want to be thanked for it, and I will not be able to stop her thanking me. That is the whole of what I have got and it is not enough to keep a yard open and I am not going to pretend it is."

---

At about ten past three on the Friday a woman came into the survey office at the works and asked whether she could have the list of the eleven.

She was about forty-three and she had a cardigan on over a coat and she had come eleven minutes and she was not angry, and she said, before anybody had offered her anything, that she had heard there was a piece of paper on the gate with eleven names on it, and that she was one of the eleven, and that she had been told about it on a Saturday morning in the second week of January by a man in a works jacket who had said *you are on it up there* and pointed at a gate.

"It has been up since the eighth," Nev Peart said.

"I know it has been up since the eighth. I have walked past that gate every day for a fortnight and I cannot read it in the rain, and I have got a bad chest and I am not going up on a chair."

Peart put his coat on, took the form off the gate, brought it in, and stood in the middle of the survey office with it in his hand and read the eleven names out loud in his own voice, once, at about ten past three on a Friday afternoon, to four people and a woman in a cardigan, at the speed a foreman reads a list and not any slower than that.

He put it back on the gate afterwards, in the rain, with the string through the same corner.

"That is the whole of what is on that piece of paper," he said. "There is a date at the top and a place and eleven names, and there is a line at the foot that says how many went home, and the eleven are in my capitals because I filled it in at ten past two on a Thursday with one pen, and that is the worst handwriting anybody has ever produced and it is mine. You are not getting a copy, because a copy of that form is a piece of paper with eleven names on it in somebody's bag, and I have seen what that does, and I am not doing it in January."

"Then how am I supposed to know that I am on it."

"You have just had it read to you by the man who wrote it, in a building, with four people in it, and that happened about a minute ago," Peart said, "and there is no piece of paper anywhere that says you were told and there never will be, and if you want to be told again you come in here on a Friday and you stand where you are standing and I will read it again, and that is the arrangement and it has no form in it and I have thought about that for a fortnight and I would do it this morning."

Nobody in that room said thank you, and the woman in the cardigan said, "Then I will come on Friday," and meant it, and came on the fifth of February, which was the last working day of the booking, and was read to again.

At about four o'clock the woman of sixty-one from number nineteen Rennard came to the gate and read the form herself, on her own, standing in the rain with her coat open, and nobody from the works had told her it was there and nobody from the works knew that she was coming, and she read all eleven and then stood for about a minute and then said, "Right," and went home.

That is the whole of what a district does with a record, and it is not on a form, and there is no provision anywhere in this city for the day a person stands in the rain at a gate and reads eleven names and then says one word and goes home, and nobody in that yard watched her do it except a man in a coat who was on the far side of the cut and happened to be looking at the gate.

---

The other nail above the counter at number two is a leaflet nail, and it has been one since 2014, and there have been four things on it since the beginning of December and every one of them has come from somebody who wants something done.

"One for a hearing about the works," Mrs Postridge said. "One for a consultation about the alignment, and that is a leaflet with a form on the back. One for a charity. And one that came on the ninth with a photograph of a woman on it, and I put it on the nail, and it took it four seconds, and I have thought about those four seconds every day since and I would like it recorded that I put it up."

"Why did you put it up."

"Because it is a leaflet nail and there was nothing else for it to be," Mrs Postridge said. "And because I have run this counter for twenty-nine years and in twenty-nine years the only thing a nail on a public wall is good for is a person who wants to tell you something, and I have never yet known which of them was lying, and I have never once had a way of finding out, and that is the arrangement, and I do not get to change the arrangement because I have had a difficult week. The paper above the counter is not a leaflet. It is a piece of paper in about two hundred and forty hands. I would like somebody to be able to tell me the difference between those two things one day, and until then I am going to keep them a foot and a half apart."

---

They shut at four on the Friday and the office cleared out and the barrier came down and there was nothing on that gate but the notice, the four biro words under it, and the form, and the other nail still empty.

"That form is coming down," Peart said, at about ten past four, to nobody, to the gate. "Not this week. On the last working day of this booking, because after that I do not have a booking, and after that there is no reason on that gate for a foreman's accident form to be on it, and I have not been asked to take it down by anybody and I have been waiting since the eighth for somebody to ask me to and nobody has and I would have taken it down on the Wednesday if they had."

"And if nobody asks you in four weeks."

"Then it comes down on the fifth of February because it is the last day of a booking I am in the middle of," Peart said, "and I would like it noted that a piece of paper about eleven injuries coming off a gate is not a leaflet, and that there is a form in this building for leaflets and there is no form in this building for that, and that has been the arrangement for nineteen years."

Elias Rook got the 11 at about ten past five and sat on the far side of the cut with his right forearm across his knee, and there were three documents in this district, on a gate, on a piece of string and on a nail, and two empty nails, and one of the three was going to come down in thirteen days on a date that had been set by a foreman and not by anybody else, and nobody in the district had voted on it and nobody was going to.
