# Chapter 166 — A Yard With A Booking And No Work In It

Cliff Marner was one hundred and nineteen days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and forty-nine days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and forty-nine days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Monday morning, the second of February 2043, and paid the fare, and there were three working days to the fifth of February, and the yard had a booking and no work in it, and had had no work in it since about half past three on Friday afternoon.

Nerys Hobbs had told him that at seven in the morning in about nine words, on the telephone, from a building two miles and eleven minutes away, and had not said anything else, because she had said in a yard on Friday afternoon that there is no form in this city for a woman of forty-seven with nothing to sign and she had not looked for one.

---

Nev Peart had the kettle on the bench at about a quarter to ten and did not put it into a cup.

"There is a thing I have to say to this yard," he said, "and I am going to say it once, now, while all five of you are stood here, and then I am going to make the tea, and I am not going to say it again today, and if any of you want to ask me about it afterwards I will answer you."

Nobody sat down.

"The day work came off Rennard on Friday at half past three and that was the last of it. There is nothing on for this week. There is nothing on for next week. The booking runs to Thursday the fifth, and on the sixth of February I do not have a booking, and the board has to come back for money, and in my experience it does not come back in the same year it has already spent it, and I said that out loud to a locked gate on Sunday the eighteenth of January and I am saying it again to five men because you are the five men and the gate is not."

Doy said, "Right."

"It is not right," Peart said. "It is what there is. I want the difference in the room said out loud so that nobody in this yard spends the rest of the day thinking I have said something I have not."

---

Ted Crees, who was sixty-three and who had been on that crew since before the collapse and who had a bad hip and had said in January that he was not going to put a load on a ladder on a floor, took his cap off.

"Does the notice come down," he said.

Peart had his hand in his pocket and it came out with the string in it, a piece of white string about a foot long that had been round the corner of a works' accident form since the eighth of January.

"It comes down on Thursday the fifth," he said, "at the end of the day, and it is mine to take down and I have not been asked to by anybody and I have been waiting since the eighth of January for somebody to ask me to, and nobody has, and I would have taken it down on the Wednesday if they had."

"You never said."

"I said it out loud to a locked gate on a Friday afternoon in January," Peart said, "and there was nobody there, and I have been carrying that decision about for a fortnight with nobody in it, and I have just told five men instead, and that is what has happened, and I am not going to stand here and pretend to you that I decided it in a room. I decided it in a yard with my hands in my pockets and then I had to say it out loud to somebody, and it turned out the somebody had to be you."

Ted Crees put his cap back on.

---

"You will have worked out," Peart said, "that in about four years somebody is going to stand in a room and ask me what my job is in this district, and I have got an answer ready and I have had it ready since the twenty-sixth of January and I have said it to one man in a yard and not to five."

"Go on," said Doy.

"There is not one," Peart said, "and there has not been one since the twenty-ninth of November. I have never in nineteen years been able to answer that question and neither has anybody in this building, and I am going to say it out loud in a room when I am asked, and I am not going to have anybody come at me afterwards and be able to say a truer one."

Nobody in that yard said anything for about four seconds, and then Doy said, "You have just said it in a yard to five men."

"I know exactly what I have done," Peart said. "I made that sentence on the Monday for a room in February. It is now the Monday. And I would rather it had gone in a room and I am not going to pretend to you that it has, and I am telling you that I have lost control of when it happens about ten days, and the only person in this district that has anything to do with that is a man who comes on a bus, and he is not going to be able to put it back either."

---

He made the tea at about ten past ten and gave it out in five mugs on the bench, and the barrier was up and the terminal was off and the gate outside had a notice in a frame in it with four biro words under it, and the other nail had been empty since Christmas and was going to be empty until the fifth.

"I am not shutting this yard," Peart said, at about a quarter past ten, to nobody in particular and to everybody. "There is a difference between a yard with no work in it and a yard that is shut, and the difference is that a shut yard has a padlock on it and a man can look at the gate and know there is nothing coming, and I have got a booking until Thursday and I am going to be in this building every working day until the fifth, and if the board comes back with money then this yard is the one that is open, and if it does not then I will be here on the Friday anyway with nothing to do, and I would rather be here with nothing to do than be somewhere else with nothing to do, because anybody in this district can come and find me."

Nerys Hobbs was at the end of the bench with her coat on and a clipboard she had not written on.

"There is no form for that either," she said. "A foreman keeping a yard open is not in this district's paperwork anywhere. It is not a decision. It is a man deciding to be in a building, and the only record of it will be that he was, and there is nobody to read that but him."

"Then it will be me," Peart said, "and I have been reading my own book in this yard for nineteen years and I have never once needed anybody to check, and I am not going to start."

---

Elias Rook had been in that yard for about an hour and a half and had said almost nothing in it, and he had one thing left to say and he said it at the gate at about ten to eleven, to a man of fifty-five with a gray dog who was standing at the wire with a dog that had a bad back and could not get out of the way of a lorry.

"Mr Pellow struck his own address out of that sheet on Saturday morning in a shop with a shopkeeper's pencil, in his own hand, and put his own name in the fourth column."

"I heard," Roy Pellow said. "Nine people told me by about eleven o'clock and about four of them were pleased about it and I would like you to understand what it looks like from the other end of a pavement. Six months ago I would have given a man a card and gone in. Yesterday I was the ninth person to tell a woman with a shopkeeper's pencil what to do with her own wall, and I did not have to say anything at all, and I am going to have to live in that."

"I did not tell her to do it," Elias Rook said, and then stopped, because that was not a defense of anything and it sounded like one.

"No," Pellow said. "I know. That is why it worked. If you had said a word I would have taken it off again on Monday."
