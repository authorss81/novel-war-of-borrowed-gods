# Chapter 163 — Four Strips Of Tape

Cliff Marner was one hundred and sixteen days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and forty-six days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and forty-six days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Friday morning, the thirtieth of January 2043, and paid the fare, and there were five working days to the fifth of February, and the counter at number six was open and would shut at half past four, and the four signed ruled lines were on the table where they had been since the Tuesday before last, and about eleven people had walked in and looked at them and nobody had signed for them and nobody had taken them away, which is what a table is for.

The day work finished at about half past three that afternoon. Twenty-two to thirty-two Rennard, internal, first floor, five men, five days late since the eighth of January, and after Friday the thirtieth there was nothing left in that booking except two days of a booking with nothing in it.

---

The extension went on on the twentieth of January, on purpose, wrong, by a woman with a shopkeeper's pencil, so that if it were done properly it would be another four foot sheet and there would be a foot and a half between that and anybody's hand.

What nobody on that row had worked out in the eleven days since is what a wrong extension does to a column.

On the white paper the four columns are four columns, and the fourth of them has a heading over it in capitals underlined twice, and the heading is a name. On the brown strip the first column starts about an inch and a half to the left of the first column above it and the fourth is worse than that, and by the time somebody had been writing lines in the strip for a week, the fourth column had run off the right-hand edge of the strip and onto the plasterboard, nine inches to the right of the sheet, at whatever height the person writing it could reach.

There were fourteen of them, in biro, on the plasterboard, in about eleven hands, going back eleven days.

*9 MURROW · 17th · came about half nine*

*21 RENNARD · 19th · nobody at the door*

There is no heading over either of those. There is nothing over any of the fourteen that says what they are, and there is no way of telling which line of the strip a name belongs to except by counting down from the top of the sheet in a cold shop with the light behind your head, and the counting is done by a person, and the person has to be the person who put the paper up.

Mrs Postridge found them at about eleven on the Friday morning with about a yard of her counter moved out of the way, and she did not count them out loud, and she did not tell anybody until about twenty past eleven when there were four people in the shop.

"I have known about those since the twenty-second," she said, "and I have not said anything about them, and I am saying it now because the woman at seventeen Rennard was in here on Wednesday telling a man in a coat that somebody has to read her a column. I have thought about that for two days and I have got to the end of what I can sit on."

"Tell them what," Elias Rook said.

"That there are fourteen names on the plasterboard about nine inches to the right of that sheet, not under anything and not in line with anything," Mrs Postridge said. "That is all of it. I have not read them. I am sixty-two and I am not getting on a chair and I am not going to stand under a wall and read fourteen names off a wall to check whether they are in the right order, and neither is anybody else in this district. And in about four years there is going to be a person in an office who is going to be quite certain that the fourth column on that sheet is complete, and it is not, and there is no document anywhere in this city that says it is not."

Nobody in that shop said anything for about four seconds, which on a Friday morning in Cutbank Row is a long time.

"I am not taking it down," Winifred Alagoa said, from the back of the shop, before anybody had asked her. "Mrs Postridge, you are not taking it down and I am not letting you, and you fitted that batten yourself on a Saturday and you will not be thanked for it, and I am not having a woman of eighty-four be the reason a nail comes out of my wall."

"Nobody is taking it down," Mrs Postridge said. "I am not going anywhere near it. What I am going to do is this, and I am saying it in a shop with four people in it because that is where I say things, and I am saying it to a man in a coat because he is the only person in this district who ever turns up when I say one."

She put the pencil behind her ear.

"Anybody who comes in here and wants the fourth column read to them, I will read it to them. That is all of it, and I want it understood what it is not. It is not a rota. I am not a rota. There is no list, there is no name at the top of anything, nobody has asked me, and I have not said yes to anybody. I am sixty-two and I run this counter and I have run it for twenty-nine years, and in twenty-nine years the only thing anybody has come to this counter for is to be sold something or to be told what they were owed. And if I am in here on a Saturday and somebody comes in, I will read it, and if I am not in here then they do not get it read, and that is the arrangement. It is not a promise. It is not a service. And I would like it noticed that I have said it in a shop and not in a room."

Elias Rook wrote it down afterwards, on the far side of the cut, with his right forearm across the page, and did not read it back in the shop, and nobody signed it and nobody took it away.

Alagoa was standing at the end of the counter with the pencil in her hand and she had said nothing at all.

"You have just done the thing I did on Tuesday," she said.

"I have not done anything."

"You have made yourself the woman who stands there," Alagoa said. "You did it in a shop with four people in it and you did not choose the shop and you did not choose the four people, and that is the whole of how I did it. I have not slept since Tuesday and I am not going to sleep this weekend either, and I am not going to tell you that this is a consolation, because it is not one. You did it because a woman of eighty-four said a sentence in here on Wednesday. I did it because a man of forty-one said nine words in here on the twenty-first and I did not want them repeated. Neither of us did it for the district. It happened to us."

"Four days to Saturday," Elias Rook said.

"Saturday is not a fix for any of it," Alagoa said. "Saturday is about twenty people standing in the cold and me reading the first column out loud. On Saturday nobody will be standing in this shop reading the fourth column to anybody unless somebody decides to stand here, and there is no reason on this earth why one of them should, and I am not going to tell them why, and if you tell them why then it is a program, and I have had a piece of paper off one of those in this district in January, and I would like to stand in a shop on a Saturday morning on my own account."

---

At about half past three the last of the day work came off Rennard Street and went up the road in one vehicle with the tip down and the wheels off the soft ground, and five men went home at about four, and Nerys Hobbs signed the day sheet for the Friday with the date and the hour and the crew on it in her own hand and put it in the tray, and then stood looking at the tray for about a minute.

"Saturday," she said. "Sunday. Monday. Tuesday. Wednesday. Thursday. That is six days of a booking with nothing on them. The booking ends on the fifth. After the fifth there is not a booking. And I have been signing things in this district since the sixth of September and there is no form anywhere in it for what I do on Friday the sixth."

"Haven't you got one," Elias Rook said, and he knew the answer before he asked it, and asked it anyway.

"There is no form for a woman of forty-seven with nothing to sign," Hobbs said. "I did not look for one. I have never looked for one. That is the whole of my method in this district and I have never had to say it out loud before today, and I have said it out loud because you are standing in this yard and because there is nobody else in it on a Friday afternoon at half past four."

---

He got the 11 at about ten past five and sat on the far side of the cut for an hour with his right forearm across the page and fourteen names on a wall in his head that were under no heading at all.

In four days it would be the second Saturday, and about twenty people would stand on a corner at half past ten because a woman had written two lines in pencil at the top of her own sheet and had not slept since, and in a shop on that corner a woman of sixty-two had just said out loud in front of four people that she would read a column to anybody who asked her, and had also said out loud that she was not a rota and had no list and no name at the top of anything, and the two of those sentences had been in the same mouth in the same minute, and both of them were true, and the only thing that joined them was that a woman of eighty-four had said a sentence in a shop on a Wednesday afternoon, and nobody in this district had asked her to say it, and nobody was going to.
