# Chapter 152 — The Fourth Of The Four Nights

Cliff Marner was one hundred and five days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and thirty-five days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and thirty-five days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at half past four on Monday afternoon, the nineteenth of January, and paid the fare, and there was a lamp in his coat and a bad shoulder that had been bad for six days and was the better for a Tuesday depot and worse for a night.

Fifteen to twenty-one Rennard, two to twelve Cutbank Row, crew of four. That is on the fourth row of a notice behind glass and it has been there since the thirty-first of December, and nobody in this district took it down and nobody was going to.

Peart's five conditions were still on the board in his own hand and Hobbs had read them on Friday and had changed no word of them, and the second one was the driver's and had never been the driver's to give and had never been argued about since the eighth of January.

---

The low-loader was not coming down Rennard and had not been since the tenth, and everything that came off that stretch came off it on four backs, and there was a man of sixty-three on the crew and Peart had told him on the Monday which parts he was carrying and had told the other three to carry the rest.

Elias Rook stood on the east side, where a foreman had put him in December, with a lamp in his coat he had not taken out, and the thread came on under the length of that street at about half past six and stayed on for nine hours and gave him nothing at all, which is what it had done every time since the first of January, and which was by then the only fact about it he was entitled to.

At about a quarter past seven they got to seventeen.

The woman opened the door before the second knock, which she does, and she was eighty-four or close to it and she had her coat on and her bag packed and had been standing in her own front room in her coat since about half past four.

"Am I the first," she said.

"You are the first on this stretch," Doy said, "and I am not going to tell you that you are the first tonight, because there is another block after this one and I have not been down that yet."

"I have read it on a nail," she said. "There is a nail in a shop on Cutbank Row with a sheet of paper on it that is four foot long, and it says a date and a street, and there is not one door number on it anywhere, and I have been on this row forty-one years and I worked out on the thirty-first of December which night my street was on by counting the weeks, and I have had my bag packed since half past four this afternoon and I would like you to know that I worked it out and I would like it noticed that nobody told me."

"Nobody told you because I did not print it," Peart said, from the road. "That is my decision and I made it in about nine seconds on the thirty-first of December and I have made it once and I will make it again, and you are the reason I made it, and I would rather you were angry with me than written down."

She looked at him for a second and then went back inside and left the door open, which is what a person does when they have been told the truth and have not got anything else to say about it.

---

They were on Rennard for most of the night. It went at the speed of a crew with a ladder and a soft road it was not allowed to drive a lorry down, and the low side of that street is where the ground comes off the bank of the Cut and goes flat, and there was no vehicle of any kind on it at any hour from seven in the evening until twenty to seven in the morning, and that had not been a thing anybody had thought about on the eighth of January and had been the whole of the design ever since.

At about half past nine they had a wardrobe down a stair at fifteen with the ladder on the landing and its foot against nothing at all, because a landing is a floor and a floor is not a wall, and a man of sixty-three got to the top of it and then stopped and stood there for about ten seconds before he would put his weight on it.

"Two men on a ladder is a load on a rope," he said. "One man on a ladder is a man looking for a place to fall, and there is no wall on that landing and there is not going to be one, and the floor under that foot is a floor and it is going to stay a floor."

There is a way of holding a ladder foot that involves a man's whole body and about eleven minutes, and Elias Rook did it with his right hand and the flat of his right forearm under the rail and his shoulder against the wall on the other side of the stairwell, and his left hand was in his coat doing nothing, which was its job, and the wardrobe came down it at four inches a second and the wall took the lot of it.

"Nothing on that ladder but the man and the thing he is moving," the man said, afterwards, to nobody. "I have been doing this nineteen years and I do not have a word for what that is, and I would like it noted that I did not have one."

At about ten o'clock they came to nineteen.

It took four knocks and about two minutes, and nothing happened, and Elias Rook was standing on the east side of the road with a lamp in his coat and a woman of about sixty watching him from about ten feet away through a front-room window, not moving, with her hand on the back of a chair.

The door came in on the fourth knock and she was in the frame in a coat over her nightdress with her hair up and a lamp of her own in her left hand, and she said, to Doy, "I know it's Monday. I read it on the nail on Saturday. I did not come down on Saturday and I am not going to tell anybody why and there is nowhere on that paper to write it."

"Then we will not ask," Doy said.

"You are the fourth man that has said something like that to me since Saturday," she said, "and one of them was a man with a right arm that does not work who has been standing on this road on all four of the nights since the sixth of January and has not come up my path once, and if he is standing out there I would like him to know that it was noticed and that it is all right."

Elias Rook said that he was standing out there and that it was all right.

She looked at him for a moment longer than he wanted and went in, and they took the room in eleven minutes, and the lamp on the wall stayed on until they had the last thing through the door.

He did not ask her about Saturday night. He had a sentence ready about it at about half past nine and he did not say it, because the sentence was for him and not for her, and because there is no column on a piece of paper for a woman to say why she was in a house in the dark with a stair in it, and because a man who asks that question on a Monday night at ten o'clock has decided that being in the house was a thing that needed explaining.

---

They came off Rennard at about half past one and were on the lower half of the east side of Cutbank Row by about two, and the lorry came up the row behind them with the tip down and the wheels off the curb, and the shop and the skip and every door on that stretch took about three hours and the crew did not hurry any part of it.

At about twenty past three he was standing at the north end of that block outside number twelve with a cone in his right hand, and the flat over the shop that sells door furniture is at the top of the row, past the end of the block and about forty yards up, and the fanlight over that door had a light in it.

"That yours," Doy said, coming past with the ladder.

"No."

"Then it is not on the block," Doy said, "and I have got no card for it and I am not walking up to it, and if that is a woman in there on her own then she has got a shop and a boiler and a lock, and there is a man on this road with a lamp in his coat who is not going to be the one who decides otherwise."

"That is correct," Elias Rook said.

He stood at number twelve for about four more minutes and did not go up, and the light stayed on, and there was nothing in the air on that street and nothing in his hands, and in the morning he was not going to find out who was in it and he was never going to ask, and the cost of that was going to be a thing he carried for the rest of the winter and never once put anywhere.

---

At about half past five the crew walked Rennard and the lower Cutbank together, two men side by side, and at about ten to seven the barriers came down and the vehicle went out with the lamps in the rack and the ladder on it and nothing had gone over the soft road at any hour in ten hours and five minutes, and nobody was hurt.

The woman of eighty-four came out of seventeen in her coat with her bag at her own front door and stood in the road and watched four men and a ladder go past her, and there was no furniture to watch go past, and she had known that on Saturday afternoon and had still packed the bag at half past four.

Nerys Hobbs was in the doorway of the survey office with the sheet on the trestle and three of the four ruled lines on it signed and the fourth one empty, and she had her pen in her hand and she did not put it in her pocket.

"I sign it in the morning," she said. "With the barriers down and the hour on it, and it is the fourth of four and after this morning there is no line on that sheet, and there is no form anywhere in this district for what happens on the fifth of February, and I am not going to write one, and neither is anybody else in this building."

Elias Rook got the 11 at about ten past seven in the morning and sat on the far side of the cut for half an hour with his right arm against his chest.

There were four nights in a booking of twenty-eight and all four of them had been walked and three of the four lines were signed and the fourth would be signed at about half past six that morning, and in about seventeen days the board would have to come back for money, and in Nerys Hobbs's experience it did not come back in the same year it had already spent it.
