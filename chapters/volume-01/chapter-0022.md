# Chapter 22 — A Ward With a Name On It

The Office truck was gone from Ash Street at eight in the morning, and Teodor stood in the road watching the space where it had been as though it were a bird that had flown out of a tree he had been shouting at.

“It was there four days and now it is not there,” he said. “Somebody came at half six and drove it away. I did not ask anybody to drive it away. I asked.”

“It was moved for a gas main on the Meridian,” Inez said, from the gate. “Not your street. I asked for it on Friday and did not tell you, and I am telling you now, and if you want to complain about the not-telling I will take that in writing.”

“I do want to complain about the not-telling.”

“Good. Tuesday,” Inez said, and went inside, and Teodor watched her go with the expression of a man who had got exactly what he asked for and no idea whether he had wanted it.

---

Everything else about Saturday was money, and the money had a shape, and the shape was a shed behind a transit depot and a man called Denny Prokop with a card table and eleven spools of cable on it.

He was perhaps sixty, sunburned, with a nose that had been broken and reset by somebody who had opinions. He was extremely good-humored and he did not once pretend to be a better man than he was.

“Seam-rated,” he said, before Elias had the money out, before anybody had said what they wanted or how much. “Two hundred and forty meters, four-core, shielded, jacketed for wet. I will tell you the truth about it, because you have all got the look of people who will find out and be disappointed in me.”

“Go on,” Inez said.

“It is rated for a static seam field at fifteen meters, unenergized, for four hours. It is not rated for what you have. There is no cable in this city rated for what you have, because nobody knows what that is, and the man who prints *seam-rated* on a spool is me, and I print it because it is the only word that makes a buyer stop walking.” He rolled a spool with one finger. “It is the best cable I have. And if I told you it was made for the thing you are doing, you would believe me, and in two years you would find out and think I had lied to you. I would rather be expensive than cheap.”

“How much,” Inez said.

“There is a number for a cooperative and it is not the number on the card. Cut to length and delivered, eight thousand eight hundred. The card says eleven thousand one hundred. The two thousand three hundred is that I am standing here on a Saturday and my own yard has two hundred people in it who are not getting anything this month, and because a woman I know ran your laundrette's electric for six days without asking anybody and my whole street talked about it for a week.”

Ru Sabar, who had come down to the gate without being asked, said, “It was six days and not nine, and I would like the number correct this time.”

“It is six days. I was told nine by a man who likes you. Eight thousand eight hundred.”

The whole transaction took about a quarter of an hour. Inez paid it out of the cooperative's fund in front of four witnesses and made him initial the receipt twice, once for the sum and once for the rating, and Prokop laughed at the second one and signed both. Then he said, at the gate, to nobody in particular, “There is a man on the west bank who sells cable at half that.” Inez said, “I know.” He said, “His is a different length and a different year, and I have seen the jacket on a length of it, and you know that too.” Inez said, “I know,” and he got in his van.

---

What they had by ten o'clock was this: two hundred and forty meters of good cable bought at a price that included somebody else's rent; batteries; two anchor lamps; a wooden frame with four panels of seam glass that Ru Sabar had made out of a door and a grain store window; two mattresses; a cylinder; a records desk Petar Lisk had carried in himself and would not let be unloaded anywhere but the corner with the least traffic; and a hole in the money of one thousand one hundred.

“What is the hole,” Elias said, because somebody had to ask and it was his job to ask questions in a room.

“The hole is the wall,” Inez said. She had it on the board already. “The wet end of this hall is where the twenty-three are and the dry end is where the records are, and the records are a table under a radiator with a leg propped with a folded case sheet since Monday. If a fire starts in here the first thing to go is a folder and the second thing to go is our ability to argue with anybody.”

“How much is a wall.”

“Eleven hundred. And a door, and a lock, and somebody who can build it, and the somebody is my cousin, and my cousin is not available, and the second somebody is a man in the Third Ward who takes cash and does not ask what it is for and is not good.” She looked at the board for a while. “That is what the ward costs. Not batteries. A wall, so the paper is a room and not a table under a radiator. And the wall is a quarter of what this building needs to be a building, which is four thousand four hundred, and I have the other three thousand three hundred written down in a list you would not enjoy reading.”

---

What happened next was that Ru Sabar said the thing out loud that everybody else had been thinking since Wednesday, and she said it while she was coiling cable, which is how she said everything that mattered.

“You have been treating that corridor like a wound for six days,” she said. “Every plan in this building has been a plan about not going near a place that hurt somebody. It is damp concrete with a strip light and a door at the end, it is not the tunnel, and it is not the door that changed. I have walked down it twice a day for eleven years with a laundry trolley. I know where the floor dips and I know which light is out.”

Nobody had said anything for a few seconds.

“Write it down,” Elias said.

“Do not write *her* down,” Inez said. “Write the corridor down. Ru Sabar is not infrastructure.”

Elias wrote it down.

---

The access instrument came out to the gate at eleven forty, and it came because of Hollis Vane's four findings, which is a sentence nobody expected to be able to say.

It was issued under the standing designation's own survey clause, which has been in force since 2034 and which nobody has ever had cause to use, and it was signed by **Ilse Brauer**, the archive floor's supervisor at the Spine depot, who would turn up again on Monday. She read the whole of it aloud before she handed it over, which told Elias she had been made to.

“Ninety minutes. Twelve hundred to one forty-five. Non-invasive. No tool on the structure, no fix, no shoring, no line. A named person of the lower service run walks it, a second person witnesses and signs, a third person from this office is present the whole way with a camera. You photograph, you measure, and everything you produce goes into the node with the rest of your data tonight, unedited, and you do not get to hold the only copy of it.”

“Why,” Elias said. “Why now, after four days of nothing.”

“Because your archivist has written that this office cannot presently show the moment at which a classification was decided,” Brauer said, “and a board is going to be asked this afternoon to accept a cause or admit it has none, and the ground that cause would be about is a ground with a route across it that belongs to no current survey and that nobody in this building has ever walked, including me, including the man who drew it in 2029.” She folded her hands. “I am refusing to have a survey imposed on a structure that has never been photographed by the party imposing it. If that corridor is on your paper and not on mine, then any finding this afternoon about the ground is a finding by a team that is its own only baseline. And I am issuing this at eleven forty rather than after the board, because a survey the board has already heard of is a survey, and a survey it hears about for the first time in the afternoon is an imposition, and I would like this one on the minutes as the first.”

Elias did not want to give the data up. He felt the wanting arrive before he had made an argument for it, and he had almost made one.

Tomas got there first.

“Do not refuse it,” Tomas said. “Elias. Listen to me before you say the thing you are about to say. That corridor has been off the paper for six days. There is no record of it anywhere except twenty-three people's heads, one old man's memory of a floor dip, and a page in a notebook in your coat. If you refuse a paper copy, then on Monday you stand in front of a board and argue about a space that officially does not exist, with no instrument and no witness, and every person in this hall hears you get told it is not there.”

“It goes in the node.”

“It goes in the node *and* it stays on our paper,” Tomas said. “They do not get the only copy. They get the same copy everybody else gets, which is the arrangement you spent Wednesday negotiating and which I supported. You are about to confuse giving a copy away with losing a copy, and you have confused it before, and it cost a door.”

“All right,” Elias said. “Give Brauer the same copy. On the node, with the keeper's name on it, in ink, not in a form field.”

Brauer looked at him for a second. “There is no keeper field.”

“Then write it in the remarks. Remarks are not checked.”

They got it in the remarks. Brauer did it herself and initialed it twice, and Joon photographed the initialed page before they left the gate, which is the single most valuable thing anybody did all day.

---

The lower service run went down off the surface approach past the old fuel line and came up out of the ground at a loading lip behind the west apron relay, and it was exactly as dull as it had always been: a run of damp concrete, emergency strip at ankle height, a floor that dipped about nine meters in for storm runoff, an inspection niche at twelve, a painted band at shoulder height that had been a different color every time the city repainted it.

And at the end of it, thirty-one meters from the lip, the narrow leaf in the bowed frame, leaning about six degrees, with its bright scrape mark and its two handles at two heights.

Nobody went near the door. That had not been on offer and nobody had asked.

**Emil Baros** walked it, because it was agreed in four minutes that if a route was going back on the paper then somebody from the actual event should be the name next to it, and Emil Baros had come out of the alcove with a child's school coat in his arms and had lost four names out of his head on the first Sunday and had stopped apologizing about it on Thursday because somebody had written them on a piece of paper and that had turned out to be enough.

He was sixty-eight, and he stopped twice on the way down and once on the way back, and each time it was at a place where the floor had moved under a boot.

“There,” he said, at nine meters. “That is the dip. It is not a dip, it is a fall, and it is about a hand deep, and if you come down here with a trolley you go over it and the trolley goes over it, and I have watched two washing machines go over it.”

Petar Lisk wrote it on his clipboard and read it back and made Emil say it again, because that was the job, and Emil said it again.

At the end Emil stood in front of the door and did not touch it.

“This is the one that was not on the plan,” he said.

“This is the one that was not on the plan,” Elias said, and then, to Brauer, who was six meters back with her camera up: “You have that on your camera.”

“I have that on my camera,” Brauer said, “and I have got the date on it, because I have been photographing structures for twenty-two years and a photograph without a date is a rumor.”

The measurements took most of an hour. Elias ran the laser himself down the full usable length and then again with Petar reading the far end, because Petar's eyes were better than his and because a nine-year-old holding the far end of a tape is the most reliable optical instrument in the building. Emil Baros signed the keeper's line in his own hand, with his name and the date, and then made himself read it back, because he had watched Tomas do it twice and had decided the practice was worth stealing.

**LOWER SERVICE RUN — SECTOR 4C — USABLE SPAN 31.0 M — KEEPER: EMIL BAROS — RESIDENT**

It went in three places. The survey sheet, with the laser readings and two photographs and Brauer's initialed remarks page attached. Joon's custody log, with the time and the two witnesses. And the hall board, in Inez's hand, in chalk, where a resident could read it from a cot without standing up. The Office had one too, and under the terms of the instrument it went into the sealed node that night, and nobody in the hall counted it as ours.

And then, at three o'clock, they used it, and they were finished before the board convened.

The bed frame came down first, on a dolly, with Emil Baros in front of it and Petar Lisk behind it and six residents making a noise about it that made a radio operator three streets away look up from his board. Then two mattresses, a chest of drawers that had belonged to a man who went into a tunnel, spare lamp batteries in crates, and a hundred and forty pounds of rice in two sacks, because rice in a hall where twenty-three people are being fed for an unknown length of time is not a metaphor.

“Left hand's gone,” Elias said, halfway down with the line, and gave the line to Kellan without being asked, and the handover took four seconds and Emil Baros went on ahead with the lamp as though nothing had been said, which was exactly right.

Eleven trips, forty minutes, one corridor, one resident, one boy with a clipboard, and one Office supervisor at the top of the ramp photographing a mattress going down thirty-one meters of concrete at ten past three in the afternoon.

---

The review board sat at half past four with the finding on the wall behind it, and it took two hours and ten minutes, and it did not seal and it did not clear.

It was Sable Arden in the chair, a Meridian labor rep called Osei Kwarteng, an independent structural engineer named **Dr. Ilona Brecht**, the clinic's medical adviser, and a clerk with a recorder. They had the finding in front of them and the digest behind it and about forty people in the room who were not allowed to speak and were not going to.

**Granted.** The citizens' request for a joint structural inspection was accepted and converted into a condition: no survey of the west apron node may proceed except as a joint inspection with a resident observer present and a copy of every measurement in the residents' file. The original of the 05:02 dispatch sheet is to be produced to Hollis Vane, in the hall, under supervision, inside a stated window. And the minutes are to record, in Vane's exact wording, that the classification is provisional pending a cause, and that if no cause is established the board must say so in writing rather than allow the standing site default to become the file's answer.

**Withheld.** The hold was neither extended nor lifted. The provisional classification stands. The site reverts to the standing designation at midnight, which puts the pre-ticked cause box back in the file. And the survey condition carries a date: a joint inspection, or a written statement of refusal, within eleven days.

Sable took the loss on the minutes and kept the site, and Brecht explained the void's proximity to the clinic in three sentences without raising her voice, and Elias understood that both of those things were true at the same time and that the room had just watched them be true at the same time.

“We did not seal and we did not clear,” Sable said at the end. “Nothing moved, nothing was given, and we lost an afternoon. In eleven days somebody produces a cause or produces a refusal, and at that point this site is still mine to seal. And I will answer your archivist's four findings in writing by Monday, Mr. Rook. Three of them I agree with.”

“Which one do you not.”

“Finding four,” Sable said. “Because Finding Four is not an archivist's finding. It is a request from your side of the room, and I am not obliged to treat a request as a finding, and the minute I do, this institution starts taking instructions from a union hall in the west bank, and then we are somewhere neither of us wants to be.”

Hollis Vane, from the wall, said, “Director, I wrote Finding Four.”

“I know you did, Mr. Vane. I have been arguing with your finding for two days. Do not be flattered.”

---

At eight that night the board had four things on it and the hall had thirty-one people in it, and Petar Lisk read the day's lines out loud because that was his job and nobody had offered to do it for him.

**BOARD: NO SEAL. NO CLEAR. SURVEY CONDITION, ELEVEN DAYS. DISPATCH ORIGINAL TO BE PRODUCED.**

**HOLD EXPIRES MIDNIGHT. AFTER MIDNIGHT THE SITE IS STILL SEALED AND STILL PROVISIONAL AND WE HAVE NO CLAIM ON IT.**

**LOWER SERVICE RUN: 31.0 M. KEEPER: EMIL BAROS. WALKED TODAY BY E. BAROS, WITNESSED BY P. LISK, OBSERVED BY I. BRAUER, OFFICE. IN THREE PLACES.**

And then, in Inez's chalk, underneath all of it:

**A ROUTE THAT NO CURRENT DOCUMENT IN THIS CITY MENTIONS IS NOW ON THREE PIECES OF PAPER THAT WE CONTROL, WALKED TODAY BY A NAMED PERSON WITH A WITNESS, AND WE WILL HAVE TO ANSWER FOR IT.**
