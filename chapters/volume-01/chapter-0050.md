# Chapter 50 — The Sealed Tunnel

The order came up on the Spine at ten past nine on Thursday morning with a seal number on it, and under the seal number was a single phrase: *catastrophic progressive structural failure attributable to legacy installation condition*, which is nine words that mean *equipment failed*.

Sable Arden signed it at eleven forty with the door shut, on the evidence she holds, and she is right on the evidence she holds, and everybody in that hall has known that for a month and nobody ever enjoyed it. Hollis Vane entered it into the site file by hand, in the fourth column, in a file that held his own Finding 1, accepted nine days ago with a mechanism that takes nine weeks to clear the desks. He did not remark on that. Joon Park wrote down that nobody had asked him to.

“That figure is not the site’s number and I am not offering it as the site’s number,” Vane said, when he had finished, to nobody. “It is a count of the persons this site produced, and it is a correct count, and it is not the count of the people who were in the ground. Both sentences are now in one file, in two inks, and in four years one of them will be read and the other one will be looked for, and in nineteen years I have never been able to make those two sentences into one sentence and I have stopped trying.”

“Do not let them be consolidated,” Nadi Bell said.

“In nineteen years I have never let anything be consolidated,” Vane said. “That is a job description.”

---

They carried eleven cardboard boxes out of the building between nine and eleven on Thursday morning, in daylight, on an ordinary street, in a handcart.

Two copies of every measurement, including the eleven meters, the day it changed, and a man's own account of finding out with a cart. Every patient name in Lena Mir's hand, including Perpetua Oyelaran's and the man who died on Sunday, whose card on the wall says *he was alive when he came through*. Every survey frame, including three that were never printed. The nine-line disclosure and the nine-line arithmetic, both in Tomas Vale's handwriting. The refusal log, complete. And a sheet with eleven words on it, a relay stamp seen twice, and a line saying the meaning is not written down.

One of those boxes went out of the city on a bus on the Sunday morning by an ordinary road, on the Chapter 21 precedent, carried by a man who had left for his sister and come back and would not say what was in it and did not have to.

---

At four o'clock on Thursday afternoon, the thirty-third day, the last one on the schedule, Elias Rook put a handset on a line and fed it eleven meters down a corridor for the last time.

The man picked it up on the fourth ring.

“Three things,” Elias said. “The first is short, the second is the reason for the first, and the third is the only one I need from you. You can put the handset down at any point.”

“You have never asked me for anything in twenty-five days,” the man said. “Go on.”

“The corridor is being sealed this afternoon. There is a closure order on the tunnel, a structure over the loading lip, and a document saying why. The document is real and it is not true, and I have given a copy of the true one to the woman who signed the false one, so she has both.”

“That is the second thing with a preamble on it,” the man said. “Say the first one.”

“You are inside the thing that is being closed, and I did not know that until Sunday, and I am not going to ask you anything about it.”

“Everybody asks me,” the man said. “Not you. A contractor in the first week. Two men with a clipboard in the second. A woman with a form in the third who wanted my name and could not have had it if I had said no. Nobody has ever said *I did not know that* before, and you have said it four times.”

Elias said the second thing, which arrived fully formed.

“If I put you in the file, you are real to the Office. You are in a seal order with a chain and a number and somebody's signature on it, and the seal has to be written around you or written through you, and a hole in that seal is a hole in the only thing standing between this street and two hundred and ten meters of something nobody has ever located.” He stopped. “If I do not write you down, the seal closes, you are behind it, and the only person in this city who knows you are there is me.”

“I am not a rescue,” the man said.

“No,” Elias said. “You never were. That is what has made thirty-three days possible.”

The line hissed.

“Do not write me down,” the man said.

Elias did not argue. That is in the log, and the log says it is the only moment in thirty-three days when he was asked for something and took it.

“All right,” he said, “and nobody in my building is going to write it, and I am going to say out loud what I am doing instead, so that it is a thing you know rather than a thing you find out later.” He said it into a handset in the cold. “On the thirty-third day, at four o'clock, a man at the end of eleven meters was asked whether the hall would record him, and he declined, and the hall did not record him, and the seal went on at six, and the reason is in our book with the date on it and no name on it and my name on it and the initials of a nine-year-old who writes down what people do not say.”

“You are going to write down that I exist,” the man said, in a level voice, “and leave out the only part that would let anybody find me.”

“Yes.”

“That is worse than not writing it. That is a door with a sign on it in a language nobody in the building reads.”

“Yes,” Elias said. “It is. And there were four available and this is the one I picked, and the other three are that we open it again in eleven days, that I do not go, and that I will not come up the ramp. Those are the whole of what I have.”

The man was quiet for about a minute and a half.

“It is not warm on my side either,” he said. “It has not been warm since Monday. And I am not going to tell you what I am going to do about it, and you will not come up the ramp, and I will not be here on a Thursday after this one, and you should write that I said it, because it is the last thing I am going to say to anybody and I would like it to be a sentence in a book and not a thing that happened to me.”

“It goes in the book.”

“Eleven meters,” the man said. “Write eleven. Not a number. A length. And put the day on it.”

“Eleven meters, and the day, and the line between,” Elias said, “and your own words, and the cart.”

The line went flat.

---

The tunnel was sealed at six on Thursday evening by six people from the Rescue Service and twenty-two residents of a hall on Ash Street who were told exactly what they were standing beside and stood beside it anyway. The last thing across the mouth was a sheet of plywood with the date on it in Emil Baros's hand.

The hall stayed up on two households' consent and four carded draws a day and a woman with a lock-box, and Lena Mir's round on a page anybody can read, and a corridor light somebody fixes without being asked, and nineteen people with a function each, and a keeper who is going to be asked to walk a line eleven meters away in the morning when it is light. And two hundred and ten meters of void under an apron people walk over, which is still there and which nobody has looked at.

The broker's license named this site, and a license that names a sealed site is a license that names a wall. Nobody in the hall has told R. Adare anything and nobody is going to, and the room above the shuttered print shop on Calder Street had eight people in it on Wednesday night and none of them knew why.

---

Elias Rook and Mara Okafor stood at the end of the front room at half past eight with the board behind them and eleven boxes in a garage.

“Rule,” Elias said. “Not for us. For anybody.”

Mara said it faster, because she had been holding it since Tuesday night, and Petar Lisk had to be sent home to fetch paper and was not in the room for any of it and wrote it down afterwards from what two people told him, which is how a rule gets into a book in that building.

“Any offer, any boundary, any refusal, and any condition somebody is asked to perform before they give anything goes to the people who pay for it, in the words it was said in, before it is acted on. If they say no, the answer is no, and it does not matter how late it is or how much has already been spent.” She looked at him. “I have had two days to make it smaller and I could not, and I am not going to say it smaller in a room that has to read it.”

“It goes on the wall in the morning. In your hand, because you said it.”

“And the half that is mine. Nobody inherits her. If there is a Mara in a branch who lends a hand, that is a woman deciding about her own capacity, and I do not get to be angry at her later, and I am not going to be the woman who says a future version of me agrees with me about this.”

“Nobody inherits her,” Elias said. “And nobody inherits me.”

He put the pen down and said the other half of it to the room, because she had a rule about things being said to a room and not to a person and he was going to observe it.

“My name goes under hers on the board in the morning, and my half is four things I cannot do any more and do not miss. I cannot call a future person. I cannot get back to the woman who lent me a hand on Sunday, and if a voice that sounds like mine comes up out of a corridor tomorrow, the answer is that I am not the person who asks. I cannot make the anchors carry anything. And I cannot turn nineteen people with a function each into an answer to the question the City keeps asking, which is who is in charge of this. The answer is going to keep being *the twenty-two*, and I am going to have to live in a building run by people I have not met.” He put his hand flat on the table. “That is where I am. I cannot reach anybody from here and never could, and the difference is that I know it now in a sentence I can say out loud.”

Mara looked at him for a while.

“Nothing has happened between us,” she said, “and I want it said out loud tonight, while there is somebody in this building to hear it, because in a month somebody is going to write a thing down about us and it is going to mean that a future version of one of us said something.”

“No,” Elias said. “And no future version of either of us has promised us anything.”

“And the last hard one. In five weeks, everything that has gone wrong in this building has gone wrong because somebody made a promise before they knew the facts. Including you. Including me. And I can say nothing has happened only because you have refused four routes and a road and a hand that might have saved a man's life. I am not going to tell you what it was for, because you do not know.”

“I do not know,” Elias said.

---

He wrote the notebook up at half past nine, at the table, with the lamp. He wrote the eleven words and the stamp and the second time, and that he had refused it, and that the refusal had cost him a line. And then he wrote the sentence that took twenty minutes and that he crossed out twice and did not cross out the third time.

*I am giving up the claim that a good rescue means I bring her back next. She is on a relay under the water past a wall, speaking for people who are not on a list, and she has told me twice not to count them, and the only road to her is the one I have refused four times. If I get to her it will be because she is still answering, and not because I have earned it.*

And then, on the same page, beneath the signal, in ballpoint, he wrote the name of a man who came out of a chamber under Kestrel Station on the thirtieth day at twenty minutes past nine in the evening, on a board carried the length of a corridor by four people, and who said his own name twice — once into Lena Mir's ear and once to nobody in particular — and who was fifty-eight and weighed fifty-one pounds.

There is no line for her in the classification that went up this morning, because an equipment failure does not carry passengers. There is no field for her in the four columns of the aisle ledger and no row in the sealed node. The Civic Spine terminal in the records room on Ash Street, which has been on since Monday, will not find her name, and neither will the Office's.

The name is in ink, on paper, in a book, on a page beneath a signal from a woman under the water. The envelope is in the front of the notebook under that page, and it is not sent, and the reason under it is not a survey any more and it is not a night of somebody's feet in a ring on Calder Street. It is four words. *The road is sealed.*

---

The wall gave one last echo at eleven o'clock on Thursday night, in the front room, where everybody was sitting down for the first time in three days. It did not come out of the terminal, or a handset, or a machine in a building the team cannot enter. It came out of eleven meters of concrete, through the plywood, the way it had come since the ninth day, and it was not loud, and it said his name.

“Elias.”

He stood up. Nobody else stood up, and that is in the log, and Joon Park wrote that nobody else stood up and that he wrote it with his hand shaking.

“Say it once more,” Elias said, because his left hand had gone completely and he wanted to be sure the words were for him and not for the room.

“Elias Rook. You will be asked for a great many reasonable things over the next while. Here is the one that matters. Promise me that you will not force a future person to answer when that future person says no.”

Elias stood in a hall with twenty-eight people in it and a lamp by the door, and behind him were four things signed on a wall, and the fifth was the one Petar Lisk had written for him in the second week: *he is not the person who asks his own future for anything.*

And he understood that this had been the trap since the ninth day. A man who had spent thirty-three days learning that a promise is a thing you make before you know the facts had come to the end of a hall where the only door made of words leads into a corridor he cannot walk down, and on the other side of it a man he had never met was asking him to hand over a version of himself who had not been asked.

“I am not going to make that promise,” Elias said.

He said it out loud, in a room with twenty-eight people in it, and then said the other thing, because it was the only true thing available and it was neither an answer nor a refusal.

“I do not know what I will do. I know what I have done thirty-three times. Thirty-four is not mine to give away, and I am not going to hand a man I have never met a promise about a man I am going to be.” He put his right hand flat on the table, because the left one was gone. “And if that is the same as no, then somebody who is not me can write that down.”

Petar Lisk picked up the pencil. His hand was not steady either. He wrote it at the bottom of the wall in the ninth-year-old capitals and read it out.

**HE DID NOT MAKE THE PROMISE. HE ALSO DID NOT SAY NO. HE SAID HE DID NOT KNOW, AND HE SAID THIRTY-FOUR IS NOT HIS TO GIVE AWAY, AND HE ASKED SOMEBODY ELSE TO WRITE IT DOWN.**

Nobody else stood up.

And in the records room, on a lamp, there is a page in a paper notebook with a woman's name on it in ballpoint, beneath eleven words from a relay under the water, and there is nowhere in the city to type it in.

The eleven meters stays eleven meters. The two hundred and ten stays two hundred and ten. One woman stays under Ash Street with her name on a wall. Nine credentials with no names sit in an archive in Cordell that does not answer to Havenport. And a woman on the Meridian is right about all of it, and she is going to keep being right.

The question nobody in that city can answer yet is the one Petar Lisk writes on the board at seven in the morning, in his own hand.

*Who edited the Kestrel response record so that a living passenger and a woman's route were both called administrative errors — and what does the Continuity Office believe that a single approved outcome would prevent?*
