# Chapter 28 — Adare Comes Back

R. Adare came at ten past ten on Friday night, on foot, with his coat over his arm, and he had picked his hour with the care of a man who has spent a decade finding out when a street is empty.

He had picked a hall with forty-two people in it, which he had clearly expected to be smaller.

“The Service recalled two of you this morning,” he said, before he was asked. “It is in the Quarter Sheet, because the Quarter Sheet prints everything and pretends not to. So I will not insult you by pretending I do not know that the rope work in this building is now you and one other man, and that one other man is sixty-four and is a safety instructor, and that neither of you is a confined-space technician.”

“You read a paper,” Elias said.

“I read four papers,” Adare said. “And I have a licensing application in, and I will come to that, and I want to get the polite part out of the way first because you will all assume the rest is a threat and it is mostly a form.”

He set his laminated card on the table, face up, and did not sit down.

“Seven was a bad number. I told you on Tuesday that seven was a good number and that six was workable, and I was telling the truth, and it was still a bad number, and I have been in this business for eleven years and I have watched people put six and then five and then four into a tension and each time the arithmetic gets worse, because a tension is not a door, it is a load, and a load with fewer people in it is a load that goes somewhere else. Four in it tonight is not a safer version of seven. It is a worse version with a smaller number on the card. I am telling you that because I would not take your money and lie about it afterward.”

“What do you want,” Elias said.

“Two things and a warning. The two things I have had all week.” Adare turned the card over. “The route, at four, tonight, and I have a firm end. And east bank accommodation by morning, for a family, with a powered bed and three days of medical power from a generator that is not on any feeder, and a doctor’s letter I can get by six, and no form, no Office, no incident reference, nothing that follows them.”

“Whose family,” Inez said.

Adare did not look at her.

“Osei,” he said. “His wife. Lena Mir can tell you what her back is doing and Lena Mir has told me, and I have asked her and she has answered me, because I asked her as a man who could find her a bed and not as a man who wanted something out of her. She is not in this building. She is at the clinic on a non-powered bed on a ward that shares a budget with a hall that is going to be dark two hours a day for the rest of the eleven days, and the hall is right, and the dark hours are right, and the arithmetic still puts her on a frame in the dark by Thursday.”

Nobody in the hall looked at Lena Mir, and Lena Mir said, from the wall, in a voice that nobody in the room had heard her use, “That is true and I answered him and I would answer him again. He asked me plainly and he paid for a bed that I have not got money for. It is true, and it is also the exact reason I am not going to let this room feel clever about it.”

“And the warning,” Elias said.

“The warning is that I have lodged an application for a broker license inside your classification, and you cannot stop it, and you are not going to be able to make it go away by being difficult at me, because difficulty is what a form is for.” He put his hand flat on the laminated card. “A license is a form. The form says a person may facilitate branch routing within a designated seam site for a fee, with consent recorded, with a witness, with a return. Everything I have offered you since the second week has been that form with the compliance parts done properly and a bit more care than it needed. You have refused every one of them, and the reason is not that it is dangerous. It is that you would rather be illegal than answerable.”

“That is not an answer to anything,” Elias said.

“It is an answer to why you are about to refuse me a tenth time and I would rather you did it with your eyes open.” Adare looked around the room, slowly, at the beds and the mats and the block wall and the four hundred hours on the board. “I am not going to do anything to anybody in this building. I want to be extremely clear, because I have read what you people write about me and I would like the record to have one accurate sentence in it. I do not take anybody. I do not hurt anybody. What I do is come back in three weeks with a form, and the form will be in the hands of a neighbor, and the neighbor will be somebody you have been arguing with about a parking space for a week, and it will be a reasonable request, and it will be answered in a reasonable way by a reasonable person, and by the third of those the wall in this hall has a door in it that somebody else built.”

He took his hand off the card.

“That is not a threat,” he said. “I have threatened people. This is not that. This is a description of how a place that is doing everything right loses a thing anyway, and I have watched it happen to a clinic in Cordell.”

---

Elias opened his mouth to say the sentence he had said on Tuesday, and Nadi Bell got there first.

She was sitting at the back with the child's school coat over her knees where she had sat every day for nine days, and she stood up, and she did not raise her voice, and the hall went quiet in a way that had nothing to do with authority.

“Say the offer again,” she said, “but say the part about the row.”

Adare looked at her.

“My sister's name came off a response list on the second day,” Nadi said, “and you told me on the third day that you could put it back by six that evening, and that it would not be difficult work, and that it was work somebody has to be paid to do. And I said no, and Joon wrote it down, and you signed it, and it is in five buildings.”

“It is.”

“I have had nine days to think about what a person is worth who can put a name back on a list,” Nadi said. “And I have decided he is worth exactly what it costs the people who have to stand in a line to be found. That is his price. Not his fee. His price. And the fee is four hundred and the price is every person in this room who would have to be somewhere else instead of here, and you do not get to sell the fee and pretend the price is somebody else's decision.”

The hall did not make a sound.

“You want me to write it down,” Adare said.

“Yes. The offer, all of it, in the order you made it, and the time, and my refusal, and my name on it, and give it to him.” She pointed, without looking, at Joon. “The same as Tuesday. Same form. Because a man who has been refused once can tell a story about the refusal, and a man who has been refused twice with two pieces of paper cannot, and the reason the first one worked is that I stood on my own feet and made a stranger write it down before I left the room. I am not going to be a woman who was asked twice.”

Adare looked at her for a while, and then he took a pen out of his coat, and he wrote it, and he wrote all of it, and he signed it **R. ADARE** and tore it off and put it on the table, and did it willingly, which was the detail Joon had written down three days ago and had expected to see again.

“Denny Prokop told me about you,” Joon said, reading it. “At the gate on Saturday, in a line about cable. He said you were the man who wrote things down when women asked him to, and I said I did not believe him.”

“Prokop is a decent man with a bad yard,” Adare said. “I do write things down. It costs me nothing and it is the only product I have.”

---

They refused him at eleven, and it took forty minutes and it was not one refusal, it was six, because the room had learned something in nine days about what a refusal is for.

Petar's exit drill went off at half past ten with nobody expecting it, because Petar had decided that a man who can build a door in a form can also build a scarecrow and put it outside the gate, and the scarecrow had a form on it and a description of the service and a weekly fee. It was made of a mop handle and a coat and it was extremely funny and it was the most frightening object anybody had put on that street in nine days.

Osei voted to keep it up for another week.

And the six refusals were read out, and Nadi's was the third, and every one of them had been written down by whoever made it, and the last one was Elias's, and it was four sentences and it did not contain the word anchor, because he had learned that the word only ever appeared in order to be refused, and a refusal that names the thing it is refusing is a document, and a document can be produced in a room where nobody is in danger.

He said: we are not doing this tonight, or on any of the eleven days, and we are not using anybody in this hall, or on this street, or in this clinic, as part of a tension, and if a license comes through I would like the record to show that we were asked and we said no in front of forty-two people who can read it, and I am not going to pretend that saying it again is easy, because it is not, it is just repeated.

Then Inez read the count, and the count was that no card in this hall had been withdrawn in nine days in order to take an offer like this one, and she said so, and it was the only number in the whole argument that she had asked for.

---

Joon walked Adare out to the gate, which was the only polite thing anybody did for him all night, and came back in with the signed page and a question.

“Who do we tell?”

“He is licensed,” Elias said. “That is the thing I have been not saying for an hour. He is not a criminal. Everything he has offered is a service with a form, a consent, a witness and a return, which is more paperwork than we have ever done, and the reason we have called him a broker for nine days is that we did not have a better word and because he is frightening and useful and neither of those is the same as illegal.”

“So it is a licensing question.”

“It is a licensing question, inside a classification, on the Friday before a Monday, with a written warning lodged in a hall with forty-two people in it.” Elias looked at the board, where the two dates were up in Joon's hand. “Which means the person who has to answer it is the Director of the Havenport Continuity Office, and the record now shows that she was told at ten past eleven on a Friday night, by name, in front of witnesses, and she cannot answer it with a form she writes in a warm building and files on Monday morning.”

“She can answer it on Monday morning,” Joon said. “That is exactly what a Director does.”

“Yes,” Elias said. “And this time she has to answer it into a room that already knows the shape of the question, and that is the whole of what we actually accomplished tonight, and it is not nothing.”
