# Chapter 24 — The Box at the Depot

The Spine's archive floor was below the freight bay, down a ramp, past the battery room, and it was colder than the tunnel had been by nine degrees, which everyone agreed was the single most reassuring thing about it.

Ilse Brauer met them at the bottom of the ramp with a list.

“The board's order is production to a named archivist,” she said. “The named archivist is Mr. Vane, who is at his desk on the far side because he has been in this building since seven and has not sat down. You will not touch the box. You will not touch the document. One of you photographs, one of you reads aloud, and Mr. Vane decides what is photographed. The window closes at twelve twenty because the transfer clerk comes back at twelve thirty and the aisle log is her aisle.”

“And after twelve thirty?”

“After twelve thirty the aisle log is a book on a cart and anybody can say they did not see you.”

Joon wrote that down.

“Photograph the aisle log first,” he said.

“That is the first useful thing anybody has said in this corridor,” Brauer said.

---

Hoyt was at the desk with a scanner and a bad chair, and she was twenty-six, and she had been the clerk who wrote **LOG ABSENT PRIOR TO TRANSFER** on a transfer sheet in the rain eight days ago.

She looked at Joon for slightly too long.

“You are the dispatcher,” she said.

“Yes.”

“I have read your file. Copies only. The medical ledger and the manifest and the three-column page.” She said it without hostility, which was worse than if she had been hostile, because it meant she was deciding something. “Mr. Park, I want to say something and then I would like to go back to work.”

“All right.”

“This request is now in an access log. Not yours — the one your archivist made under the board's order. It has a time on it, and it has a subject on it, and the subject is *Kestrel incident, pending classification, box 214*. And in about four months, when this is either a closed file or an open one, somebody is going to pull the log and see that a rescue team asked a specific question about a specific box on a specific Monday, and they are going to be able to say the team knew what was in it.” She looked up. “I am not saying no. I am saying you should know that asking is not free and that I have told you so in a room with a recorder in it, which is the only way any of this is any use.”

“You can,” Joon said. “I want to say one thing back and I am not going to make us look small to be polite about it. I would rather be a small request that is exactly what it is than a polite one that is a tenth of what it is. Put every word of it in the log. I have spent a week watching a document get softened by somebody who meant well, and it cost a man his name on a page, and we are not doing that in reverse to make a clerk like us.”

Hoyt looked at him for a second longer and went back to work, and Joon found that he did not much care whether she liked him, which was new and slightly uncomfortable.

---

Box 214 was on aisle nine, third shelf, and it was not heavy, and it was not large, and Hollis Vane carried it himself to a table in a room with a camera in the ceiling and a second camera on the wall and no window.

He opened it with a key he wore on a chain inside his coat and did not let anybody see, and he said, as he did it, the thing that made him what he was: “Chain of custody: I take it out of the box at eleven oh four, in this room, with Mr. Park photographing the whole of the box and its label before anything is removed, and I put it back at eleven forty-one. If either of those times is wrong, Mr. Park will tell me, because he has the camera.”

Joon said, “Eleven oh four, correct.”

Vane lifted the first folder out and set it down and did not open it until Joon had counted the camera past the label.

Then he opened it, and read, and stopped.

“Ah,” he said.

He turned the sheet around so the table could see it.

It was a dispatch sheet. Saturday's. Two names on the sign-out, a two-person Continuity inspection team for west apron signal cabinet 4C, out at 05:02, back at 06:50. The first signature was a scrawl and Hoyt, looking over from the desk, said, “That is Vashti. That is definitely Vashti, that is her loop.”

The second signature line was not blank.

It was not a name.

It was a string of characters written in pen, in the same block, in the same size, in the box that was for a person: **MNT-4C-17**.

Nobody said anything for four seconds.

“Say that again,” Elias said, “and read it the way you would read it into a record.”

Hollis Vane read it again, slowly, checked the two lines against each other, and then did something Elias had not expected him to do, which was to sit down.

“This is not a signature,” Vane said. “A signature is a person taking responsibility for a thing. This is a credential string written in a box that asks for a person. In the field, on a sign-out sheet, a class-three maintenance credential cannot be used to close a two-person Continuity inspection. It cannot open a public alarm. It cannot sign on the platform side. What it can do is take a local lockout, isolate a local feeder, hold a door in a maintenance state, post a shoring permit, and post an unindexed local route entry.”

“Then it cannot sign that line at all,” Elias said.

“It cannot sign that line **lawfully**,” Vane said. “Which is not the same statement, and I would ask you to notice that I have made the distinction deliberately and that it is the only thing keeping me from writing something I should not write.”

He looked up.

“Here is what we have. At 05:02, two Continuity people signed out for cabinet 4C. One of them is Vashti and says she logged a signal fault and nothing else. The other line is closed with the credential that was in the cabinet at 05:17:11, fifteen minutes later, making a local route entry in a sector the public index does not carry. That is not a person. That is the same object twice, in two places, in one morning.”

“So the 05:02 sheet and the 05:17 entry are the same hand,” Joon said, writing fast.

“They are the same credential, which is not the same as the same hand. I will not let those two be confused in my file and I would ask you not to confuse them in yours.”

Elias put both hands flat on the table. His left one reported nothing at all and he let it lie there, and Joon, who was watching him do it without making anything of it, moved Hoyt's chair half an inch so the hand would not be knocked.

“Then where is the person,” Elias said.

“There is no person in this document,” Vane said. “There is one person in it. That is the finding, and it is the whole of the finding, and I have been an archivist for nineteen years and I have never written that sentence about a sheet before and I do not like it.”

---

The aisle log was the part Joon had asked for first, and it sat on the table through most of the morning, and it was the only piece of paper in the room that nobody had planned for.

It was a bound ledger, ruled in days, four columns: hour, aisle, box, subject. Saturday's row for aisle nine had two entries, one above the other.

The first was at 09:40 and read, in full: *Kestrel site records, 4C, material pending transfer.* Ordinary. Legible. The kind of line a hundred of those pages have.

The second was at 14:12, in the next line down, in the same hand, and it read: *Rout. arch. rev. — aisle 9 — 214.* Routine archive review.

And the fourth column, the subject, was blank.

Not ruled over. Not struck through. Blank, in the space where a subject is written, in a column that exists on every page of the ledger for as far back as anybody had looked.

Joon read it three times and then said, quietly, to nobody, “It has a field.”

“It has a field,” Vane said.

“Your archivist's finding,” Elias said, looking up, “was that the sector roster has no holder field, so nobody can be blamed and nobody is. This has a field. Somebody could have written a subject in it and did not, on the day of the collapse, an hour and forty-eight minutes before the crates came back from the west apron.”

“Yes,” Vane said. “And I want to put the two side by side so that nobody mends this one by accident. A field that does not exist cannot be blank. It is clean, and it is unfixable, and every report for seven years has said the same honest nothing. A field that can be blank can also be filled — tomorrow, by a person who was not there, who will be entirely accurate about their own afternoon and entirely silent about somebody else's.” He closed the ledger. “A blank field is not a hole. It is a place where a name goes later, without anybody lying.”

Nobody in the room liked that sentence and everybody wrote it down.

“When,” Elias said. “When was the box put back and when was the 14:12 entry compared to the transfer?”

“Box out at eleven oh four, back at eleven forty-one today, both on this camera and both on Mr. Park's. It was in the depot already on Saturday afternoon — Saturday's dispatch sheets go to the day file, and the day file is this box, and the crates from the west apron went to a different aisle entirely. Whoever came at fourteen twelve on Saturday came for this box and not for the site records, and the site records are on the same aisle four boxes down, and they are still sealed.”

---

They walked it out through the freight bay at eleven fifty, and the sun was the wrong color for the hour and Elias had not noticed what time it was for two days.

Hollis Vane walked with them to the top of the ramp, which was more than the rules required and less than anybody would have called a friendship.

“Mr. Rook,” he said. “I am going to do the thing I said I would not do.”

“Which thing.”

“I am going to give you a copy.” He said it like a man picking up something hot. “The board ordered production. Production means production, and I have made my copy and the copy is in your dispatcher's hand and it is going into five buildings, and the Office has one too, and the room said four out loud before anybody had to count, because the Office had a legal right to one the moment the board ordered the document out of a depot. I am not doing it because I think you are right. I am doing it because a finding that exists in my building only is a finding that my building can revise without anybody noticing.”

“Fine,” Elias said, and then, because he could not help it, “Thank you.”

“Do not,” Vane said. “You will be tempted in about six weeks to treat me as somebody who is on your side, and I am not. I wrote four pages this week and two of them were against the Office's classification and two of them were against your side of the room, and the fourth one Sable Arden rejected by name on Saturday because she says it is a request and not a finding, and she is entitled to say that, and I have written back to say that she is entitled to say it and that I wrote it anyway.”

He got back down the ramp. Joon watched him go and then said, quietly, “He is going to be our witness in this and he is going to hate it.”

---

The block wall came down off a pallet truck at two o'clock because the hall's end door is thirty-one inches wide and a pallet of concrete block is not, and because the loading lip at the near end is the only way a hand truck gets in there, and that is the only reason the corridor is on any map at all.

Emil Baros walked in front of it. **Anja Ro** walked behind it with a clipboard she had not been issued and had taken from a table, and she counted as she went and said the number out loud at every stop, and at the last stop she said, “Thirty-one,” and then went to the inspection niche at twelve meters, where they had clipped the Saturday survey sheet inside a plastic sleeve on Saturday afternoon, and read the number off it twice.

“That is the whole thing,” she said. “It is on a piece of paper in a wall. If somebody had to prove it existed next year, they would stand here and read that.”

Nobody answered her, and she carried on counting, and the count came out the same on the way back.

---

The block wall went up on the dry end of the hall from four to midnight, by four residents and a man from the Third Ward who was not good and was fast, and by nine o'clock there was a door in it with a lock on it, and behind the door were two tables and a fire safe and every copy of everything that had happened in a week.

At nine that night Joon read the log entries out, and Inez wrote them on the board, and Petar Lisk copied them onto his clipboard and read them back.

**MONDAY: THE 05:02 ORIGINAL WAS PRODUCED TO H. VANE IN THE SPINE ARCHIVE ROOM AT 11:04, PHOTOGRAPHED, AND RETURNED AT 11:41. COPY HELD BY J. PARK, BY THE RESIDENTS' FILE, BY THE OFFICE, AND BY H. VANE. FOUR.**

**MONDAY: THE SECOND SIGNATURE LINE ON THE 05:02 DISPATCH SHEET IS THE CREDENTIAL MNT-4C-17. IT IS NOT A NAME. NO HOLDER IS IDENTIFIED.**

**MONDAY: THE AISLE LOG SHOWS BOX 214 OPENED ON THE DAY OF THE COLLAPSE AT 14:12, UNDER *ROUT. ARCH. REV.*, WITH THE SUBJECT FIELD BLANK.**

**MONDAY: THE LOWER SERVICE RUN WAS WALKED AT 15:00 BY E. BAROS, WITNESSED BY A. RO, USED TO MOVE NINE PALLETS OF BLOCK, COUNTED TWICE AT 31.0 M. IN THREE PLACES.**

And then Inez, in chalk, under all of it, with two other people's sentences inside hers:

**THE BOARD GAVE US THE BOX AND THE BOX GAVE US A CREDENTIAL INSTEAD OF A NAME AND A LOG WITH A HOLE IN IT. SO THE ARCHIVE IS NOW PART OF THE PROBLEM AND NOT ONLY THE ANSWER, AND I WANT THAT WRITTEN DOWN BEFORE ANYBODY IN THIS ROOM STARTS BEING RELIEVED ABOUT THE FIRST LINE.**
