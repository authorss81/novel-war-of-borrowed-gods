# Chapter 13 — MNT-4C-17

The west apron relay sat on a steel frame behind the old fuel line, thirty metres outside the yellow line, where it had been taking the damp for eleven years. Joon had been feeding it battery for two days. It did not need power to be interesting.

Nobody opened it quickly. Elias wanted to. Tomas made him stand on the far side of the frame with his hands visible while Joon worked, and when Elias asked why, Tomas said, “Because you are going to want to fix something in there, and there is nothing in there to fix. There is only paper, a card, and one bell that has been ringing for forty-four years without anybody listening to it.”

They had a little under three hours.

The cabinet was a steel box the size of a bread locker, painted the same dull green as the tunnel, with a mechanical fault bell on top, a wax-tape recorder inside the door, and a card reader at chest height with a green light that had gone amber. Water had run down the back of it for a decade and had come to rest as a grey crust along the bottom lip.

Joon had the door open in four minutes. Inside: a printed circuit card on a sliding rail, a strip of terminals, the wax tape under a hinged glass, and a folded card holder clipped to the inside of the door.

The card holder was empty. Not torn. Empty, with the retaining clip snapped back into its closed position, which meant it had been opened by a hand and closed by a hand.

“The page is gone,” Joon said.

“The stub book is gone too,” Elias said. He had found the slot for it in the bottom of the cabinet and the felt strip where the volume had lain. The felt was clean. The dust around it was not.

“So somebody who knew this cabinet took the paper log out, and it was not water.”

“Or it went to the Office four years ago for the archive and never came back. I am not telling you which is more likely. I am telling you that the paper is gone and the machine remembers.”

Joon looked at the card reader. “One read.”

“Explain.”

“The contacts are corroded. It will read now or it will not read again. If I put power in twice I get a partial answer and a dead contact, and a partial answer on a signalled form is something the Office can use against us forever.” He laid his palm on the cabinet, well clear of the reader. “So we read it once, and we decide now what we are looking for.”

“Read what it says,” Elias said. “Not what we hope it says.”

Joon read the caution card aloud first. The record, he said, would hold: entry type, sector, credential, sequence, and the local time. It would not hold a name. Names were not on the card. Names were on a sector roster in a different building, on paper, in a drawer, and the drawer had a custodian and a sign-off and a transfer history, and none of those things were in this cabinet.

“Then we get a number that points at a name somewhere else,” Elias said.

“Then we get a number that points at a name somewhere else,” Joon agreed, “and we do not get to skip the part in between.”

He powered it.

---

The card answered with a flat click and a small green fan of light, and Joon read for eleven seconds and then cut the power with his thumb, exactly as he had said he would.

“One entry,” he said. “Type is route. Sector is 4C. Credential is MNT-4C-17. Sequence number four. Local time 05:17 and eleven seconds.”

“Sequence four,” Tomas said. “There were three more that morning and the card only keeps the recent set.”

“Can we get the earlier three?”

“No. The card rewrites its own buffer when it is full. Those three are gone and the Spine never had them, because the Spine was not told about them.”

Tomas put one finger on the cabinet, not on the electronics, and looked at the printed credential as though it were handwriting.

“MNT is a maintenance credential,” he said. “Not a person. Sector, then slot. 4C is the west apron and the Car 3 service level, which is why that service door is a 4C door and not a Spine door.” He moved his finger along the sector lettering. “The slot number is the order in which the credential was issued for that sector. Seventeen was early. The Meridian retrofit renumbered every sector in 2035, which is when the four-digit blocks started. Anything still in the old three-number form came out of the pre-retrofit issue.”

Elias did the arithmetic without wanting to. Two thousand and thirty-five. Seven years.

“So it predates the collapse by seven years.”

“It predates today by seven years. It was issued before the renumbering and somebody has been carrying it since. That is not the same as saying it was current. The whole point of a credential is that it survives its owner.”

Elias looked at the printed field again and felt the ground shift very slightly under his own certainty.

“Walk me through what it could have done.”

“Class three. Lockout and local route entry.” Tomas held up three fingers and put them down one at a time, as though he were removing weight from a load. “It could isolate the east leg, take the local feeder off the Spine, open a service door in a maintenance state, post a temporary shoring permit, and log a route entry that the public index would not see until somebody reconciled it. It could not raise a public alarm. It could not change a passenger record. It could not sign anything on the platform side.”

“So at 05:17 someone with local maintenance authority took that sector private and opened the route, and the route did not show up on the public index until the carriage did.”

“That is what the card says. I am not going to tell you who was standing at the cabinet, because I was not standing at the cabinet.”

---

The wax tape was the thing that made the afternoon worth the walk out.

Under its hinged glass, the fault recorder's arm had been drawing a line on a paper strip for forty-four years, dropping a blunt tick whenever the relay annunciated anything at all. Most of the tape was the flat grey of a machine with nothing to say. The last forty centimetres was not flat.

Three ticks clustered near the start of the marked section, then a gap, then two more, close together, and then a long unmarked run.

Joon read the times off the printed margin and then read them a second time, and then handed the glass to Elias without a word.

05:17.11. And 05:24.40.

Tomas sat down on the fuel-line kerb.

“That is a mechanical pen on a mechanical clock driven off the same rectifier as the annunciator,” he said. “It does not know what time it is except from the current. It cannot be edited without removing the tape. It cannot be made to print a false mark by anything anybody in that station could do from a platform.”

“It is a witness,” Joon said. “An actual object that was in the room and did the thing.”

“It is a witness to a bell ringing,” Tomas said. “Say it that way and I will sign it.”

---

They took an impression of the tape instead of the tape.

Elias wanted the strip itself. Tomas explained, without unkindness, that a strip removed from a cabinet on a site scheduled for evidence seizure at four o'clock is a strip that appears in a report written by somebody else, and that the safest copy of a mark is a second mark.

So Joon fed a blank length of wax tape into the recorder with the pen arm lifted, laid it against the marked strip so the two surfaces met, and ran a soft graphite stick across the back of the blank. The raised ticks came up on the new tape as shallow ribs, readable with a finger and, when Joon photographed them with a ruler in frame, readable on paper.

It took forty minutes. Elias read the times off the marked strip aloud and Joon read them off the impression and Tomas read them off the impression, and on the third pass they agreed, and Anja Ro was not there and did not need to be, and the number on the sheet was a number three people had checked.

Then Elias tried to hold the marked strip flat against the glass to re-check the 05:24 tick, and his left hand opened on the cabinet and closed on nothing.

“Left hand's gone,” he said.

“Give me the strip,” Joon said.

He gave it. He did not make a joke about it, and he did not say that he could see it fine, and both of those silences were new and both of them cost him something.

---

The last twenty minutes were about what to write, and Joon fought for it in the way he fought for everything, which was loudly and in front of witnesses.

“Do not put the code on the public sheet,” he said. “Not next to her form. Because the moment that code is on a wall where residents are reading it, the next thing a reader does is connect it to the Office, and the next thing after that is that the Office did it. We do not know that. We know a credential in a maintenance series was used at 05:17 and that a classification was opened at 05:24, and those are two facts with an hour between them, and a person is not in the middle of them yet.”

“You are protecting a colleague who has not been accused of anything,” Elias said.

“I am protecting twenty-three people from a headline that is wrong, and I am protecting the version of this we can defend in a room. And I am protecting the depot clerk who signs sector transfers from having her name printed in a district bulletin because a code came out of a box.”

Elias wanted to argue. What he actually wanted was to be the one who put the sharpest thing on the table first, and he had wanted that since the tunnel, and he recognised the wanting as a kind of vanity dressed in a work jacket.

“Then put it in a form that is true and does not accuse,” he said. “Write the code. Write the sector. Write the sequence and the time and the bell. Write *holder unknown — roster held at the Spine depot, not in this cabinet.* Write *no individual identified.* Put all of that on the public sheet, and keep the raw card image in the working file with my notebook.”

“And if the Office asks why we are interested in their own inspection credential?”

“Because their own inspection credential is the only thing on the table that reached the route before the alarm did,” Elias said. “That question deserves an answer in daylight.”

Joon wrote it. He read it back twice. Nadi Bell, who had come down to the door in a coat over her shoulders, read it standing up, and said, “That is the first thing anyone has written down all day that does not want something from me.”

The copy went into three places. Joon kept one. Elias's went into the notebook with the fibre tape, and he wrote the date and time and both signatures on the outside, because his left hand could not sign a name legibly and he was not going to pretend it could. The third went into Inez's cooperative lock-box in the Cinder Quarter, under a card that said, **HELD BY RESIDENTS — NOT BY THE RESCUE TEAM.**

“That is not an accident,” Inez said, taking it. “If your Office comes with a box, the box does not get to be the only one.”

---

They were twenty minutes from the seal when they got back to the west apron, and Joon stopped walking for a moment in the middle of the apron to look at the fuel line, and the seal truck, and the station behind them.

“The card was read once,” he said. “The tape is going into a box at four o'clock. The paper log was gone before we got there, and it was gone cleanly, and whoever took it knew the retaining clip.”

“Then we work from what is in the cabinet,” Elias said. “Two signatures, one of them a credential, and a bell that rang twice before the alarm.”

Joon nodded and then said the thing he had been holding since the frame.

“The sector roster is the only place a name lives. 4C transfers, sector by sector, on paper, at the Spine depot, in a drawer with a custodian.” He pulled his collar up against the rain that had started. “The Office can read that drawer. We can only ask to look at it. And if we ask in writing, they will answer in writing, and the answer will arrive in a version they chose.”

Elias thought about a bell that had been ringing for forty-four years in the rain with nobody listening, and about a page lifted out of a felt-lined slot by somebody who knew the clip, and about the seven years between the issue of a credential and the morning it was used.

“Then we do not ask in writing,” he said.

Joon looked at him.

“Tomorrow,” Elias said. “Somebody goes to the depot as a person with a reason, and asks the clerk what 4C's transfers looked like, and the answer is hers and not ours.”

“That is asking in writing with better manners.”

“It is asking a person. It is a different thing and it is the only thing we have left today.”
