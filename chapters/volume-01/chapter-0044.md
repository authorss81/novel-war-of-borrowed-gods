# Chapter 44 — Split Authority

Ru Sabar's card ran from seven in the morning on Friday and ran out at six in the evening, and Inez wrote both times on the card in her own hand before it went in the envelope, and when she read the board at seven she read the times as well.

“This is her card, for the machine shop's compressor, and it powered the camera and the monitor and the corridor repeater yesterday. It was a yes at seven on Thursday morning and she gave me one condition, and the condition is a time.” She held it up. “*Give it back on Friday. It is not mine after six.* And that is not a figure of speech. If it is in the envelope at ten past six tonight with a second day written on it, then what she gave me on Thursday was not a card, it was a bridge, and I have spent a month telling this hall that a withdrawal which can be resumed is not a withdrawal.”

“So the instruments have power until six,” Elias said.

“And after six the corridor repeater goes with them, which means that at ten past six on Friday evening the man at the wall is off the wire whether anybody wants him on it or not.” She put the card down. “I want that on the board in those words. I do not want to be the one looking up and explaining it to him on Monday.”

---

Perry Dunne came at eleven with his toolbox and did not knock, because he has never once knocked, and he put the box down in the middle of everything, which is a thing he does when he is about to say one thing and then fix something.

“The week was for me and my shop and a street with nobody on it, and what I actually said both times was that this building has rearranged itself around not needing two men.” He took his hand off the box. “I am giving it to whoever ends up carrying a body out of that hole, and I am not asking to be told who, because if you tell me I will have an opinion and I do not want the opinion.” He picked the box up. “It was the only thing I ever asked for and I have just given it away on a Friday morning. That is my business and not a contribution, and Petar can write it down as a fact and not as a favor.”

Petar Lisk wrote it down as a fact.

---

The map was on the table at one in the afternoon, and it was drawn by Emil Baros, and nobody had asked him to draw it and everybody understood by two o’clock why it had to be him.

He had been the keeper at the eleven-meter mark for thirteen days. He had stiff paper from a survey pad, a carpenter's pencil, and a ruler he had taken off the hall wall because the hall's ruler is a thing that gets used. He had drawn the run to scale from the first measurements taken on the second day, and put in the dip, the quarter inch of water, and the brick lining at nine-two, and beside it, in his own hand, *survey, Wednesday, Ruzicka, three frames, chain 4C.*

He had not drawn the far twenty meters. There was nothing to draw, nobody asked him to, and Petar Lisk noted later that the map contained no absent thing, which was the first time in a month a document in that building had managed that.

Elias Rook looked at it for four minutes and said nothing, which is a thing people in that hall had learned to read. Then he picked up the pencil.

“Three ways in,” he said. “The run is eleven meters and is sealed at eleven by a wall a man has stood behind for twenty-five days and will not talk about, and I am not going to use it. The apron is out because of the 2038 pour and the crack and Tomas's number and two hundred and ten meters of void somewhere under this street that nobody has ever been told where. So the third.”

He drew it in one line, from the dip at nine-two, through the brick lining, into the chamber.

“That is the whole of it. Nine meters of corridor, a wall, a chamber. The wall is a meter and a bit thick, and if we open it nobody goes round anything, and the crew goes in the way they came in and comes out the way they came in, and nobody is on a rope over a void whose edge we cannot find. I have been building that plan since six this morning and I have got it, and I am going to say it out loud, and somebody is going to stop me.”

Nobody stopped him, because he had handed it to the room, and rooms do not always take a thing you hand them.

“Inez,” Elias said. “Power. The hole is open and the camera is in the chamber and the monitor is on a person and the repeater is on the wall. How long can you hold that.”

And Inez Sato said the thing that ended the plan, and she said it in seven words and she had known it since nine in the morning.

“The hall is dark at two.”

---

“You have written a night operation,” Inez said.

“An operation that will take most of a night.”

“An operation that will take most of a night, built around a card that expires at six, and you have not once asked the hall what it looks like at two, and I have been waiting since nine o'clock for you to ask me, and I want it in the record that I waited.” She did not raise her voice. “Four carded draws a day. Two of them are a lamp, a cooler and a pump. The clinic margin runs the east ward from six to six. At two the building goes dark, and it has gone dark at two for a month, and you have never once built a plan around it.”

“I do live here.”

“Then you know it and you did not use it, which is worse.” She put her hand flat on the drawing. “If your operation runs through two o'clock then this hall goes dark at two. Not the crew. Not the instruments. This hall, the four households on the card, and a nineteen-year-old on a powered frame who cannot turn himself.”

“Can you hold the power to four,” Elias said.

“No.” She did not soften it or explain it. “I could hold it to four by taking the machine shop's card and writing a second day on it, and Ru Sabar has told me twice on two separate mornings that the card is hers and not mine, and if I write a second day on it because a plan needed it then I have taken a card off a woman who told me where the line was.”

The room did the thing it had learned to do, which was to be quiet for exactly long enough to be uncomfortable.

“So the operation is a daylight operation,” Elias said.

“The operation is whatever the power allows,” Inez said, “and the power allows daylight, and daylight is now seventeen hours, and I am telling you that the plan you have just drawn cannot run from first light to six because the card says six, and so the plan has to be a plan for the hours between, and I would like the plan to say that in those words and not to say *as available*.”

---

Mara took it next, and she did not go after the power or the rope or the wall.

“You have been saying *the retrieval* for three days,” she said. “I have let you, because I was tired and because it is a small thing. It is not a small thing.” She turned the map around so the line Elias had drawn pointed at her. “Retrieval is a word about objects. You retrieve a body. You retrieve a document. You retrieve a thing that has a location and no opinions. And you have drawn a line through a wall into a chamber with six people in it and one that will not lie still, and you have called the line *the retrieval*, and if that goes in a document then somewhere in four years there will be a piece of paper that says the retrieval failed, and the paper will not say whose fault, and the fault will be arithmetic.”

“Point taken,” Elias said, and he did not sound like a man who had been taken from.

“Take all of it. I will give you the rest for free because I want it in a document.” She put her hand next to his line on the map. “I will not write a protocol for those seven people, or a triage order in advance, or a sentence in any document saying what a person in that chamber is before anybody has looked at that person. What happens to a person between that wall and this street I decide in the corridor, out loud, one at a time, and it is not written down first. If you want it in the plan, the plan says *the physician decides, and the physician has not decided, and you will be told when she has.*”

Emil Baros wrote that down on the map in pencil, in his own hand, in the margin, and nobody had asked him to write anything down all afternoon, and he did not stop for eleven minutes.

---

Elias put the pencil down at ten past three and stopped building.

“There is a version of this week where I am the one who has the whole plan in his head,” he said, “and I draw it for you, and every one of you can see the shape of it and tell me which piece is wrong, and at the end of the day there is a plan nobody in this room disagrees with, and it is mine, and I built it out of five people's information and my own arithmetic, and I took the decisions that belong to all five of you and put them in one man's head, in a way everybody calls collaboration.” Nobody said anything. “I did that for eight days this month and it is the reason the eleven meters is eleven meters. Not the seam. Me. I drew one perfect plan and it cost twenty meters of corridor and a man who has been behind a wall for twenty-five days, and I have spent every day since telling this room the problem was authority and not ground, and I would like somebody to check whether that is still true, because I no longer know.”

He looked at the five.

“I am going to say the sentence I have been not saying since Wednesday morning, and I want it in this room, in daylight, before we make anything. There is no plan I can draw that gets everybody out. There is no version of Thursday, Friday, or Saturday where seven people come out of that chamber and nobody stays in it, and anybody who tells you there is one is selling you a line on a map, and I have been that man three times in this month, and the third time it cost a corridor and a family and a name in a form.” He put his hand flat on the drawing. “So we are going to go and open a wall with a plan that cannot promise every person a safe outcome, and the plan says that, in those words, in the hand of a person who is not me, and it expires.”

---

Emil Baros drew the operating plan at four o'clock in the afternoon on the back of the drill-hours sheet, traced it in with chalk so the residents could add to it, which is the only way a plan on that wall has ever been allowed to exist.

It had a name at the top that was not Elias's.

**KESTREL CHAMBER. FIRST ENTRY. DRAWN BY E. BAROS, KEEPER, AS OBSERVER TO THE THIRTEENTH DAY.**

It carried the five domains with a name under each, and the power column with the dark hours written in as *0200–0400, not moved, and here is what is on the ward at 0200*, and Ru Sabar's name with a time against it, and the second copy going out of the building on Sunday on a bus.

It carried a fresh expiry, which was the first time an operating plan in that building had ever had one.

**THIS PLAN EXPIRES AT 0600 ON SUNDAY, OR WHEN THE FOURTH PERSON FROM THAT CHAMBER IS THROUGH THE WALL, WHICHEVER IS EARLIER. IT DOES NOT CARRY OVER. SOMEBODY HAS TO WRITE A NEW ONE AND IT WILL NOT BE THIS ONE.**

And it carried a fresh stop page, printed that afternoon, with six lines on it, and the first line was a name the team had not had to be given.

**TEODOR. IN THE BUILDING. STOPS A CHAIN, A DRAW, A SURVEY, AN ENTRY, OR AN OPERATION, WITH NO REASON REQUIRED, AND NOBODY CAN OVERRULE HIM AND THE HALL CANNOT. HE HOLDS NO CARD. HE IS THE SIXTH NAME AND THE ONLY ONE WHO IS NOT ON A LIST.**

Petar Lisk read the whole thing out at seven, all of it, including the times, and the last line he read was not on the plan and was not in his hand and was in the ward page in Lena Mir's handwriting, from Thursday morning, under the day.

**THE PERSON WHO DECIDES WHAT HAPPENS TO THESE FOUR IS NOT YET KNOWN. SHE WILL BE AT THE MOUTH OF THE HOLE AND SHE WILL NOT HAVE DECIDED, AND YOU WILL BE TOLD WHEN SHE HAS.**
