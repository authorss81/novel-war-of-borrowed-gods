# Chapter 23 — The Listening Campaign

They started at ten because ten was when the batteries were warmest and the hard cut was at four, and Inez wrote both on the board and Petar Lisk announced both on Ash Street with a megaphone he had been given on Friday and had not given back.

The team was four people, which was the most the access instrument allowed and the fewest that anybody liked. Elias, Joon, Rafi on the line, and a gas tech from the Meridian named **Blythe Okonjo**, who had a gas meter and no opinions about anything except her gas meter. Mara was at the top with the cylinder and she said so out loud, in the hall, in front of thirty-one people, because the condition had been hers and the record was going to be hers.

“No medic on the line,” she said. “If anybody is found, treatment happens where they are found, in their air, at their pace, and it happens because they are there and not because we arrived with it. I am at the top. I have a cylinder and I have a hard cut and I am not going down that ramp today.”

“Conversation, not an extraction,” Inez said. “Petar, say the rest of it.”

Petar read it off his clipboard, badly, and with total authority. *If the line goes quiet and nothing is said, the result is no contact, and no contact goes on the wall with the same weight as the knock, and nobody is to write almost in any document in this building.*

Then Elias read the new stop page, which had a date on it and five names, and one of the five names was the hall.

---

The lower junction had not changed, which after a week was itself a thing to be grateful for and not a thing to write down as a result.

The altered wall was where it had been. The horizontal gap near the floor let in a strip of daylight that had nothing behind it, and the Civic Spine terminal still marked the space as solid concrete, and the gas curve did the same slow smooth thing it had done on Wednesday, oxygen down and carbon dioxide up in a room that had been shut long enough for the air to have opinions.

Camera one read two human-sized signatures at four and a half meters, weak, three meters apart.

Camera two read one, at the wall, and a bloom at nine meters that might have been a person and might have been a hot pipe.

Joon wrote both, exactly as he had written them on Wednesday, in the same words, and the fact that they still said the same thing was the best news anybody had all day.

“No knocking,” Elias said. “No talking first. We listen for an hour, then we say one thing, then we listen again. If nobody says anything back we stop at four and we write *no contact*, and it goes on the wall with the knock.”

“And if something is said?” Blythe asked.

“Then we write *contact made* and nothing about who, and nothing about how many, and nothing about what condition anybody is in. We cannot know any of that from a hole in a wall and I am not going to let us guess it into a document that outlives us.”

---

The first hour was water.

Water pushed through the floor gap in short pulses carrying dust and the smell of burned cable, and every ninety seconds the strip lighting along the junction ceiling made a small noise it had made every ninety seconds since Saturday, and somewhere above them the surface went about its business and a train that was not involved came through four hundred meters of city and made the floor hum.

Joon wrote the times. Elias sat with his back against a rib and his notebook open on his knee and did nothing at all, which was the hardest thing in the plan.

At eleven hundred and twelve Joon's pencil stopped.

“Was that something,” Elias said.

“That was a relay,” Joon said, writing. “Thirty-ninety line. It does it about every ninety seconds and I have been counting it since Wednesday and it is not a person.”

“Write what it is, then.”

“I am writing what it is. *Acoustic event, unidentified.* That is the honest line. If I write *possible contact* then in a month somebody reads it in a building we cannot get into and concludes that a person was here on Sunday, and we did not know a person was here on Sunday.”

At two o'clock Elias said, into the recorder, that a person was behind the wall and that the team was listening and would not come in, and then stopped talking, and then sat for another forty minutes with the microphone on the concrete and nothing coming back.

The truth was that he had wanted to stop at two, and that he did not want to admit it at four, and that the wanting was not a reason and was not going to be a reason and had better not be written down anywhere.

Nobody asked Halla Dren anything. Elias said that out loud at two fifteen, to nobody, in case anybody in the room had been thinking it, and Joon said “no” and put it in the log with the time, and the log said *no contact with H. Dren sought or offered. Prior request declined and recorded.*

---

At eleven minutes to three, the voice came.

It was not a shout and it was not a whisper. It was a man who had been talking to concrete for a long time and had learned to put words out at a size that traveled, and it arrived through the bore line at about the volume of somebody two rooms away, and it said:

“You are the third group. The second one knocked in code for eleven minutes and then went away and I have been listening to you breathe since you came down.”

Joon's hand was on the recorder and off it and on it again. Elias felt every muscle in his back take up at once and made himself put them down again, because the fastest way to lose this was to sound like a rescue.

“Left hand's gone,” he said. Nobody needed it. He said it because the practice was the practice.

“My name is not the first thing,” the voice said. “You have got somebody in a clinic who needs to know that I am on my feet, and I cannot prove it and neither can you, so do not write it. Write that there is a voice. That is all I am asking you to write. Do not write how many of us there are, because the answer is not a number you can use and the number that is in your camera is a guess.”

“Write *voice at the wall, no count, no condition*,” Elias said to Joon.

“Writing it.”

“We are not coming in,” Elias said. “Say that back to me so that it is on a machine and not just in my head.”

“You are not coming in. I have written it down. I have a piece of this wall I can scratch on, which is more than you have.”

Elias understood, in the middle of everything else, that the man had been keeping records longer than they had, and that this was not a small thing, and that he could not ask about it because the boundary he had just been given was that nothing is being asked about the person.

Then the terminal on the wall woke up.

```text
Recommended re-entry: NORTH SERVICE ROUTE. Access currently unavailable.
Aftermath ahead: route will not survive another opening.
```

Elias said nothing. He had spent a week learning what the sound of his own wanting to answer a machine sounded like from the outside, and he heard it, and he kept his mouth shut.

Mara's voice came down the ramp line from the top, flat and immediate. “I saw it. Nobody touches that. It has been on our screen since the second week and it is a sentence written by a person who wants a crew down there.”

“It says the route will not survive another opening,” Elias said.

“It says the route will not survive another opening,” Mara said. “Which is either a warning or an advertisement, and you cannot tell which from a box on a wall, and in a few minutes you have a hard cut.”

---

The man came back at three twenty, and he sounded tired in a way that had nothing to do with the hour.

“You are still there,” he said. “Good. Do not go and ask the woman who runs shafts.”

“We are not going to ask her,” Elias said. “We refused her once and it is written down and we are not going to go back and make her answer for it.”

“Then you are the first people who have understood anything.” A pause. “I want to tell you something and I want you to hear it as a want and not as a request, because I cannot ask you for anything. I have nothing to give you and I am not going to pretend otherwise. Do you want to hear it.”

“Yes.”

“I use your corridor.”

The words came out plainly, with no drama in them at all, and Elias found that he had to put his hand on the concrete to be sure he was touching something.

“The lower service run. The damp one. The strip at the ankle height and the fall in the floor at nine meters and the door at the far end with two handles on it,” the voice said. “People come through it. Not many. Not a group. But they come through it, and they are not on any list of yours or anybody's, and when they come through they are on the other side and they keep going, and that is the entire arrangement.”

“How many people.”

“I told you. The number is not a number you can use. What I am telling you is that it is not empty, and that it was not empty on Saturday when I came this way, and that this afternoon I watched a man of sixty-eight go down it with a clipboard and sign his own name at the bottom, and that there was a woman from the Office at the top of the ramp with a camera. That is all I can see from here. I cannot see your hall. I can tell you that a man who walks a road and signs it is a man who has decided the road is a fact, and that is worth more to me than your instruments, and I suspect it is worth more to you than your instruments, and I would not say so out loud if I were you.”

Elias looked up the ramp toward the daylight, and could not see anything.

“Emil Baros is a resident,” he said. “He is a survivor. He was in the alcove.”

“There were seven in that alcove,” the voice said. “I am not going to tell you which one I was, and I would ask you not to want to know, because the wanting is what brings the rest of you down here.” “I am going to stop now, because you have been decent about this and I do not want to spend it. Three things. One: keep it on the paper. All of it, the length, the keeper, the photographs, the witness, all of it in every building you can reach and in the one you cannot. A road that is only in a list is a road that gets closed by somebody who never had to walk it. Two: nobody comes in. I am telling you the shape of it so that you do not do the kind thing. If one person goes into that run, it shuts. Not because of you. Because a run with a body in it is a run that gets closed by people who are frightened of runs, and I have watched it happen to other people. Three: do not write about me. Not the name, not the count, not how I am. Write *voice at the wall.* If you write more, then in a year somebody will come down here to find out whether I am all right, and that is the thing I am asking you not to do.”

“And the day after tomorrow somebody could close it anyway.”

“Somebody could,” the man said. “The Office could. The board could. Something in the ground two hundred meters from your clinic could turn into an emergency in a month, and the emergency would not care whose name is on the keeper line. So do not expect me to say the system is on your side. The system is a list. You have spent a week being honest with lists. I have spent eleven days on the other side of one, and the difference between the two of us is not courage, it is which side of the concrete we happen to be on.”

Then he said: “You should say something now so that I know it was a person and not a machine reading my words back,” and Elias said, “This is Elias Rook, and it is forty minutes to my hard cut, and I am not coming in, and the corridor is on the paper in three places and it will be in five by Tuesday, and I have a notebook and my name is on all of it,” and the man said, “Good,” and the talking stopped, and the concrete went back to being concrete.

---

They were on the surface and accounted for at four, and Petar Lisk checked them off against the stop page, and the batteries went onto the cooperative charger one at a time by hand.

Joon wrote the entry while the others were still taking their boots off, and he read it out before he initialled it.

**Sunday, 14:51 to 16:00. Lower junction, bore line, passive listening. No medic on the line. No entry. No door, no break, no opening. Result: contact made. One voice at the wall. No name. No count. No condition recorded, because none was given. Terminal displayed a recommended re-entry during contact. It was not actioned. The recommendation is on the record and was refused. No contact with H. Dren sought or offered. Prior request declined and recorded. Purpose, in the words of the voice: the lower service run is in use by people who are not on a list, and a run that is only in a list gets closed by somebody who never had to walk it.**

He put the pen down.

“Nothing in that says *almost*,” he said.

“Nothing in it says *almost*,” Elias said. “It says a man is alive and he is on his feet and he has been for eleven days, and he has asked us for one thing, and the one thing is that we do not make him into a rescue.”

Inez wrote the finding on the board in chalk, and underneath it she wrote the want, in the man's words, and did not tidy them.

**HE WANTS THE RUN ON THE PAPER. HE WANTS NOBODY IN IT. HE WANTS US NOT TO WRITE HIM DOWN.**

Petar Lisk read it out three times because that is what Petar Lisk does with a line the room has to remember, and then the boy put the lamp down on the sill and went out into Ash Street to see whether the repeater was still carrying anything, and the last thing Elias saw him do before the lights went was take the card off the wall and fold it in half, so that the man behind the wall would never know how many people had read it.

Inez wrote one more line underneath, in chalk, and it was not for anybody outside.

**WE HAVE BEEN ASKED WHETHER WE ARE ALLOWED TO DO THE ONLY THING ANYBODY HAS ASKED US FOR THIS WEEK.**
