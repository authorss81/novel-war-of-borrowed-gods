# Chapter 12 — The Map Under Dispute

By one in the afternoon the long table had become a hearing room without anybody deciding to make it one.

Joon had ruled the surface with a broom handle and hung the paper map over the radiator with two clothes pegs. Survivors sat where they could see it. The Civic Spine terminal sat on the opposite end of the table with its screen tilted up, and one of Sable's staff members sat beside it with a folded copy of the field survey, and the other stood at the door with his arms folded, and nobody asked them to leave.

Elias stood at the head of the table with his notebook open and a pencil in his right hand, and he discovered within two minutes that he could not do what he had come to do.

The service door was forty-one inches at the frame, twenty-seven at the narrowest point, set in a lintel that leaned about six degrees toward the corridor. It had two handles at two heights. It had a scrape mark along the lintel that was bright metal on old grey concrete, and it had eleven wet footprints in the trough of the floor, and it had a mechanical anchor scar where his own wedge had bitten. All of that was in his notebook in his own handwriting.

All of it, if he drew it, was going to be a picture of a door instead of a measurement of a wall.

His left hand was resting on the table near the paper. He told it to hold a pencil. It held nothing.

“Left hand's gone,” he said, out loud, in the middle of a room.

Joon was already moving. He put a steel rule in Elias's right hand and a fresh sheet under it and said, “Say the numbers. I will write them.”

“I can draw the gaps.”

“You can draw the gaps badly and then they are evidence of a bad drawing. Say the numbers.”

Elias read them out. Joon wrote them, and Anja Ro, who had spent the morning checking names against the manifest, read them back as he went, and by the fourth measurement the room had joined in and was calling out corrections — this is wrong, that is the wrong wall, the handle was higher than that, I was on the left side, I passed on the right.

He handed the pencil to Joon.

In the tunnel that had felt like losing a tool. Here, at a table with thirty people watching, it felt like something else. He had spent six years believing that a rescuer's value was in the thing he could do that nobody else could do, and the afternoon had been teaching him, one small failure at a time, that the thing worth protecting was the record — and that a record belonged to whoever could check it.

The not-quite-oral admission sat badly in him. He let it sit.

---

The first contradiction had arrived with the survivors, and it had arrived before anyone opened a terminal.

The young woman from Car 3 — Petar's neighbor, who had refused to move at first and then crossed — stood up when Joon asked for the corridor.

“There was a stair,” she said. “Concrete, with a green sign. It said NINE. And there was a fire cabinet on the left, and it was red, and I remember the handle because the paint had gone at the bottom of it.”

Niko Senn shook his head. “I was behind you. That was a wall. I put my hand on it. Cold, with a joint in the middle, like the panels by the ticket machines.”

“You did not pass me,” the woman said.

“You were not in front of me. You were in front of me.”

“Emil was in front of me,” said Emil Baros, from the kitchen door. “And I remember a stair.”

Niko put his hands flat on his knees. “Then one of us is lying, and it is not me, because I have a brace on my leg that I could not have removed.”

“You are not lying,” Elias said. “You are describing different corridors. The question is whether you are describing different times, different directions of travel, or different branches, and we cannot answer that from this table.”

The woman looked at him with real anger. “I am not going to be told what I saw.”

“You are not. I am telling you what I can write. I can write that four people remember a stair, that one of them remembers a fire cabinet and one of them remembers a wall, and that all four of you came out of the same carriage through the same door. That is what happened. The rest of it is unresolved and it stays unresolved on this paper.”

Joon wrote **MEMORY CONFLICTS UNRESOLVED — FOUR WITNESSES — DO NOT MERGE** across the top of the sheet, in letters large enough to be read from the cots.

Anja Ro stood up next. She had been the woman in the orange coat, and the one behind the glass with both palms flat against it.

“That was me on the platform,” she said. “I called your name because I had watched the door for eleven minutes and it did nothing until you touched it. That is all I know that you do not know.”

“And the figure behind you.”

“I could not see a face. The carriage lights went along from the front and he was in the last window and he never moved when the lights reached him. He was not breathing the way the rest of us were.” She sat down. “I have thought about it since. I have not slept. That is the most useful thing I have.”

Nobody wrote that down. Joon wrote the part with a time on it, and Elias made him add the rest, and Joon added it in smaller letters and said, without looking up, that the record was not a place for people to be tidy.

---

The terminal was the third thing at the table, and it behaved the way the tunnel had behaved, which was to say it behaved correctly.

Joon asked it for the Kestrel route history for the incident window. It answered with a single clean line: a route from the west apron to the surface ramp, verified, no intermediate connections, and a passenger count field reading zero for Car 3.

“Sixteen assigned,” Elias said. “Fifteen in the carriage. One found below. And seven people from a service alcove who were never in the carriage at all.”

“The intermediate connections are not in the verified set,” the staff member said. He said it patiently. He was perhaps thirty and he had a smooth bandage on his forearm where the door had opened on him. “When a seam is active, the Spine suppresses anything it cannot reconcile against the surveyed network. That is not a policy decision. It is what happens to the index when a place stops agreeing with itself.”

“Fine,” Elias said. “Then show me the suppression, not the result.”

Joon typed it in. The terminal paused, and then the screen changed.

```text
Suggested correction: CAR 3 SERVICE — no verified connection.
Prior entries in this window will be removed to match.
```

The room read it over his shoulder.

“That is the whole argument in one box,” Elias said. “Delete the entries and the index is true. Everything we have — the scrapes, the prints, the handle on the wrong side, the anchor scar I made with my own chisel — becomes an error, because the index would no longer contain anything for it to disagree with.”

“If the correction is accepted, the route will read as continuous and the report will be consistent,” the staff member said. “Right now the report says a train emptied into a tunnel that had a door in it. That is not a thing I can file.”

“Your report already says Car 3 was empty.”

He looked at the floor. “It does.”

Elias turned back to the table. “Do not accept it.”

Joon shook his head. “I cannot accept it. Accepting it writes a correction into the incident history and the old entries go, and when somebody asks in three weeks why the paper and the screen disagree, the answer is that the paper is out of date. That is how it starts.”

He photographed the screen with the paper visible, then wrote the suggestion out in his own hand and signed it and dated it, and put the sheet under the battery lamp where everyone could read it.

**INDEX PROPOSED DELETION OF 05:42–06:18 ENTRIES. NOT ACCEPTED. DRAFTED BY J. PARK.**

---

The recorder went on at twenty past two because Joon insisted the room hear the raw file rather than his summary of it.

Static first. Then a relay clicking over something large and wet. Then a man's voice, stripped flat by distance, saying that a clean map means someone is standing on the missing people, and that the paper and the raw audio have to be kept, and that they must not make the map agree.

It was eleven seconds long. Nobody in the hall spoke through it.

“It does not come off the emergency channel,” Joon said afterwards. “I checked the carrier. It came in on the Kestrel maintenance relay, the same physical line as the north stair, from under this station. It is not a broadcast. It is not the Spine. Whatever is carrying it is inside the building.”

Emil Baros had come halfway up off his mat. “That is a crew voice,” he said. “Not a machine. I have listened to machines my whole life. Somebody is briefing somebody.”

“That is what Joon says too,” Elias said. “It is what we can say. We are not putting a name on it and we are not building a case out of a recording we cannot place.”

The woman's voice on the earlier raw file — Nell's, present-day and branch-crossed, the one that said the north stair was a branch and not an exit — Joon played once, quietly, for the room. He stopped it before the second half.

“That is Elias's sister,” he said. “Engineer. Transmitted after the carriage appeared, which means it crossed the seam while we were already underground. It is her present voice in this time. It is not a message from the future, and it is not a location.”

Elias did not look at the terminal. He looked at the map on the radiator, at the door drawn on it in a hand that was not his, and at the gap Joon had left in the line instead of joining it to a wall.

---

The thing the table produced, in the end, was not a map.

It was a page headed **SERVICE DOOR — WHAT IS PROVEN**, and the page had three columns, and the three columns were the whole moral argument of the afternoon.

**UNSAFE.** Proven. Structurally unusable as of 06:18. The frame carries a lateral load it was not designed for. The floor is settling. The lower panel has folded inward. Nobody goes through it.

**SHIFTED.** Possible. The frame has moved in depth. The handles are no longer on the same plane. The black gap behind the lintel measures deeper than the corridor it opens into. We cannot measure how much deeper, and we cannot measure it from this table.

**PHYSICALLY ABSENT.** Unproven. The concrete at the lintel is continuous, the old painted-over patch from the platform side is where it has always been, and the door-frame survey of 2029 still shows a door there. We have no instrument that can distinguish a wall that closed over an opening from a wall that was never cut, and we will not write one down as an absence we have established.

Joon read the three columns out. Then he read the line at the bottom, which he had written without asking anybody.

**These are three different states. A rescue record that collapses them into one is not more honest. It is less.**

Nobody voted on it. The two Office staff members read it, and the younger one, the one with the bandage, asked whether he could photograph it for the site file, and Joon said he could photograph it if the photograph carried the caption, and the young man said yes.

Elias put his hand flat on the paper with his right hand, in the space beside the third column, and did not fill it in.

He had drawn the door in a tunnel in under two minutes, and it had been the best drawing of his life. Handing the drawing to somebody else had cost him nothing he could not afford, and had bought him a page that twenty-three people had helped write.

---

The seal notice came at a quarter to three, brought in by the second staff member, who put it on the table face up this time.

SITE ARCHIVE AND CONTAINMENT — 1600 HRS. All Kestrel maintenance records to be transferred to Office custody. Original paper logs, circuit cards, and route books to be sealed with the structure.

Elias read it twice.

“The route book,” Joon said. He had gone very still. “The 05:17 entry is not on the Spine. It is written in a paper log that lives in a cabinet at the west apron relay, because the Spine has never had a clean copy of anything done to that sector.”

“Which means at four o’clock it goes into an Office evidence box in an Office warehouse,” Elias said.

“Which means at four o’clock it goes into a box we cannot open without a warrant and a supervisor and eleven days.”

The clock on the wall said a quarter to three. There were twelve hand-written names in a notebook in Elias's right hand, four of them just recovered from an old man who could not hold them in his head, and twenty-three people in the hall behind him who had spent the afternoon being told that the record belonged to them, and a maintenance cabinet out past the west apron that was about to be closed by people who were not lying to him and would still take the only copy of the question.

He looked at the page with the three columns, at the photograph of the terminal's suggested deletion, at the twenty-three names on the manifest, and at the sealed envelope in his coat pocket with a note to his sister in it that nobody had read.

“Tomas,” he said. “Tomas, get your coat.”

Nobody asked why.
