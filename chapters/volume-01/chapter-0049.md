# Chapter 49 — The Living Route

The ward opened at six on Wednesday morning and nobody in that building called it that until Nadi Bell read it off the wall at seven, by which time it had been running for an hour and everybody in it had known what it was.

Lena Mir did the round in fifty-two minutes with two nurse-assistants and no physician in the building, which had been the arrangement since the second week and was now the arrangement for twenty-eight people instead of twenty-two. Anja Ro carried water and then carried a board. Ru Sabar stood at the laundry door with a chair belt and a light for six hours because the corridor was wet and there was one person on it every forty minutes. Oren Pike ran the corridor line because he was the only person in the building who had said he could do it and had then learned it twice.

The Ambrose sisters were on two tables at the dry end with a curtain between them that Nadi Bell put up herself, because they had come out of the same chamber, one of them seven and one of them eleven, and nobody had asked whether they wanted to be in the same room and it turned out that they did, loudly, and Nadi wrote that down too.

Emil Baros had asked, out loud, in front of nineteen people, to be put on the sheet as a keeper, and Lena Mir had refused him the line and not the sheet, and he had accepted that, and then asked what he was instead, and been told he was going to be asked to walk a line eleven meters away. He said he would do it in the morning when it was light, which is the first thing anybody in that hall has heard him say about himself that was not a measurement.

The power came from two households on Bell Row. The laundry and the corridor repeater came off a card on Ash Street. The east ward came off the clinic margin until six.

---

Tomas did his arithmetic on the wall at ten past seven in the morning, in front of everybody, with a pencil and the board, and it took him forty minutes and it is in his own hand and it is nine lines long and it is the only structural document anybody in that building has ever seen a man refuse to shorten.

“There is a person on the floor of that chamber,” he said, “and she is nine meters under Ash Street and behind a wall we cut on Sunday, and I have been telling people since Saturday that I would not put a tool into a pour from 2038 with a crack in it, and that number is the reason this room exists, and it has not moved, and I am going to do the sum out loud instead of saying it again.”

“Nine lines,” Petar Lisk said.

“Nine lines. One: the shored face at nine-two is carrying the ceiling of that chamber, and if I take the shoring out the ceiling comes down on her, and that is not a risk, that is a certainty, and I put those jacks in myself and I signed them. Two: the load goes to the apron. Three: the apron has a pour in it from 2038 that is on no drawing in four buildings and a crack a meter and a half long that nobody is allowed to touch. Four: the 2031 contractor survey puts two hundred and ten meters of migrating void under this street and has never once said where. Five: I do not know which way it goes, and if I move the shoring and the ground answers, the answer will be a stamp, and a stamp is a sentence I cannot unwrite.” He put the pencil down and picked it up again, which he had not done in three weeks. “Six: I have been the man at the lip for six days and I am not going into that chamber, and the reason is not the crack. A man at the lip is a man who can be asked to stop something and a man in the chamber cannot. Seven: if I go past the shored face I put my own weight on a structure I have told nobody is stable, and there is a nineteen-year-old in this building with a power over my plans and I would like him to keep it. Eight: I have a form from the Meridian asking whether I intend to be available to give evidence at any future inquiry, and I have not answered it, and if I move the shoring that form becomes a question about my judgment instead of about a stamp in the ground, and I would like to keep it that way.”

He read the ninth line off the board, and then said the part that was not on the board.

“Nine. There is one person left in that chamber and I am not going to get her, and I have been telling this room since Tuesday morning that the operation is not complete, and I want it written in my name and my hand that I said it, because in six weeks somebody is going to stand in an inquiry and say *the neighborhood had a plan and the plan expired*, and the plan did not expire. I did.”

Nobody spoke for a while, and then Inez Sato said the sentence that made it a decision instead of an opinion.

“Then the plan is not going to be renewed for her,” she said, “and I am not going to ask you to renew it, and I am going to write on the board at seven o'clock tomorrow morning that the operating plan expires at six, and that it expires on the fourth person, and that there is a woman in that chamber at the time it expires, and I am going to write her name, and we are not going to be able to unwrite it, and I would rather it were on the wall in my hand than in a man’s mouth in a corridor.”

---

Joon Park's terminal came back at twenty past four on Wednesday afternoon, nineteen hours after it had gone, on the same carrier, and it did not come back the way it had gone.

It came back with a **Warning: the branch is changing** on the top line, which was the first time any of them had seen that heading in this building, and then eleven words, and then a gap of about four seconds of nothing, and then a voice.

Eleven words. Joon wrote them down as he heard them and read them back twice before he wrote anything else.

> *I am not the road. I am the person who lives beside it. Do not count us.*

“I am not going to say anything about that for about a minute,” Joon said, “and I would like everybody to notice that I said so, because I have watched this room build a meaning out of a Tuesday afternoon before, in a closet, on a lamp.”

“What is on the carrier,” Elias said.

“A relay handoff in the middle, four tenths of a second, the same length as the gap in the file from the nineteenth day, carrying a stamp.” He held the sheet up so the room could see it, which is what he does. “`K-7Q` and four digits. The same nine characters and a letter as the one in the node's copy, in a format that is not in the Havenport stamp set indexed to this file, seen once before, not placed. It is the second time. I am writing down that it is the second time and I am not writing down what it means, and if anybody says I knew, I will read this sheet out loud, and it is dated.”

“It is a branch-side relay and it is not a place,” he said. “And not a route. And not a person. And I am not going to be the man who says *do not answer her* and I am not going to be the man who says *go to her*. I am the man who wrote down the time.”

Elias Rook stood in a hall with a board on the wall and a person nine meters under the street and a lamp by the door, and he said the thing he had been building all day.

“She said it last Sunday and she said it in a different set of words and she was right both times,” he said. “*Do not count us.* And the relay she is on is a branch-side relay, which means there is a thing on her side that hands signals, and the only way I know to get to it is the way the Office has just spent three days offering me, and I have already refused it, and I am not going to un-refuse it at four in the afternoon on a Wednesday because the four people who refused it with me are tired.”

He put his hand flat on the terminal.

“Write this down and read it back. *Nell Rook is on a branch-side relay under the water, past the wall, on a stamped segment, speaking for people who are not on a list. She has told us twice not to count them. There is no place in this record that can be treated as home, and I am not going to travel toward a relay because a woman whose route I have already refused once is standing near one.*”

“That is a good sentence,” Mara said, from the far end of the room, and it was the first thing she had said to him in three days, and she said it the way she said the difficult things, which is out loud and without softening it.

“Write it down and read it back,” Elias said, “and put it in the book in her book, and then give me a piece of paper and a pencil, because I have to write something in a notebook and I have not been able to for three days and I am not going to be able to tomorrow either and I would like to do it while it is in my hand.”

---

The last person in that chamber was thirty-four years old and her name is **Perpetua Oyelaran** and she is still under Ash Street.

There is a difference between a refusal and an obstacle, and this hall knows which one it is looking at. The nine lines are in the record in his own hand and not a summary of it. Joon Park wrote the sentence that makes it a fact rather than a mystery.

Petar Lisk read the board at seven and read the last line twice because he had read it once and had not believed it, and then he wrote underneath it, in his own hand, in capitals nobody in that room could mistake for anybody else's.

**WE HAVE ONE PERSON UNDER THE STREET AND WE KNOW HER NAME.**

---

The ward held, and the count moved, and the count moved the way it always moves, which is that it goes down when somebody gets better and everybody hates it.

Nadi Bell wrote it on the board on Wednesday night in her own hand, under everything, in the part of the board Petar read first.

**WE ARE TWENTY-TWO. WE ARE ALSO TWENTY-EIGHT PEOPLE IN THIS BUILDING AND BOTH OF THOSE ARE TRUE AND ONLY ONE OF THEM IS A COUNT. THERE ARE SIX NAMES IN THE MOVEMENT RECORD THAT WERE NOT IN IT A WEEK AGO AND EVERY ONE OF THEM IS A PERSON AND NONE OF THEM IS A RESIDENT AND NONE OF THEM IS A NUMBER.**

The hall was dark at two. Petar Lisk had the lamp and Osei Kwarteng sat up with him for an hour and a half and said nothing, and at half past three Ansel Duarte called out from the frame that the corridor light was crooked, and Anja Ro got up and fixed it.

And the things the team wrote on the wall that night, under the keeper's line, in order, with a name against each one, because a list of things you will not do is the only kind anybody in that building trusts: they will not take the order back up. They will not stop the second copy of this week's record from leaving the building on a bus. They will not renew the operating plan. They will not put a bit in the 2038 pour. They will not ask the man at the wall anything.

And the last one, in Joon's hand, because it was a record statement and not a plan.

*No future person was asked for anything this week. Seven people were located. Six came out. One is under Ash Street, and we know her name, and the reason is written in full and is signed by the man who wrote it.*
