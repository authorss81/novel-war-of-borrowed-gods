# Chapter 32 — Tuesday

The refusal went in at nine in the morning in Inez's hand, on the back of the citizens' request because the Office's form did not have a box for it, with five signatures above it and forty-two residents' cards listed on a second page, of which fourteen said no and were on the sheet with their names.

The reasons were four lines and none of them was brave.

**THE SURVEY CONDITION REQUIRES A JOINT INSPECTION WITH A RESIDENT OBSERVER. THE HALL HAS NO TRAINED STRUCTURAL PERSON EXCEPT ONE MAN, WHO IS SIXTY-FOUR, AND NO CONFINED-SPACE ROPE PAIR, BECAUSE TWO OF THREE TICKETS IN THIS BUILDING WERE RECALLED TO A GAS MAIN ON THE THIRTEENTH.**

**THE LOWER SERVICE RUN IS ELEVEN METERS AND A MAN AT THE FAR END OF IT USES IT. GOING TO LOOK AT THE POUR MEANS GOING TO LOOK ALONG A RUN. LOOKING ALONG A RUN ON THE DAY AFTER A ROAD GOES MISSING IS A RESCUE.**

**A WOMAN NINETEEN YEARS AHEAD OF US TOLD US THAT A NAMED PLACE BECOMES A PLACE TO BE CLOSED, AND ASKED US NOT TO NAME IT, AND WE REFUSED HER ONCE AND RECORDED THE REFUSAL, AND WE MAY NOT ASK HER AGAIN.**

**WE WOULD RATHER HAVE A DOCUMENT THAT SAYS IN ITS OWN HAND WHY WE DID NOT ASK, THAN A PERIOD OF NOT ASKING THAT SOMEBODY READS IN TEN YEARS AS A DECISION.**

Joon read them out because that was his job, and Petar Lisk wrote them on the board in full, and then read the board out again in the hall at seven that evening to nobody in particular, which is what the board is for.

Nobody argued about the wording. The argument happened on Sunday and Monday and had been had twice, and the four lines were the residue of it, and Inez had cut the fifth line out three times.

Then Joon wrote the two numbers on the board side by side and underlined both of them, which nobody had asked him to do and which was the single most useful thing he did all day.

**SEVENTEEN DAYS SINCE THE COLLAPSE.**

**THE BOARD'S ELEVENTH DAY, COUNTED FROM THE SATURDAY, WHICH IS THE SAME TUESDAY.**

**THESE ARE NOT THE SAME NUMBER. NEITHER ONE IS THE OTHER ONE. DO NOT LET ANYBODY USE THE FIRST TO PROVE THE SECOND.**

The condition lapsed at ten past nine. The resident observer did not exist from that moment. The residents' copy of every measurement under the survey condition did not exist from that moment. The standing designation was unchallenged for a cycle, and the standing designation had a cause box in it that had arrived pre-ticked from a pack somebody opened before the first alarm.

Inez read that last part out twice and then said, “It is a yes that costs us the joint inspection. It is not a yes that costs us the run. The run is not ours and was never ours to spend. If somebody wants to make me feel clever about this I will put them on the ramp with a tape.”

---

They went to the Spine at half past eleven, because Brauer had said half past eleven on Friday and had said it in a way that meant she would do it at half past eleven whatever else happened that day.

She had. The bound ledger was on the flatbed with a book weight on it, open at the page with the 14:12 entry, and Ilse Brauer had a fresh pair of nitrile gloves on and a face that anybody who had ever run a records room could read from the door: she had already found the problem.

“The column runs past the edge of the glass,” she said, before anybody spoke. “That is not a fault in the ledger and it is not a fault in the scanner. The ledger is four columns wide because the office that wrote it in 2031 had a fifth field for a supervisor's initials that the current template does not have, and the field was dropped out of the print and not out of the book, so the column is still physically in the ledger and it is half an inch past the platen.”

“On Monday you did one pass,” Joon said.

“On Monday I did one pass because a page produced under a board's order goes into the node that afternoon whether anybody is reading it or not, and because I was filing it and not reading it, and because I initialed the time and did not look at the frame.” She said it the way she said the reading on instruments, out loud, before anybody used it. “I have put that in the log and I would like it to stay in the log, and I would like the next person who looks at my initials to understand that I am not a person who looks at frames.”

Nobody said anything about mercy, because she had not offered any and would not have accepted it.

“Can you do it properly,” Elias said.

“I can do it properly now. I have moved the book to the far edge of the glass, which takes the whole width of the page into two frames, and I am photographing rather than scanning, because a photograph of a bound page does not pretend the book is flat, and then I am stitching them. Two frames, one column.” She put her hands flat on the bed. “It will take six minutes and the stitch is done by hand and the hand is mine, and the result will be a document that has been touched by a person, which is a thing I am supposed to avoid.”

She did it. The room watched the two frames come up on the screen side by side, and Brauer aligned them on a fix mark in the printed ruling, and the fourth column was there.

The 14:12 entry came up in full: *Rout. arch. rev. — aisle 9 — 214* — and then four printed boxes' worth of ruled space with nothing in any of them.

Three days ago that column had not existed in any document in that building.

“Brauer,” Joon said. “On Monday, your copy of this page had no subject column at all. Which means that for three days there has been a record in this building of a box being opened on the day of the collapse with a time and a reason and no place where a person could have been written — and nobody in this building could see that it was missing. Is that right.”

“That is right,” Brauer said. “And now everybody who pulls aisle nine can see the field, and it is empty, and in about eleven months somebody with a reason will fill it in, accurately, about their own afternoon, and never once about the person who was not there.”

“I am not going to ask you to do anything about that,” Joon said. “I want to say it out loud in a room, and I want Hollis Vane in it, and I want it written that I said it out loud before the frame existed.”

Vane was standing at the end of the bench with his coat still on, because he had come from the Office and was going back to it and had not pretended otherwise. He looked at the stitched page for a while.

“A rescanned page is not the same as a page nobody looked at,” he said. “I want to be exact about why, because it is going to be read the other way round by somebody in the next six months and I would like the sentence in my mouth and not in a report. A page nobody looked at is a blank page. It is honest. Nobody has claimed anything about it and the damage is that there is nothing to claim. A rescanned page is a page somebody looked at, twice, on a Tuesday, with a hand on the stitching, and it now says *there was a subject and we did not write it down*.” He put a finger on the empty boxes without touching them. “I made this document worse and I made it true, and I do not know which of those the building prefers, and I have been doing this for nineteen years and the answer is always worse.”

“Did you read the whole book,” Elias said.

Vane looked at him.

“No,” he said. “I read the page. In nineteen years nobody has read the whole book, because the book is four thousand entries and the index works, and if the index works then the book is a thing you consult and not a thing you read, and that is the correct procedure and I would defend it in front of a board.”

---

The transit operations desk at twenty past two was Bex Amadi's, and Bex Amadi had been frozen on this file since the sixth day for reasons that were her protection and had never once been anybody else's good luck.

She had the confirmation in a printed tray with a clip on it and she slid it across and then sat back down, which is a thing clerks do when they have decided to be helpful and a thing they stop doing when they have decided to be helpful in a way that gets them noticed.

“Shuttle six,” she said. “The Saturday. Two runs, an early and a mid, and the routine confirmation came out of the system this morning and it is a count and not a manifest, because shuttle six was a count service and always was, and a count is what you get.”

Joon read it.

The early run was four. The mid run was five.

“The official building count for the west apron that morning is four,” Elias said. “That is the number in the incident file, and the number the Office has used ever since, and the number that came off the response list when the digital row was built.”

“Yes,” Bex Amadi said.

“Five on a bus. And the file says four in the building. So either the count on this form is wrong, or there was a fifth person in that building on the morning of the collapse and the file has been wrong for seventeen days.”

“Or,” Bex Amadi said, “the fifth person got on a bus that morning who was never in the building, and the count is right about the bus and the file is right about the building, and the two of them are both true and the thing that is missing is not a number, it is a manifest. And there is no manifest, because it was a count service, and a count service does not carry a count service passenger list, and it has not carried one since 2038, and nobody has missed it until this week.”

“Nine days,” Joon said. “On the sixth day you said nine days. It is seventeen.”

“It is seventeen,” Bex Amadi said, “and I would like that in the record in my words rather than yours, because here is the reason and the reason is a good one and I am not ashamed of it. I said nine. The freeze on any query that touches the incident went on at eleven forty on the sixth day, and a freeze that touches the incident is a freeze on the incident's own paperwork, and the paperwork sits in the queue behind the freeze, because the paperwork *is* the incident as far as the queue is concerned. Every day the freeze is on, that confirmation moves one place further back. It has been moving one place a day since the sixth day and it came out today because a clerk in rotation who has never met any of you felt like being helpful.”

Nobody in the room said thank you to Bex Amadi, because thanking her for a thing that had cost a woman her row for five days past the promise would have been obscene, and Bex Amadi, who had been doing this for twenty-two years, looked like somebody who had noticed that and was grateful.

“That is the confirmation,” she said. “It is a count. It is true. It is on a form. And it does not have a name in it anywhere in the world, and I am not permitted to go and look for one, because looking for one is a query that touches the incident, and I have a job.”

---

They walked it back to Ash Street in the last of the light and Nadi Bell was on the step of the hall with the child's school coat over her knees, in the cold, at ten past four, in a way that everybody who had been in that hall for seventeen days had learned not to comment on.

Joon put the confirmation on the table in front of her without a preamble, which was the respect she had earned and also the only way to talk to her.

She read it twice.

“Five,” she said. “Then she was on the bus.”

“She was on a bus. That is now a fact with a form under it and not a guess, and I am sorry that the fact is still not a name.”

“It was never going to be a name from that desk,” Nadi said. “It was never going to be a name from any desk. Do you know what a count is, Joon? It is a number in a box, and a number in a box cannot lose anybody. A name can be lost, which is what happened. A count cannot, because there is nobody in it to lose. They did not remove my sister from a record. They were *never in the record*. They were a number that went down to four because a number is what you write when a person gets in the way of the count, and every person who got in the way of the count got subtracted, and nobody had to decide anything, and there is a form.”

She put her hand flat on it.

“Do you want me to tell you to push,” she said. “I will not, and I want it noted that I am the one who does not want you to push, and that in this room that is the least listened-to position a person can take.”

“I was not going to,” Joon said. “I wanted to hear you say it.”

“Then hear it properly, because I am not protecting the Office.” She took her hand off the paper. “Push on this and the freeze stays on and the confirmation goes back in the queue, and the next clerk who feels helpful is a clerk who feels helpful in six weeks. And every day the freeze is on is a day the thing that removed her is still in charge of the paperwork about the thing that removed her, and I have watched that logic eat a street.”

“Then what is left.”

“What is left is that the count is now five and it is on a form and it is in four buildings,” Nadi said. “That is not nothing. For seventeen days the answer to *how many people were in that building* was a number in a warm room, and now it is a number that says five, in four buildings, with my sister's arithmetic on the same sheet, and a date on it. In six weeks somebody in that building is going to have a reason to fill in a field, because the field is now visible, and when they do, I want the count to be already wrong on paper in a place they cannot reach.”

She picked up the school coat and stood up.

“Do not make me hopeful,” she said. “I have been hopeful in this city before and it cost me a row.”

---

At seven Petar Lisk read the board, and the fourth line from that afternoon had not been changed by anybody, and it was not Joon's and it was not Elias's, and it had been on the board since two o'clock because a four-year-old next door could write.

**THEY HAVE FOUND OUT YOU CAN SEE A BLANK NOW. THAT MEANS SOMEBODY ELSE CAN TOO. WE DO NOT KNOW HOW LONG THAT TAKES.**

And underneath it, in Inez's chalk, because the hall dark begins at two in the morning and somebody has to write what the day bought:

**REFUSAL FILED. THE JOINT INSPECTION IS GONE. WE KNEW WHAT IT COST WHEN WE CHOSE IT.**
