# Chapter 15 — The Local Vote

Inez Sato called the meeting for seven in the evening because seven was when the Quarter's second shift came home and could be angry in person.

The agenda was one line on the board by the battery room door: **FEEDER BANK 4 / REFUGE LOAD / REQUESTS FROM THE RESCUE TEAM — 1 ITEM.** Underneath, in smaller letters, she had written **POWER IS NOT A GIVEN. POWER IS A WEEKDAY.**

The hall filled in stages. Lena Mir came first, still in scrubs, and sat near the front because she had worked the clinic's night shift for eleven years and could tell in the dark who was lying. Petar Lisk came with his clipboard and sat on a crate in the doorway, because he had discovered that a clipboard makes a nine-year-old into a person with a job. Niko Senn came with his brace. Nadi Bell came and sat at the back with the school coat over her knees. Ansel Duarte was carried down on a door panel by two residents and propped against the radiator, and when somebody began to move people so he could see, he said, “Don't,” and they stopped.

Perry Dunne came late and stood at the back with his arms folded, and Inez did not ask him to move, and did not look at him.

The Office's generator truck was parked on the street outside. It had been there since four o'clock, idling nothing, and its presence was on everybody's mind in a way that nobody said out loud.

Elias came in with a map under his arm.

“Inez,” he said. “I want to put a survey proposal to the room. It is one page. It is bounded, it is on our own time, and it needs a hard cut at six so your reserve bank is not touched. I can put it up in—”

“No,” Inez said.

He stopped.

“You have got as far as *proposal*,” she said, “and you have said *needs* and you have said *hard cut*, and both of those are you telling me what my power is for. Sit down. This is not a briefing. This is the room you are asking.”

There were forty-odd people in the hall and at least nine of them were not the rescue team, and Elias discovered that he had no idea what a room like that wanted to hear. He had spent eight years being the one who knew how long the window was.

He sat down.

The first twenty minutes belonged to the residents, and none of it was about him.

A woman named **Ru Sabar** stood up and said that she ran the laundrette on Ash Street and that her bank of this hall's power had been in place since the eleventh of the month, and that she had not asked and had not been asked, and that she wanted it written down that nobody had asked her. Inez wrote it down on the board while Ru Sabar watched.

Then a man she called **Teodor** said that the Office was parked on his street, and that when the Office is parked on your street, the things you do on your street are visible, and that he did not want his block to become a place where a form gets filled out about his laundry. He wanted the truck moved for the hours the team was inside the building. Inez said she would ask, not promise, and Teodor said ask, then.

Then Perry Dunne.

“I want to say the other thing,” he said, and the room went quieter than it had been all evening. “I am not angry at the rescue team. I am angry that the second day of my life back I am sitting here having this conversation, because a road came apart under a railway nobody uses. My shop has no power. My freezer is on my neighbour's and my neighbour is in a bad way. And every day this building has a reason, the reason is the tunnel, and when the tunnel is finished the building will have had its whole life reorganised around a hole in the ground.”

“No,” Inez said.

“There it is again.” Perry almost smiled. “Fine. I have said it. Now the vote.”

Then Lena Mir stood up.

“I will say the sensible thing and then I will go sit down,” she said. “There is a reasonable case for a short survey, and it is not a rescue and it should not be called one, and the people who are hurt will not be any less hurt tonight whether we vote for it or not. If we say yes, we are not voting that anybody goes down there. We are voting that if somebody competent comes and says the safe way to look is the boring way, the power is there for the boring way, for six hours, and then it is ours again. That is what I am voting for. I am not voting for a crew that can now ask for our batteries whenever the mood takes them.”

The room made a low sound of agreement. Elias felt the difference between Lena's version and his own proposal settle into him like cold water, because hers was not a smaller version of his. It was a different sentence, in which the crew was not the subject.

Inez picked up a stack of cards the size of playing cards. She had ruled them herself, three columns, and each one had a tick box and a signature line.

“This is the same card the power bank uses,” she said. “And it works because nobody has to raise a hand and stand there while other people decide what their raised hand means. Three options. Consent. Refusal. Withdraw later. You fill one card per person in your household, and you hand it to the person at the table, and I read the count out loud with the refusals in, because a vote that reports only the yes is a different vote.”

“Refusals in,” Joon said, from the wall where he had been recording, “is going to look like a bad number to a reader who does not know this room.”

“Let them have a bad number,” Inez said. “Let them have a real one.”

She came to Elias with a stack.

“One card each,” she said. “Not for the rescue service. For you.”

“Six of us,” Elias said.

“Rafi, Kellan, Tomas, Mara, Joon, and you. Inez does not count herself; I hold the power and I do not get a vote on my own power.”

Elias took a card. He looked at it for longer than a card deserved, because he had realised that he did not know what he personally wanted done with the Quarter's electricity, as opposed to what he wanted the survey to be able to do, and those were different things and the room could tell them apart.

He ticked **CONSENT**, signed, and handed it in.

Mara's card said **CONSENT** with a line under it: *I consent to the boring way. I do not consent to an entry.*

Tomas's said **CONSENT** with a longer line: *I consent to a survey with a stop threshold written before anybody goes down. I will hold the stop, and it will not be a discussion.*

Joon's said **CONSENT** and, underneath, *The records stay in this building. All of them, including the copies.*

Elias read that one twice and felt something shift in his chest that was not gratitude exactly, because Joon had not said it to him.

Inez counted for four minutes. It took four minutes because nobody would sit down and the children kept coming through and somebody had to keep finding them cards.

“Consent, thirty-one. Refusal, nine. Abstain, four. Withdrew their card and came back, one, and I am counting that one twice on purpose because it happened twice.”

A murmur went through the room at *thirty-one*, and Elias felt the shape of the thing tilt under him: nine people had said no in a room that had been described to him, in the abstract, as the Quarter.

“Nine refusals,” Inez said. “Say them out, because if I do not say them, they are just a number against the yes.”

She read them.

Three refused the survey load on the feeder. Six refused the rescue team inside this hall after ten at night, on the grounds — which she read verbatim, twice, because she had promised — that *after dark this building is where we sleep and we are not doing that in front of you.* One of those six was a man named **Osei**, who added, when Inez read his line and looked up, “Not a complaint. A preference. I want my daughter to be able to sleep.”

“Elias,” Inez said. “Two of those nine are against the team specifically, and one of them asked me to say so to your face. Would you like to hear it?”

“Yes.”

Perry Dunne stepped forward. “I want the crew gone by Friday,” he said. “Not out of spite. Because every morning after you go there is another morning somebody from outside tells me what my quarter needs, and I would like a week where nobody tells me anything. You are not bad at it. You are just here.”

“All right,” Elias said. “Friday. I will tell the Office that the neighbourhood has declined and the neighbourhood decides its own calendar.”

“You do not have to take that on.”

“I want to take it on,” Elias said, and then heard how much he liked saying it, which was exactly the kind of liking he had been warned about by nobody except himself.

Inez read the terms aloud, and they were hers, and every one of them was harder than anything Elias would have written.

The refuge would stay open to the twenty-three until the medical lead said they could be moved, and not one day beyond. A limited survey of the west apron and the lower junction might use the Quarter's load for a maximum of six hours, from a stated start time to a hard cut, announced in the hall and on the street. No resident would be used as an anchor, in any sense of the word, including metaphorically, and if anybody used the word that way they would be asked to leave. Any person may withdraw their own card, their own lamp, and their own name from the record at any time, and a withdrawal needs no reason and no meeting. The Office would receive a power summary and would not receive the residents' log. If the Office asked the cooperative to surrender the lamp records, the answer would be no, and the answer would be written down in advance so it could not be softened in the moment.

And one more, added after a discussion that took eleven minutes and that Elias lost on a point he had thought he had won:

The rescue team would not take a load instruction from anyone in the hall except the person whose load it was. If the hall went dark, Inez would cut the hall and the team would take its own lamps, and nobody in this building would be asked to stay in the dark to help a crew that had come from outside.

Elias looked at that last line for a long time.

“If the power goes,” he said, “we will be on batteries within ninety seconds and we will be slower and we will still be here.”

“That is the answer I was looking for,” Inez said.

---

Afterwards she wrote the split on the board in the hall, where it would still be on it in the morning, in a hand large enough to read from a cot.

MARA — medical triage, and the stop on moving any patient.
TOMAS — structural limits, and the stop threshold, written before anyone goes.
JOON — records, custody, and what gets published.
INEZ — local power, and the right to cut it.
RESIDENTS — consent to the space, and the cards.
ELIAS — physical movement, and no sole command.

Nobody had asked for that last line. Elias had written it himself, in the space under the rest, and when Inez read it back she tapped it twice with the chalk and said, “That is not a promise. That is a job description. Keep it where I can see it.”

The hall broke up slowly. Lena went to the clinic. Ru Sabar counted her bank out loud to a man taking notes for the residents' own record. Petar walked the exits with his clipboard and found one blocked by a chair and moved the chair, which was the most useful thing anybody did all evening.

At the door Nadi Bell was waiting with her coat on.

She had listened to the whole meeting, and she had not asked for anything, and now she said: “You have all got plans for the tunnel. I would like to know one thing about a bus.”

Elias stopped.

“My sister worked the west apron relief desk,” Nadi said. “Shuttle six ran the apron before the first bus every morning. If you can find out whether it went out that morning, you will know whether she was standing at the desk or on the shuttle. And if you cannot find out, then I will spend the rest of my life wondering, and I would like to spend it on something else.”

“That is not a theory,” Joon said, coming up behind them with his coat over his arm. “It is a person we can ask.”

“Then ask her,” Nadi said. “But understand what it costs me. The moment somebody with a badge starts asking about shuttle six on the morning of the collapse, my sister is in a room somewhere being told she was never related to me. If that happens, I will know, and she will know, and it will be worse than the row being missing.”

“You can be in the room,” Elias said.

“I cannot be in the room if it is a room,” Nadi said. “I can be in a street. I can be on a telephone line that is not the Spine if such a thing exists. And I can tell you now, in front of your dispatcher, that if you come back with her name on a page to prove something about a road, you will have used me, and I will know that you used me, and so will you.”

Joon took out a pencil and looked at Elias, and Elias found that he had a great many reasons why he wanted that name on a page and not one that survived the sentence.

“Write it down the way she said it,” Elias said. “Both parts.”
