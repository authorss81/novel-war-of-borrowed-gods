# Chapter 4 — The Record of a Rescue

The surface triage threshold was the mouth of a loading ramp behind Kestrel Station, where the wind came off the Salt Meridian carrying diesel, wet concrete, and the bitter smell of burned insulation. Elias helped lift Tarin Voss onto a wheeled stretcher, then stepped back before someone could tell him to do more.

His left hand had gone from numb to a dull, hot weight. He could move the fingers when he looked at them. He could not tell whether they were touching rope, metal, or nothing.

Mara kept one hand on Tarin’s chest seal. “He is stable enough to move. Stable is not fixed. Keep him warm and keep the seal visible.”

The fifteen Car 3 survivors came through the ramp in a wet procession. Some carried dust in their hair. One had lost a shoe. Niko Senn leaned on a responder and cursed the stair with every step. The woman in the orange coat gave her name as Anja Ro and then went to help another passenger lower himself to the pavement.

Anja paused beside Elias. “I called your name because you were the only person on the platform who looked at the door before it opened. I thought you might know what it meant.”

“I didn’t,” Elias said. “I’m glad you called.”

The answer did not make the door safe. It made the person who had called part of the record.

Elias moved down the line checking hands, feet, and the ability to follow a simple instruction. A medic stopped him when he reached for the next person.

“Your left grip is gone,” she said. “Let me test the other one.”

“It is fine.”

She put a capped pen in his right hand. He dropped it.

“Fine enough to climb stairs,” she said. “Not fine enough to pretend.”

He put the pen in his pocket instead of arguing. Beyond the triage barrier, Joon sat beneath a temporary canopy with two battery radios, a portable printer, and paper maps spread across a milk crate. The civic terminal beside him showed a clean incident page: **STRUCTURAL ANOMALY / CONTAINMENT IN PROGRESS**.

Elias took the paper manifest from Joon’s hand.

“Sixteen assigned to Car 3,” Joon said. “We have fifteen bodies, names, and one blank seat. The terminal’s passenger count keeps cycling between zero and twenty-eight, so I stopped reading it.”

“Twenty-eight?”

“It counted a service crew twice, then three rail crews who are outside the station.” Joon tapped the raw recorder beside the printer. “This is better. It kept every radio call, including the calls that came after the carriage appeared. Two people heard the same instruction at different seconds.”

Elias looked at him. “Two instructions?”

“Different words. Same voice.”

He wrote the time beside the door measurements: 06:03, twenty-seven inches at the narrowest point, three cycles of movement, two handles. He copied the location of the scrape marks, the depth of the footprints, the number of people Mara had ordered through. Then he drew the gap exactly as it appeared after the last passenger crossed.

His pencil stopped at the bottom of the page.

Mara’s voice came from the medical tent. “Elias, come here when you have thirty seconds.”

He handed the page to Joon. “Copy this before the terminal refreshes.”

“I already photographed it.”

“Photograph the paper, not the screen. Keep the original with me.”

Joon looked at the left hand. “You are not writing anything else without someone checking your grip.”

“Write with your left hand.”

“I do. I also wrote the date, the train number, and my name. This is the part where you stop arguing.”

Elias almost smiled. Instead, he looked back at the service door. The ramp opened onto a broad wall of station concrete. No door marked **CAR 3 SERVICE** existed there. Two holes in the wall at shoulder height had been painted over years ago. The threshold was solid. The last Group A survivor had crossed it while the emergency lights made the concrete behind him look thin.

Joon had a paper photograph of the open door. Mara’s triage notes placed Tarin on the landing beyond it. Tomas’s inspection log recorded tool marks where the handles had been. None of those records agreed with the wall.

Joon’s printer began to chatter again. He tore off a fresh manifest and stared at it.

“Elias, I need you over here.”

Joon had gone pale under the dust. He pointed to the raw relay meter beside the recorder. Its display did not show Car 3 or the lost door. It showed a northbound rail diagnostic packet and a four-character signal code from the Kestrel maintenance system.

A voice emerged from the speaker beneath the paper stack.

“Rook.”

Elias stopped breathing for a second.

The voice was Nell’s. Not the bright, quick version from the relay room before the collapse. The signal broke the name into static and brought back the consonants flattened by distance.

“—north stair is a branch, not an exit. Don’t let the—”

The packet cut out. Static filled the speaker, then Joon’s equipment returned to its normal hum.

He looked at Elias. “That channel was decommissioned years ago.”

“Can you trace the origin?”

“Not from this. The packet entered through the old maintenance relay beneath the north stair. That means where it came from.” Joon touched the recorder. “Not who sent it.”

“Copy the audio before anything normalizes it.”

“I am. Elias, it used the same code Nell sent before the collapse. The carrier began after the carriage appeared.”

Elias heard that sentence. The signal had crossed the station after the seam opened. It carried Nell’s present voice, displaced through a route that should not have reached them. It did not prove she had survived. It did not tell him where she was. It certainly did not give him permission to take the north stair.

He had promised himself on the service apron, in the half-second before the carriage appeared, that he would follow Nell’s signal if it came. The promise had not been wise. It had been smaller than the promise he wanted to make and larger than the evidence deserved.

Mara appeared at his shoulder.

“What did I miss?” she asked.

Joon played the recording. Mara listened to the clipped message without interrupting. When it ended, she asked, “Is that a reply from the first offer?”

“No.” Elias pointed to the carrier trace. “Different relay. Present transmission, carried across the seam. It is not the future voice on the first offer.”

Joon nodded. “It is not an echo from the Civic Spine. The source is physical and live.”

Mara’s eyes stayed on Elias. “How sure are you?”

“Sure that it entered after the collapse. Not sure Nell pressed the button in this version of the station.”

“That is useful.”

“It feels worse.”

“Those are not opposites.”

She led him into the triage tent. Tarin was conscious enough to complain that someone had taken his shoes. Mara checked the chest seal, then pointed toward Elias’s wrist display.

“The offer,” she said.

Elias opened the partial notice. The text was fading, as if the relay could no longer carry it, but the boundary and return remained.

“I told everyone what it did before I accepted,” Elias said. “One body, repair, the wheel.”

Mara crossed her arms. “And what you felt after you accepted?”

He could still see the bright station that did not belong to him. The memory had a clean white column and Mara’s voice counting in a room with no smoke. It made his own hands feel slow and inadequate, which was worse than pain.

“I saw a place that may happen if I use it,” he said. “I did not tell you.”

“Why not?”

“I thought it was afterimage noise. I thought you would treat the offer as less voluntary if you knew something from her branch could affect me.”

“Do you?”

“I don’t know. That is the honest answer.”

Mara looked toward the treatment bay. Her shoulders drew in, but she did not leave. When she faced him again, her voice was low enough that the other medics could not hear.

“You accepted a capacity carrying my name without telling me how it might alter your judgment. You read my present consent accurately, then kept a material part of the exchange private.”

“Yes.”

“Fix the record.”

“I will.”

“Fix it before you touch the notebook again.”

Elias gave Joon the recorder and dictated the correction: after acceptance, he experienced a branch-specific memory that was not his own and could not separate its fear from his own. The offer had disclosed the gift, boundary, and return, but not the perceptual aftermath. He could not guarantee that the memory would not affect a decision.

Mara read every sentence and signed the medical portion.

“My consent is for Tarin’s treatment and my repair work,” she said. “It is not consent to my future self lending capacity outside the offer. If either of us needs a wider promise, we ask again.”

“I understand.”

“Put it in the shareable form.”

They returned to the canopy. Tomas had set the powered maintenance trolley beside the refuge ramp. Its rear orange wheel was still turning, throwing a thin line of warm light across the concrete. Tarin had been placed in the medical group under a blanket. Around them, survivors argued over seats, family phones, and whose shoe was whose.

Elias took his notebook back from Joon. His right hand shook slightly. He wrote the correction in full, then turned the page so Mara could read it without crowding him.

Joon saved two copies to the battery repeater and placed the third in a sealed document pouch.

“Paper first,” he said. “Screen when we can. Neither one gets to be the only witness.”

The old maintenance relay clicked beneath the canopy. A fragment of Nell’s signal passed through the speaker, too brief to understand, then vanished.

Elias watched the paper map. The route to the north stair had been drawn in pencil beside the door measurements, a line he had not yet chosen to follow.
