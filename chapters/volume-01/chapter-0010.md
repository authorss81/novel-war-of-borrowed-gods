# Chapter 10 — The Names on Paper

Evening came with rain that began before the last stretcher reached the Cinder Quarter.

The rescue service had moved the triage yard into the old union hall behind Inez’s cooperative, because the hall had thick walls, a separate battery room, and a kitchen that could heat soup without drawing from the damaged Kestrel feeder. It was not a hospital. It had no privacy curtains and no reliable oxygen manifold. It had tables, extension cords, a deep sink, and people who knew one another well enough to argue about where a cot should go.

Elias sat on an upturned crate with his left hand resting in a basin of warm water. The physician had wrapped it in a loose bandage and told him to keep the fingers still. He obeyed until a man in the corner began choking on soup, then crossed the hall to help without noticing the movement until the bandage had loosened.

Mara caught him by the elbow. “Sit.”

“I can still carry a tray.”

“You cannot close your left hand. That is not a tray problem.”

She tightened the bandage. Around them, survivors ate and argued. Niko Senn had claimed the chair nearest the radiator and was telling anyone who came close that his knee had been better during the last collapse. Tarin Voss slept beneath two blankets with a paper cup of water beside his bed. Oren Pike had his ankle elevated on a folded coat. Nadi Bell sat with the school coat she had carried, turning the fabric over as if the child who had owned it might return and ask for it.

There were twenty-three names on the ledger.

Joon had written them in three columns: the fifteen people visible in Car 3, the person found in the maintenance pocket, and the seven from the alcove. The columns were not the same. The Car 3 count had a manifest with a blank seat and a terminal report that said zero. The alcove group had no official row at all.

Mara returned with a bowl of soup and set it beside Elias. “Eat.”

“After the next round.”

“Your blood sugar is not a rescue instrument.”

He picked up the spoon with his right hand. “I remember.”

“You remember enough to write records and forget to drink water.”

He drank the water first. It tasted of copper and the metal cup. The soup was too salty. He ate it anyway.

Inez Sato was wiring a battery bank beneath the union hall’s long table. She had placed two ordinary lamps on either side of the door, a third near the ledger, and a fourth beside the kitchen. Each lamp had a small clip beside its switch. On the clip someone had written **CONSENT / REFUSAL / WITHDRAW**.

“This is not a ward for borrowing,” Inez said, noticing Elias looking at the lamps. “It is a place to see whether a person is still willing to be here. We keep the lamps on separate batteries. If one fails, the others stay lit. The seam glass goes in the windows because it records a shape when a worker touches it, not because it can stop a seam.”

“Where did you get the glass?” Elias asked.

“Three panes from a neighborhood workshop. Two from a school science cabinet. One from my mother’s front room, which she will invoice the rescue service for.”

Inez connected a copper strip to a hand-built breaker. The strip was dull, the enclosure dented, and a small label warned that no one should open it without telling the cooperative. The warning had been written in three different hands.

“The refuge stays local,” Inez continued. “The residents vote before we connect a new circuit. Anyone can take their battery or their name out. The Office gets a copy of the power budget, not the keys.”

Elias looked toward the hall entrance, where Sable’s two staff members had set up a folding table. “Sable is not going to like the withdrawal rule.”

“She does not have to like it. She has to know where the switch is.”

The hall doors opened. Director Arden entered without removing her coat. Rain darkened the shoulders of her gray jacket. She carried the same document case from the yard, and Tomas followed with a folded copy of the maintenance map.

“Your patients are visible,” Sable said. “Your report is not complete.”

“Your report was complete before the rescue,” Elias said.

Sable placed the form on the table. “The classification remains an equipment failure.”

“Because your form was filled before the event?”

“Because the first response was to a structural anomaly, and the station’s records support a failure in the east signal equipment.”

Joon looked up. “The signal equipment did not create the carriage.”

“The carriage was not the first event,” Sable said.

Elias took the form and read the timestamp again. The numbers had not changed. The rescue report had been filed at 06:27. Sable’s equipment-failure entry carried 05:24, before the first recorded alarm and before the door appeared.

Joon had found the old maintenance log. He opened it on a battery laptop, then turned the screen toward Sable. “This entry was made at 05:17. It is a route entry, not a signal report. It has two author fields.”

The first held a Civic Spine maintenance number. The second was a short internal credential: **MNT-4C-17**. Joon pointed to it. “I recognize the format. It is a local maintenance credential. It is not a Civic Spine author record.”

Sable looked at the code. “That does not make it suspicious.”

“It makes it a second person or system who touched the route.”

“It makes it an operator field that may have been copied.”

Elias set the two documents side by side. The paper measurements from the service door, the raw relay index, Sable’s equipment-failure form, and the old route entry formed a timeline with no clean order. Each one pointed at a different beginning.

Mara joined them with the medical ledger. “The offer is recorded,” she said. “The one-body boundary, the return condition, and the refusal of a second-body extension are recorded. The paper record states that the present patient did not authorize a future person’s capacity.”

Sable glanced at her. “You want the Office to treat a future voice as a consenting party.”

“I want the Office to stop treating a future voice as a silent tool,” Mara said. “They can be recorded as unknown. They cannot be erased because they are inconvenient.”

Sable’s expression did not soften, but she did not dismiss the words. “Those are different positions.”

“Show me where the form says they are the same.”

Sable did not answer. Tomas looked away.

Inez brought a box of soldered wires and set it beside the ledger. “If you are going to compare records, do it where the people can see the comparison. No locked server. No private office.”

Joon glanced at her. “You want public records.”

“I want the people whose names are in them to know who has them.”

The room became quieter.

Elias understood the demand. A public record could expose frightened people, make a wrong name permanent, and turn a private injury into a target. A private record could vanish with one battery failure or one person’s decision. The choice was not safety against truth. It was safety shared by whom.

Mara looked at Elias. “You can keep the original notebook. But the copy goes where the survivors can inspect it.”

“I want the raw audio available to them.”

“Not just to you. To anyone who has a reason to hear it, in a place where they can choose whether to listen.”

Elias nodded. “Yes.”

Mara closed the ledger. “Then we have a working rule. Any future offer comes with the gift, boundary, return condition, and refusal. We tell the people exposed to the cost. We do not let a future voice answer for a present person.”

Joon wrote the rule on a clean page and pinned it above the ledger. Tomas looked at it for a long moment.

“Old safety language used to say one voice was easier to follow,” he said. “Fewer contradictions.”

“Contradictions can be evidence,” Joon said.

“Contradictions can get people killed.”

“Clean records can get people killed too.”

Tomas looked toward the people on the cots. “I know. I am not arguing that the Office is always right. I am saying that every rule needs someone willing to enforce it before the emergency arrives.”

“Then enforce this one with us,” Elias said.

Tomas folded his map. “That is not how enforcement works.”

“It could be.”

No one agreed. No one left. Tomas took a cot at the side of the hall and began checking the straps.

The first battery bank had a signed card from the residents who wanted the refuge connected. Before Inez attached the last bank, she carried the clipboard to the cots and read the same question again. Six people consented. One person refused and took a lamp with him. Two asked to remain unnamed. Inez marked the refusals, then connected only the approved bank.

The lamps came on one by one, their colors duller than the Civic Spine lights. She placed a sheet of seam glass in a wooden frame between the hall doors and the street. Rain ticked against it. A faint duplicate shadow moved when a truck passed, then vanished.

“Prototype,” Inez said. “It will not make the tunnel safe. It will give us a place to keep people, maps, and decisions while we find out what the tunnel is doing.”

Elias looked at the glass. The shadow did not resemble him. It resembled a person standing just beyond the door, waiting for someone to open it.

He looked away.

Near midnight, he found a blank page in his notebook and wrote to Nell.

*I hear you. I do not know where you are, and I will not pretend that the signal is a route home. I will not make your choice for you. If you can hear this, keep your own record. We are keeping ours.*

He did not send the words. The relay was not a postbox, and a promise written for a person who might never read it was still a promise. He tore the page out, folded it, and placed it in the envelope marked **KESTREL / DO NOT FILE WITHOUT RECEIVER CONFIRMATION**.

Joon was still at the table when Elias approached. The route entry and Sable’s form lay side by side, their timestamps separated by a gap the terminal had not explained.

“Can you compare the times?” Elias asked.

Joon nodded. “The route entry preceded the first alarm. Sable’s failure code came after the entry and before the rescue report. I cannot attach either one to a person yet.”

“Keep both originals.”

Joon saved the comparison twice. “I am.”

At the door, Inez’s small consent lamp went dark. No future voice answered it. The refuge held anyway.

Mara set the medical ledger beside the route entry. “Tomorrow, after the night shift, we compare the old access log with the failure report. Then we decide whether the evidence supports another entry.”

Tomas nodded. “I will bring the support plan and mark every place we could make the choice reversible.”

Elias looked at the two deeper heat signatures, then at the twenty-three names on the ledger. The rain struck the seam glass in the door. A second shadow moved behind it, too faint to follow.

“Tomorrow,” he said. “We begin with the record.”

Joon wrote **MNT-4C-17** beneath the two signatures, then wrote the question under it: **WHO ENTERED THE ROUTE BEFORE THE COLLAPSE?**
