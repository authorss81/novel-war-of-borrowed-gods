# Chapter 11 — What the Survivors Need

The antibiotics arrived in a plastic tray with a strip of tape over the lid and somebody's initials on the tape.

Twenty-three doses, split between two drugs, packed at the Kestrel platform medical cache at 06:40 and handed over at the surface yard without a signature. Mara laid them out on the long table under the battery lamp and counted them twice while Elias read the label past her shoulder, because the label was printed in the old narrow type and his left hand could not hold the paper flat.

“One day and a half,” she said. “If we are generous. If nobody gets a second course.”

The hall had eleven mats, two cots, a kitchen on a hand line, and one working oxygen cylinder with a regulator and two masks. The cylinder had come out of the tunnel on the trolley with Tarin, and it had been sitting beside the medical bay ever since, hooked to a nasal cannula belonging to a woman named Sabine Frew whose lung had been damaged by the carriage buckling.

Niko Senn watched Mara count from the chair he had claimed beside the radiator.

“You are going to tell us who gets them,” he said.

“Yes,” Mara said.

“Fine. Just do not be tender about it.”

---

At seven the Office's staff member set a printed demand on the long table, weighted down with a carabiner.

It required one number, one route status, and the full legal name of every person recovered. It had a box for the time of the first alarm, a box for the time of the last exit, and a box labelled **CAUSE**, and the first two of those boxes were already filled.

Joon read it and put it face down.

“They want a consolidated list by fourteen hundred,” he said.

“They can want it,” Elias said.

“They can also explain to a magistrate why the count is blank.”

Inez came in from the battery room with a coil of test leads over her shoulder and looked at the upside-down form.

“If the list leaves this room with twenty-three names on it, every one of those names is a record that somebody can subpoena next month,” she said. “And every one of those names is attached to a room full of people who cannot get out of the Meridian tonight.”

“Agreed,” Joon said, and left the form where it was.

Elias did not find the argument difficult. He found it tiring in a way he had not expected, because for six years his job had been to turn frightened people into a list, and now the list was the thing everyone was afraid of.

---

He took the care line because Mara needed a second pair of hands that could carry things without being asked to decide anything.

That was a smaller job than he was built for. He brought water. He held a man's shoulder still while a medic changed a dressing. He read numbers out loud twice so that the person who wrote them down got them right, and he said, every time his left hand went useless in the middle of a task, “Left hand's gone,” and handed the job to somebody else.

By nine he had said it eleven times. Each time it was easier and none of it was easier.

Tarin Voss sat up on his mat at the second round, gray-faced and careful, and asked Mara for the antibiotic.

“If it will not slow me down,” he said. “I am not spending my legs being careful about an infection.”

“It will not slow you down,” Mara said. “You are also not standing up without a hand on the rail.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“It is what I am offering. Second dose tonight, not in the morning. You will feel it by the time you try the stairs and you will be angry about it.”

Tarin looked at Elias. “Is that the whole story?”

“That's her whole story,” Elias said.

“Then somebody tell me the other thing.”

Elias had been asked this by four people in six hours, and he had begun to understand that the question was not about the future. It was about whether the room was being lied to.

“A capacity was offered to me by a version of Mara Okafor seventeen years from now,” he said. “Her name is on it because she offered it. It was disclosed out loud before I accepted it, it was limited to one man, it was meant to keep you alive while she worked on you, and it ended on its own condition when a wheel on a maintenance trolley stopped turning. I did not choose the end. She did not extend it for anyone else. Mara decided the medicine. The medicine is hers.”

Tarin absorbed that. “And my name goes in it.”

“Your name goes in the medical record. The loan is recorded in the disclosure log. You can read both.”

“I want my shoes back.”

“They are under the ramp,” Elias said. “Rafi has them.”

That was the end of it. Tarin lay down and asked for the soup, and Elias moved on feeling as though he had been let off something he had not known he was carrying.

---

Niko Senn was the second person who wanted something, and it was not medicine.

“I will give a statement,” he said, before Elias had asked. “But not with my name printed. Not in a bulletin, not in a terminal, not on a sheet somebody photographs.”

“Why.”

Niko looked down at the metal brace around his knee as though it were the thing he was really asking about.

“Because then I am the man with the brace who was in the carriage, and the next time there is a collapse on this line somebody will put my name in a headline about a route that may or may not exist. I have a sister in Corvin Street who reads the civic digest on her lunch. She does not need to find out that I was in a train that the system says was empty.”

“The statement stays useful without the name.”

“Then take it without the name. Write ‘a Car 3 passenger with a knee brace.’ I will say what I saw. It will mean nothing to a headline.”

Elias felt the argument for a name form and dismantled it before it finished forming, which was new. He had spent eight years believing that a public record was the only thing that kept a person from being quietly deleted.

“Then it stays off the public sheet,” he said. “Joon takes it in this room, with you, and it goes into the working file under your name with a note that you asked not to be published.”

“That note is the part that matters,” Niko said. “Write that I asked.”

Joon wrote it while Niko watched, and then Niko read it back and made him change *declined publication* to *asked that his name not be published*, because the first version sounded like something done to him.

---

Oren Pike was the third.

He had been found on a lower landing with his arm pinned and his ankle turned, and he had not been in the carriage, and now he could not stop asking about the manifest.

“The seat is written on me,” he said. “3C-18. It has been written on me since I bought the ticket. And your man says I was in a maintenance pocket, which means the ticket says I was in Car 3 and your count says I was somewhere else, and one of those is going to be what my family reads.”

“They will read both,” Elias said. “Your seat and where you were found. Joon keeps them on the same line so nobody has to choose.”

“That is going to look like a mistake.”

“It might,” Joon said from the end of the table. “It is not a mistake. It is a person who was somewhere the carriage was not, and I would rather write that down than make the seat and the body agree when they do not.”

Oren considered this for a while. Then he asked whether he could have a photograph of his own chart, and Joon gave him one, taken with the paper and not the screen, and Oren held it in both hands for longer than a piece of paper required.

---

Ansel Duarte could not stand, could not roll onto one side without help, and did not want to be described.

He was nineteen and he had been in a service alcove with six strangers for long enough that he had begun answering questions before they were asked. When a responder from the morning shift crouched beside his mat with a phone to take a picture of his back for the incident file, Ansel said, “No,” flatly, in the voice of someone who had discovered that refusal worked and intended to keep using it.

“Your back needs looking at,” Mara said. “The picture is for the file, not for anybody's curiosity.”

“Then look at it and write down what you saw.”

“It goes in the file as a description. Joon, note that Ansel Duarte declines photographs and that the examination happened.”

Ansel listened to the note being written and something in his face let go by a fraction.

“You are not going to argue with him,” he said to Elias.

“No.”

“Nobody argues with me. I have been in a hole for six hours.”

“You are not in a hole now,” Elias said, and then heard how little that was worth, and did not try to improve it.

---

The request that took the longest to reach Mara came from Nadi Bell.

She had not asked for anything since the alcove. She sat with the child's school coat folded across her knees and the cut above her eyebrow cleaned and taped, and she asked whether she could make a telephone call.

“The hall line belongs to the cooperative,” Inez said. “It is a cooperative line, so it is logged, and anything we say on it can be pulled by the Office in about a week.”

“Then I would rather say nothing than say it where somebody reads it later.”

“There is a clinic line two streets over,” Lena Mir said, wiping her hands. She ran nights at the Quarter's clinic and had been the first person to sign Inez's power card. “It is not ours. But it is a line and it is not the Spine.”

Nadi looked at the coat. “Not today. Today I want to know whether my sister is on a shuttle that ran. That is all. Then I will decide who hears it.”

“Nadi,” Elias said, “if you make the call, we cannot stop it becoming part of the record. I will not pretend otherwise.”

“I know what a record is,” she said. “I have been off one since 05:17 yesterday morning.”

---

The rest of the morning was water, soup, dressings, a mattress for Ansel that Perry Dunne's van brought over without being asked, and a hundred small pieces of work that no one would ever thank anybody for.

Petar Lisk, the boy who had come out of the alcove carrying a fire extinguisher as though it were a prize, asked three times whether he could keep it. Elias told him the extinguisher belonged to the City and could be logged out for the use of the hall, and Petar became the hall's fire marshal, which gave him a clipboard, a nightly walk-through, and the authority to shout at adults about blocked exits. He took the job with a seriousness that made everyone nearby uncomfortable in a way that was probably healthy.

Emil Baros, the white-haired man who had handed Nadi a child's school coat, sat by the kitchen door for two hours and could not stop apologising.

“I keep losing their names,” he said, when Elias finally crouched beside him. “The six of us. I had them all in my head at nine o'clock and now I have four and one of those four is a man from the train and one of them is a woman called something with an R.”

“All right,” Elias said. “Give me the four you have. We will find the other two from the manifest, and the names go on a piece of paper so you do not have to hold them.”

“That is a stupid use of a rescuer.”

“It is the most useful thing I can do this morning.”

The old man laughed, which turned into a cough, which turned into him pressing his hand over his eyes for a while. Then he gave Elias four names and Elias wrote them in his right hand on the back of a thermal blanket and read them back twice, and Emil made him read them a third time.

---

The antibiotic decision came at eleven, and it was Mara's.

Tarin got the second dose, because there was a chest seal over a puncture that had gone through a lung. Ruta Amsel, the woman from the alcove with the broken wrist, got the second dose of the other drug, because the skin at the break was open and there was no water supply to clean it with beyond the sink. Ansel got pain relief and a mattress and nothing else, and Sabine's cannula stayed on the cylinder until four in the afternoon, when the cylinder was moved to Tarin and Sabine woke up angry about it and was told the truth, which was that the cylinder belonged to whoever needed it next.

Nobody argued with Mara. Elias, who had spent a night wishing he had a person to hand a decision to, found that he could stand in a room and watch a decision be made by somebody else, and that the feeling was not relief. It was the beginning of something with no name in it yet.

At half past eleven Inez read the power budget out loud, three banks on the medical circuit, one on the lamps, one held in reserve for the radio, and a fourth bank that was going warm and would need replacing before the evening.

Joon was already clearing the middle of the long table. He put the paper manifest in the center, the route measurements beside it, the raw recorder on a chair at the end, and the Civic Spine terminal at the other end where the screen showed a blank count field and the words **AWAIT CONSOLIDATED RESPONSE LIST**.

“The names are in here,” he said. “The numbers are in here. The index is in here, and the index is the thing that says nobody came out of that carriage.” He put his hand flat on the table. “I am not comparing any of it in a back office. If we are going to argue about which record is right, we argue about it where the twenty-three can watch us do it.”

Nadi Bell looked up from the coat. Anja Ro, who had been checking names against the manifest all morning for no pay and no instruction, put down the pencil.

Elias looked at the table, and at the hall, and at the people in it, and understood that the next several hours would be spent doing the thing he was worst at.

Not deciding. Showing his work.
