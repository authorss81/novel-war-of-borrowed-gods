# Chapter 26 — The Slower Plan

Elias wrote the plan on Wednesday morning in forty minutes, and it was the best thing he had written all year, and by Thursday night there was nothing left of it except five people who had each taken a piece of it and put their own name on it.

The original was one page. A joint inspection of the west apron node inside the board's eleven days, with the run at eleven meters, the 2031 pour, and the brick lining at seven meters as the three targets. Ward power committed from nine to nine. Five domains on one schedule. One combined team, one instrument list, one stop page, one medical standby, one gas tech, one resident observer, and a single line at the bottom that said, in his handwriting, **ONE VOICE ON THE RADIO.**

He put it on the board at eleven and everybody in the hall looked at it, and nobody said anything for about a minute, which he had learned to read as bad.

“Who is the one voice,” Inez said.

“Me.”

“There is a line on that wall that says you do not have one.”

“That is about command. This is about the radio.”

“It is the same sentence with a different noun,” Inez said, “and I have watched you use that trick twice this week, and both times a man in this hall who was going to be fine became a fact in a document.”

---

Inez killed the power in the middle of the afternoon, on purpose, and made the whole room come and look at what she had done.

She put the ward's load sheet on the table with her finger on it.

“Here is the truth about your nine o'clock,” she said. “The medical bank is first priority and it stays first priority. The lamp bank is second and it is what the twenty-three wake up in. The reserve is third and it is three and a half hours, and I have been quietly selling things on Ash Street to keep it at three and a half instead of two, and I have not told the meeting that, and I am telling you now because I am about to use it.”

“Use it how,” Elias said.

“If your survey takes the ward from nine to nine, then this hall has a medical bank that dies at ten in the evening, and twenty-three people who are mostly improving and three who are not, in a building in the last week of the month with a clinic four streets away that is also on a generator that is also on somebody's money. And the reserve does not exist as a reserve for twelve hours, it exists as a reserve for three and a half, and after that this building is a room with no lights.”

“We do not take the medical bank,” Elias said.

“You cannot have the lights without the medical bank,” Inez said. “They are on the same feeder. I have spent a week listening to people propose splitting them and none of them have ever had to carry a battery up four flights.”

She squared the sheet.

“Here is the thing you actually built,” she said. “You built a plan that requires this room's power for twelve hours with a plan's worth of cooperation, and you have described that to me as *committed*, and it is not committed. Committed is what a generator is. What you have is residents agreeing to a load, and if you ask them for twelve hours at once, on the week that the wall goes up, with a card count that has gone down twice already, then the honest name for it is not commitment. It is pressure. And it is pressure that looks exactly like consent from across a room, which is the thing this whole building was organized to be able to tell apart.”

“Then what do you want.”

“Four draws,” Inez said. “Four hours a day, spread out, each one of them a separate card, each one revocable while it is running. Between them the rest of the day belongs to this hall, and the hall has its lights, and the twenty-three sleep with the medical bank at full, and if a resident wants to pull a card at hour three, somebody pulls it and nobody argues about it.”

“Four hours of fragmentation instead of twelve of coherence.”

“Yes,” Inez said. “It is worse. It is slower. It is four times as many things to go wrong. And it is the only version of this where the sentence on the wall is still true on the twelfth hour.”

---

Tomas and Mara did not speak for a long time, and when they did, it was at each other, and it was the first time in a week that two of the five had gone at each other in front of the room.

Mara's position was that the plan could not contain a medical protocol. “If we find somebody, we are going to stand in a hole and be told things by a person we cannot see, and I am not going to have a piece of paper upstairs that says what we do next, because the piece of paper will be read aloud to the person in the hole within an hour whether we intend that or not, and the second we have said it we have stopped asking and started telling.”

“That is a refusal with no replacement in it,” Tomas said. “Mara, that is a protocol. A protocol is what happens when you have not written one down and you do it anyway, and you do it from an office, and I have watched a crew do that in 2034 and I wrote the rule that let them do it.”

“And I am saying I will not do it from an office.”

“Then say what you will do instead, because the alternative is that we go down there with no plan and we improvise at somebody's chest, and improvisation from above ground is still a reroute. It has a different name. It does not have a different consequence.”

Mara was quiet for a while.

“Two things,” she said. “No medic on the line. That stands and it is mine. And no predicted outcome anywhere in the document — no triage categories, no expected numbers, no *if we find three*, nothing. And nothing at all about what we do if we find somebody, because we do not know enough to write it and writing it would be a way of having already decided.”

“Two things is not a plan,” Tomas said, but he was not fighting it any more, and Elias understood that the argument had actually been settled about forty minutes in, and that the rest of it had been two tired people making sure.

“It is two refusals,” Mara said. “It is what we actually have.”

“Write it as two refusals,” Tomas said, “and let the rest of the room be frightened about it properly.”

---

What came out on Thursday night was four pages, and none of them was Elias's.

**THE STRAWB MAN PLAN.** Ru Sabar named it, at nine o'clock, over the objection of everybody, and it stayed.

It had no single schedule. It had four draws a day, each one a card, each one separate, each one announced in the hall and on Ash Street with a start and an end, and a hard rule that any resident could pull their own card while it was running and nobody would ask them why. It had a per-circuit card system, which the hall had never actually used, and a lamp-and-name withdrawal that took no meeting and no reason and still existed.

It had a target list and no sequence: the 2031 pour, the brick lining at seven meters, and the run at eleven, in that order of importance and not in that order of execution, and the order of execution belonged to whoever was on the ramp that day.

It had five domains and no coordinator, and each of the five could stop it, and the hall could stop it, and there was a line at the bottom of the medical page that said **WE HAVE NOT WRITTEN DOWN WHAT HAPPENS IF WE FIND SOMEBODY BECAUSE WE DO NOT KNOW, AND WE WILL NOT FIND OUT BY WRITING IT DOWN IN ADVANCE.**

And it had a medical standby at the top with a cylinder and a hard rule that treatment happens where they are found, in their air, at their pace, and that nobody on this page is authorized to move a person for their own benefit.

And on the power page, in Inez's hand, the cost.

**THE HALL GOES DARK 0200–0400 EVERY DAY OF THIS. NO LIGHTS IN THE DRY END. SEAM-GLASS FRAME OFF. RESIDENTS WHO ARE SLEEPING ARE SLEEPING. PETAR WALKS IT WITH A HAND LAMP AND A DOOR TO OPEN.**

“Do you understand what you have just agreed to,” Elias said. “Two hours a night. In the wet. With the twenty-three in it.”

“Yes,” Inez said. “I have understood it since Tuesday afternoon and I have been waiting for you to arrive.”

“Ru Sabar.”

“Yes,” Ru Sabar said.

And then she took a card out of her apron, the one she had carried since the first vote, and put it face down on the table, and said, “That is my withdrawal. Not the hall circuit. My laundrette circuit, permanently, because you are going to need the hours and I have been pretending for a week that I can run a machine shop on four hours of dark. Take it off the board and do not thank me, because I want it recorded that nobody made me and I would have done it anyway.”

Nobody thanked her.

At ten the count came back: forty-two cards in, twenty-two consent, fourteen refusal, six abstention, and one of those forty-two was a woman who had not held a card before, and who told Inez she had wanted to say no out loud in a room where the walls were covered in paper, and that she had been waiting a week to do it.

Inez read all fourteen refusals out in full, and Petar Lisk wrote them on the board, and when she came to the end she capped the chalk and said the thing that made it a plan rather than a document.

“The cost is named,” she said. “It is named by the people paying it and not by the man who wanted the operation. Two hours of dark. Ru's machine shop. A laundrette that stays shut. And six people who would rather we did nothing at all for the eleven days than do it this way, and they are right to be uncomfortable, and their names are on the board and they are not being asked to change their minds.”

Nobody in the hall could think of anything to add, which had happened four times in eleven days and was no longer a surprise to anybody except Elias.
