# Chapter 20 — Who Entered the Route

They started at ten to six, because the survey that had been stopped was stopped at 12:40 yesterday and the whole room had been carrying that number around for a night, and somebody in the kitchen suggested that if they were going to be frightened it should be frightened at the correct hour.

The hall at ten to six in the morning is a different room. The cots along the wall with three people awake on them. The batteries down to their last two hours of the medical bank. Ru Sabar, who had come in at five to open the laundrette next door, standing in the doorway with her coat on and a piece of paper in her hand, because Ru Sabar had learned on Wednesday that a piece of paper in a room beats a person saying a thing once.

Elias had the table. He had five sheets on it, one for each name on the stop page, and he wrote nothing on them for twenty minutes, which Joon found so uncomfortable that he asked about it twice.

The repeater on the hall roof woke at six with the civic digest, and Lena Mir turned it up in the kitchen, and it was the Office's answer to the objection, and it was read out by a woman with a bad chest who wanted to hear it and who nobody stopped.

The answer was two minutes long. It was courteous. It cited Meridian 2029 and eleven fatalities and the Kestrel team's own three-column page as evidence of good practice. And near the end it said this:

*The Civic Spine must carry one instruction where reports conflict, because two instructions to a crew inside an active seam is a documented cause of death. Until the survey review, the Office's approved outcome for Kestrel remains provisional. One approved outcome is not a choice about who lives. It is a choice about who has to be obeyed while the ground is moving.*

Nobody spoke for a while.

“Eleven people,” Ru Sabar said. “That is a real number. That is not a thing somebody made up to win an argument.”

“It is a real number,” Mara agreed, “and it is the wrong number to be governing this room with. It is a number about a different room, in a different year, where a crew was given two instructions by two radios. It says nothing about a woman with no row who exists anyway, and it is being used to keep her row deleted, and both of those things are true at once and they are going to be true at once for a long time.”

“It is also the only sentence in that answer I have ever heard that I agree with,” Tomas said. “One voice in a working crew. I have believed that since before that answer existed, and I signed a rule about it, and the rule is why a man died in 2034. So I am not going to stand up here and tell forty people that the Office is lying. I am going to stand up and tell them that a rule written by frightened competent people to stop a real thing from happening again will keep stopping things, and that nobody so far has found a way to be right in public without also being obeyed in private.”

Elias splayed both hands on the table — the useless one included, so that anybody looking would see both — and did not make a speech, and it was the hardest physical act of his week.

“Here is what is known,” he said. “One: the route entry at 05:17 used a maintenance credential whose holder this institution threw away in 2035, and the holder is nobody we can name, which means we do not have a culprit and we are not going to manufacture one. Two: the equipment-failure classification was opened at 05:24 in a vehicle already standing in that sector, and the officer who was in it says she logged a signal fault and nothing else, and the officer's own office cannot produce the second signature. Three: one confirmed living person behind that wall and a second camera reading we cannot confirm. Four: the service door is unsafe, and whether it is shifted or absent is not a question we can answer from this room, and I have given up wanting to answer it this week. Five: the eleven dead in 2029 are dead, and the answer that cites them is not lying, and it is still going to keep a family off a list.”

He put a finger on the second sheet.

“What is new since yesterday is this. Joon.”

Joon came up with the recorder and a page with one line on it.

“Two o'clock this morning, the Kestrel maintenance relay carried four words. It is Nell's signal — same compression, same carrier, same present voice, and I am not going to pretend I know more about it than that. The four words are *the east branch holds*.” He set the page down. “And I checked the carrier against the card image from the west apron relay, because I have been doing nothing else since Tuesday. The carrier tone is the same tone. The same path, Elias. Her voice comes in on the line that the 05:17 entry was made on. Not the Spine. Not the north stair. The old maintenance route under this station.”

Nobody had said anything about that yet, because it had taken until six in the morning for the fact to be worth saying, and because saying it out loud made the whole table tilt.

“So the credential route is her route,” Elias said.

“It is the same physical path. I cannot tell you it is the same door, and I am not going to try.” Joon looked uncomfortable and pleased at once, which was his permanent expression around a discovery. “What I can tell you is that the one thing in Kestrel that would let us understand what a maintenance credential was doing to that sector at 05:17 is the same thing her voice comes in on, and we do not have a name to put on it.”

“Then it is not a curiosity,” Inez said. “It is the way to her.”

“It is a way to a place her voice uses,” Elias said. “And I want to be very careful with that sentence, because I have spent this week wanting a route to my sister more than I have wanted anything, and wanting it is exactly the thing that will make me do something we vote against at seven in the evening.”

---

The discussion went badly for the first forty minutes, which everyone had expected and which turned out to be the healthiest thing that had happened in the hall.

Tomas wanted the node. He wanted it in a way that was almost embarrassing, because it was the only piece of the puzzle that had a handle on it: the buried paired node under the west apron, the thing that had moved the ground in 2034 and probably moved it yesterday, written off in 2035 with his own signature second on the page. He laid out a civic inspection request in four lines, and every line of it was defensible, and then he said the sentence that got him shouted at.

“If the Office refuses, I want the refusal on the page in the same typeface as the request.”

“They will refuse,” Sable's archivist said. He was a lean man of about sixty called **Hollis Vane**, and he was quiet and thorough and unimpressed. He had arrived at seven with a scanner, a pencil, and a list of what he had been asked to read. “The hold is ninety-six hours. Nothing goes below ground in ninety-six hours without a review board. A review board convenes on the fourth day. That is not me being obstructive. That is the order you accepted.”

“We are not asking you to authorise anything,” Elias said. “We are asking you to forward a request so that when the board sits, the request is in front of it. A refusal is a document. A thing that was never asked cannot be refused, it can only be ignored, and ignoring is what got us here.”

Hollis Vane wrote it down. He did not agree, and he did not refuse, and he asked what language they wanted, and when Joon said *citizens' request for joint structural inspection*, Hollis Vane said, “That is the correct title. Do not improve it. Improved titles get rejected for imprecision.”

Mara was the first hard no, and she said it at ten past seven.

“No medic on the line,” she said. “I want a medic at the top. I want a medic at the top with a cylinder and a rule that says that if we find somebody, the treatment happens where we find them, at their pace, in their air, and it happens because they are there and not because we arrived prepared.”

“Nadi was brought through a hole in ninety minutes,” Elias said.

“Nadi came to us. She could walk, and she had been in an alcove with air and six other people, and she had a reason to come out that she told me about while I was holding a rope.” Mara's voice was not hard now, it was precise. “There is a version of tomorrow where we put a medic on a passive listening line for six hours and at hour five somebody on the other side stops knocking because they have gone to sleep, and a medic on a line hears that silence and starts making decisions about a person they have never met. I have watched that happen. I have done it. I will not build a plan that makes it easier to do.”

“All right,” Elias said. “No medic on the line. That is the arrangement and it is better and I would not have thought of it.”

“You would not have thought of it because you were going to put your best people where you could reach them, which is the entire disease.”

“Which is why I have four of you.”

---

Ansel Duarte had been awake since four and had been carried down before the meeting, and he listened to the entire argument without speaking, and at a quarter to eight he made everybody stop.

“You are all talking about getting them out,” he said.

Nobody contradicted him.

“I was in that alcove for six hours with six people I did not know, and the thing that was hardest was not the wall. The wall was honest. The wall told me every ten minutes exactly how much room I had left.” He had his hands flat on his knees, the way he had in the alcove. “The hard thing was that we were going to have to leave, and I did not know that, and every one of us was deciding separately whether to say anything, and two of us did and four of us did not, and then the wall moved.”

He looked up.

“If you find somebody behind that wall, and you put a rope in, then you have made a thing where leaving is a decision they have to make about you. And you will have made it for them at six in the morning from two hundred metres away. I am not saying do not. I am saying that if you go back down there, the thing you go down there for is not *a person*.”

He was quiet for a second.

“It is a conversation. If they want to talk, we talk. If we cannot get far enough for a conversation, then the honest answer is that we have not found anybody, and you write that down, and you do not write *almost*.”

Elias felt the plan change in his hands while the man was speaking.

“Yes,” he said. “That is the success condition. A conversation, not an extraction. If the line goes quiet and nothing is said, the result is *no contact*, and it goes on the wall with the same weight as the knock, and nobody is to write *almost* in any document in this building.”

“Write it on the board,” Inez said.

Elias wrote it on the board.

---

What came out of the hour after that was smaller than a rescue and larger than a plan.

**ONE.** No entry. The service door stays shut. The altered route is not reopened, and if the survey is refused, the answer is that the door stays shut, and nobody is to describe that as giving up.

**TWO.** A passive listening campaign at the wall. No medic on the line. No door, no break, no opening. Bore line for sound, bore line for air, a camera on the line, a gas sample, the acoustic recorder running. Its only purpose is contact: knock, and wait, and speak, and be quiet, and record whatever comes back including nothing. It expires with the day. It needs a new card from the residents and a new stop page, because the old one is dead, and Elias said so out loud in the room so that nobody would think the old one was being carried over.

**THREE.** A citizens' request for a joint structural inspection of the west apron node, forwarded to the review board under the hold, with the same-relay history as the reason and Hollis Vane's advice about the title, and with a refusal to be recorded as a document.

**FOUR.** The shaft Halla Dren asked us not to name is not named, and the request and the refusal and her warning are printed on the wall together, in the order they happened, so that the next person to read them cannot find the request without the refusal.

**FIVE.** The envelope in Elias's coat came out of the coat and went into the notebook, and he wrote on the outside of it, under the old marking, in his right hand, **NOT FILED. NOT SENT. A PROMISE I HAVE NOT EARNED THE RIGHT TO MAKE YET** — and Joon, watching, said nothing at all, which was the most respectful thing he had done all week.

Then Inez asked the question that had been waiting at the back of the room since six o'clock.

“Say the promise,” she said. “Out loud. The one you are actually making, not the one that sounds good.”

Elias put the pen down.

“I am not promising to bring anybody out of that wall,” he said. “I do not have that and I am not going to say it. Here is the promise I can keep. I will not go below the west apron on my own at any hour for any reason. And I will not go at all unless four of the five of us have said yes in the same week, and if one of the five says no, I do not go, and I do not go looking for somebody else to overrule her, and I do not go and come back and mention that I went.”

He looked round the table.

“And that includes the hall. If this room sends anybody out that door, I do not go.”

Inez wrote it on the board. Elias signed first, because it was his promise. Mara signed it. Tomas signed it with the word *agreed* and then added, in the margin, that he had never in his life seen it written down before it was needed. Joon signed and dated it and initialled the copies. Inez signed last and made him initial the copies as well.

Petar Lisk copied the whole of it out in his own hand onto the back of a clipboard notice, because the boy had been listening from the doorway for two hours and had decided that a promise deserved to be big.

---

Hollis Vane left at half past eight with a list of what he had read, a list of what he had photographed, and one question.

He asked it at the door, in the ordinary voice of a man doing his job.

“Mr. Rook. You have a sister. I have read a file that contains a voice that may be hers and a carrier that connects it to the entry you are asking me to forward. I have to write a finding this morning whether or not a survey is approved. Do you want me to write that a private family connection exists as a factor in the request, or do you want me to leave it out and let the request stand on the structure alone?”

Elias had been asked to choose between a thing that was true and a thing that was clean, and it took him a long moment to understand that this was the exact shape of every decision he had been making for eight years and that the reason he had been losing them was that he had never once been asked.

“Leave it out,” he said. “Put the request on the structure. If the connection is a factor, it is a factor in me and not in the ground, and if the board ever gets a reason to think that a brother is writing this, then everything we have said for four days becomes a thing people say about nepotism instead of a thing people say about records.”

Hollis Vane wrote that down too.

“That,” he said, “is the first finding of the day and it is not about the ground.”

---

At seven in the evening Inez read the day's board out to the hall, all of it, including the part where a nineteen-year-old from an alcove had told four professionals what they were actually for, and including the promise, word for word, because she said a promise that was not read out loud was only a thing that had happened to some people in a room.

Thirty-four cards came back. Twenty-six consent, seven refusal, one abstention, and one card that had been filled in and handed in and then handed back and refilled, which Inez counted once and told everybody about.

Two of the seven refusals were from people who had consented yesterday, and one of them was Lena Mir, and Inez read her line out as well because the room had earned it.

Then they hung the listening plan's stop page on the wall beside the split authority, with the expiry date already filled in, and the hall was quiet for a moment in the way a hall is quiet when something has been decided properly and nobody can think of anything to add.

Elias stood at the long table with his notebook open and looked at the five things he had come to Kestrel to find out.

The first loan was over and could not be reopened. The service door was unsafe and possibly absent and he would probably never know which. A future version of Mara had said no to him once and had meant it. A person behind a wall that one camera saw and another camera did not had a want of their own about being found, which meant that even if he reached them, he would not be reaching them for himself.

His sister had a voice and a carrier and no location, and the voice came in on the line that a credential nobody owned had used twenty-three minutes before the world noticed there was anything wrong.

Somewhere in a drawer in a building he could not enter, there was a name.

Joon had been watching him not-write.

“Write the question,” Joon said. “That is what you do when you do not know. You write the question, so that whoever answers it has to write their name.”

Elias turned to a clean page, and wrote at the top **WHO USED THE CREDENTIAL BEFORE THE COLLAPSE**, and beneath it **AND WHO WAS THE SECOND NAME ON THE 05:02 DISPATCH SHEET**, and beneath that, smaller, **AND WHO DECIDED AT 05:24 THAT THIS WAS AN EQUIPMENT FAILURE — AND WAS IT A PERSON, OR A TEMPLATE THAT A PERSON OPENED EARLY?**

He signed it, dated it, and put the page in the notebook, and then copied it out twice for the lock-box and the Office file, and the hall went on being a hall, and the batteries kept their own time, and nobody in the room said the word *closed*.
