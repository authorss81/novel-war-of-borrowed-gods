# Chapter 25 — The Exit That Is Not There

Emil Baros was the keeper of the lower service run, which meant that on Tuesday morning at nine o'clock he went down it with a clipboard and a torch he did not need, to check the strip lighting and sign the keeper's line, which he had signed more times in three days than at any time in the last eleven years, and which he did with the care of a man who had lost four names in his head in the first week and had decided that his own handwriting was the last thing he was going to be careless about.

He came back up at nine nineteen and found Elias at the top of the ramp with a hand cart, and he stood there for a second before he said anything, which was unlike him.

“It is short,” Emil Baros said. “Short at eleven. I have measured it four times. It ends at eleven meters and there is a wall.”

---

They took the survey sheet and the laser and Joon and nobody else, because there was nothing in a corridor that wanted six people, and because the five-signature promise did not need a meeting for this.

Twenty-two meters of Meridian road between the ramp and the loading lip, in ordinary rain, past a fuel line and a fence with a gap in it that Ru Sabar had been complaining about since Monday. At the loading lip the strip was lit and the run went away into the ground the way it always had: damp concrete, ankle-height emergency strip, a painted band at shoulder height in a color that had been four colors in eleven years.

Emil Baros walked it first, and Petar Lisk was supposed to be at the far end with a tape and was not, and Elias felt the shape of the mistake before he had finished understanding it.

Nobody had told Petar. The run had been thirty-one meters on Monday afternoon, nine pallets of block ago, and there was no reason on the planet to be on the far end of it on Tuesday morning.

“Elias,” Joon said. “Stop.”

He was standing at eleven meters with the laser in his right hand and the case in his left, and the left hand had gone somewhere else entirely, and he had not said so, and Joon had said it for him.

“Left hand's gone,” Elias said. “Give me the case.”

He gave it.

---

At eleven meters the run simply stopped.

There was no rubble, no collapse, no dirt, no shoring, and no sign that anything had happened, because nothing had happened: there was a wall, and the concrete where the run ended was continuous, unpainted, the original pour with a smooth trowel finish and no joint in it anywhere, and it was the same concrete the 2029 section drawing had already drawn as the far end of a thing it was not maintaining.

Elias put his palm against it. It was cold in the ordinary way and solid in the ordinary way, and it was the ordinary Kestrel wall, and he had put his own wedge scars into a different wall in a different place a week ago and had photographed them.

The residue was on the floor, in the last two meters, and it was not residue of a disaster. It was residue of use.

The concrete from nine meters to eleven was polished to a dull shine, the shine a wet week of boots makes in a corridor this narrow, and there was a scuff arc at the ten-meter mark where a loaded dolly had been turned every day for a week and a hand truck, nose down, for eleven years. There was a floor anchor hole at ankle height with dust in it, and a clean crescent in the dust where a boot had been braced. There was a handprint in the dust on the wall at about one meter forty, a right hand, greasy, and it was not small.

Higher up, a length of emergency-strip conduit came out of the wall and stopped in midair, its end face bright and unweathered, with a loop of wire hanging out of it like a dropped stitch.

The paint band that had run the whole length of the corridor at shoulder height stopped dead in the same plane as the floor did, in the same plane as the conduit did, cut off square, as though somebody had laid a sheet of glass against the world and taken everything on one side of it away.

“Eleven point zero,” Joon said, reading the laser. “And the anchor is at ten. And the niche is at twelve, which means the niche is not there either.”

“The niche is not there,” Elias said.

“The niche is not there,” Joon agreed, and wrote it, and his hand was not steady enough for the words he was writing, so he wrote them twice.

---

They stood in it for a while, three men in a corridor that ended at nothing, and Elias had the instrument case open in his right hand and found that he was looking for the next thing to do with it.

“I want to run it again,” he said. “Full length, from the lip, to the datum mark, and I want it photographed against the Saturday sheet before we come up, and I want it in the residents' file and the Office node and the notebook tonight, and I want a second instrument.”

“Why,” Tomas said.

He was not there. That was the first thing Elias had to correct himself about, and the correction arrived before the question finished.

“Why do you want to run it again,” Tomas said, from the top of the ramp, far enough back that Elias had to raise his voice, and he had clearly been there for some time.

“Because one reading is a reading and four is a measurement,” Elias said. “Because the Office will find a way to make this one go away, and the only thing that survives that is a number with a second instrument's name on it.”

“Or,” Tomas said, “you will run it four times this week and it will come out eleven every time, and the fourth time you run it, the thing that is left of the near end will be walked on by fewer boots, and in five weeks you will have a beautiful set of numbers and no run at all, and no resident, and no man behind a wall, because a man behind a wall does not exist to a man with a laser.” He came down the ramp. “Elias, you could measure the rest of that for a year and you would learn exactly how much of it is not there. That is not the same as finding out why, and you have been confusing the two things since Saturday morning, and so has everybody in that hall, and I have been one of them for eight years.”

“Say the rest of it.”

“The rest of it is that the thing you want is not a number,” Tomas said. “The thing you want is for the map to say the right thing. And the map is not going to say the right thing, because there is no arrangement of the evidence that makes the map right, and you know that, and you are going to go down there again in about four days with a bigger instrument because a rescuer who cannot fix it can at least measure it better.”

Elias put the case down on the concrete and stood up with his right hand and let the left one hang.

“All right,” he said. “I am not going to run it again.”

“Thank you.”

“It was not that you were right about the measuring. It was that you were right that I already knew the answer and was looking for a job.”

“That is the same thing said by a better man,” Tomas said, “and I am not going to take the compliment off you in front of a wall.”

---

The second judgment of the morning came from a survivor, and it came in the same way, and Elias took it better.

Emil Baros had stayed at the eleven-meter mark the entire time, and had not touched anything, and when Elias asked him to walk it again and describe the dip and the strip and the door he said no.

“No.”

“Tell me what you remember and I will write it down tonight and it will be on paper in five places.”

“You are not writing it down,” Emil Baros said, without any edge at all, which was worse. “You are going to write down what the instrument says and put my memory in a second column so that when the two disagree you can decide which one to believe. And the instrument is going to win, because the instrument is what you trust, and I am an old man who lost four names in the first week and has never got them back.”

“That is not fair.”

“No,” Emil Baros said. “It is not about fairness. I am going to tell you what I remember anyway, because it is not mine to keep.”

He turned and looked at the wall.

“The dip is at nine and it is a hand deep and it has been there since I was fifty and it is not water, it is the ground. The strip at the ankle has four fittings and the second one from the top is out and has been out since the winter of 'thirty-nine and nobody has ever fixed it because it is not on anybody's work order. The paint is four colors. There was a niche at twelve with a shelf in it, and on the shelf there was a tin of washers and a bar of soap, and I carried the soap out in my pocket the week they closed the west barbers and I have never told anybody that.” He stopped. “And at the far end there was a door, and the door had two handles, and in eleven years of my own feet I have never once been past it — and on Saturday I stood at it because somebody asked me to, and that is the only time.”

“Why,” Elias said.

“Because it did not need me to,” Emil Baros said. “And now you know that the far part of it is not a service run at all. It is a thing that only happens when something is happening, and we are on the wrong side of it, and the thing we have been congratulating ourselves about for a week is that the people who went through it came out.”

---

The hall at eleven was full, which it always was by eleven, and Joon read the whole thing out before anybody was allowed to talk about what it meant.

He read the measurements. He read the residue. He read the conduit stub, the paint band, the handprint, the anchor hole with the crescent in the dust, the missing inspection niche at twelve with its shelf and its tin of washers and its bar of soap, and he read the date on the survey sheet they had clipped in the niche on Saturday, which nobody had thought to take down when they left yesterday, and which was still hanging in a plastic sleeve on a wall that was no longer there.

Nobody said anything about that for a while.

“The index,” Joon said, and turned a terminal around. “I asked it four ways. There is no entry. There is no route line. When I asked it to describe the structure at the lower junction it gave me a wall.”

“Read the last line.”

“It thinks it started on Saturday,” he said. “Because the first thing that ever put it into a system was us. Four findings, two surveys, a photograph, a laser's worth of numbers, and a woman from the Office standing at the top of a ramp. Before that it had a dashed line in a 2029 section drawing, in a different archive, marked *unventilated, not maintained*, which is how a city writes down a place it has decided to stop having opinions about.”

“Read the aisle entry.”

“It is not gone from the log,” Elias said. “Brauer's node has Saturday's copy with the keeper's name in the remarks, and the log entry for box 214, and the 14:12 with the blank subject. And the node is in a building, and boxes get opened in that building.”

“Write that down,” Inez said, and it was written down.

---

Then Mara stood up, which she did not do often and never for applause.

“Before anybody in this room starts arguing about instruments, I am going to say the thing that is on the ledger, and then I am going to sit down and let you all be angry about it.”

She had the medical ledger in her hand, the one with the Office's countersignature across the bottom, and she opened it to the page with the hole in it.

“This is the loan record. It is the page a man withdrew his name from on Friday, and I want everybody to understand that the withdrawal did not touch this. The offer is here. The boundary is here. The gift is here. The return is here, and the return is a wheel, and the time is here, and the countersignature is here, and there is no name on it and none of that matters, because a return condition does not need a name to be a fact.”

She put her finger on it.

“Twenty-three people came out of Kestrel between 05:42 and 06:18. Sixteen of them came through that far run, past the niche, past the dip, through a door with two handles on it, and the only thing that made that run carry a stretcher and a trolley and a man with a handrail through his chest was a hand that had been offered to me by somebody seventeen years from now. And at 06:18 a wheel on a maintenance trolley stopped, and the hand went, and the run stopped being able to carry anybody.”

Nobody moved.

“I am not saying the loan took the corridor,” Mara said. “I do not know that and neither do you, and anybody who tells you today that they know is selling you something. I am saying that the span of that run that has ever carried anybody in a record is inside a window that the loan opened, and that the only window is the loan, and that there is no Saturday without the loan in it. There is no version of Saturday on which sixteen people come out of Car 3 and the pocket under it, because in every version where they come out, the hand is there, and in every version where the hand is there, the far twenty meters is there, and in every version where the far twenty meters is there afterwards, the wheel did not stop.”

She sat down.

“So the honest finding,” Elias said, “is that a rescue worked.”

“Yes,” Mara said. “Elias. We did not break it. That is what I need you to hear before Thursday. Every version of last Saturday that has twenty-three people in this building has the loan in it. We are not being punished. We are being held to the same standard as everybody else, which is that it did not stay.”

Nobody in the hall put that down for about four minutes.

Then somebody at the back said, quietly, “The man at the wall said he uses it.”

“Yes,” Mara said. “He did. That is on the wall in his words, and he is still behind the wall, and we are not going in, because he told us not to and because the promise on this wall has five names on it. And I want it written down in the same hand as the rest of it: **we may have just removed the only way out that did not cost anybody their name.**”

---

The three-column page came down at four in the afternoon, and Elias took it off the wall himself, and Joon put it into the file, and put a cover sheet on it, and wrote on the cover sheet that the page was still true about the service door and was not to be read into the finding that followed it.

The new page went up in the same place, and it had two columns, and neither of them was an answer.

**THE LOWER SERVICE RUN — WHAT IS TRUE**

| **Saturday** | **Tuesday** |
| --- | --- |
| Usable span 31.0 m, laser, two passes, second reader P. Lisk | Usable span 11.0 m, same datum, same laser |
| Keeper's line signed, E. Baros, in his own hand, in the presence of P. Lisk | Keeper's line unsigned. He could not reach it |
| Two frames with a scale in each, in four buildings, and a copy in the Office node | Conduit end face bright and unweathered. Paint band cut square. No break, no fall, no rubble |
| Nine pallets of block, counted twice at 31.0 m, E. Baros and A. Ro, Monday | Handprint at 1.40 m, right hand, grease. Anchor hole with a boot brace in the dust |
| Sixteen people came out of Car 3 and the pocket below it, by paper count | Sixteen people still remember walking it |

And under the two columns, in Joon's hand, three lines.

He read the first one out himself, and read it out badly, and made himself do it twice.

**The span of this run that has ever carried anybody in a record coincides with the window in which the Still Hand loan was open. The return condition for that loan is a wheel, and the time it stopped is in a medical ledger the Office has countersigned. Those two facts are true and they fit.**

Nobody in the hall had anything to add, and Emil Baros, who was standing at the back with the clipboard he could not sign, said the only thing anybody said for a long while, which was that the second wheel of that trolley had a bad bearing and had had one since Wednesday, and that nobody had ever mentioned it, and that it had stopped turning anyway and on its own and at the minute the medical ledger says it did.

“Then it is in the file too,” Joon said, and wrote it down, and did not put it under either column.

**We cannot show a cause. We will not write one. A cause we cannot carry is worse than a gap, and we have a gap.**

**The service door is not in the table. Its status is unchanged and it is not to be settled by anything on this page.**

And then, at the very bottom, in Inez's chalk, the sentence the hall read twice and did not argue with:

**A ROUTE THAT WAS WALKED YESTERDAY BY A NAMED PERSON WITH A WITNESS, AND THAT A MAN BEHIND A WALL ASKED US ON SUNDAY NOT TO LET CLOSE, IS NOT THERE THIS AFTERNOON, AND THE WHEEL THAT TOOK IT BACK IS ON A PAGE EVERYBODY SIGNED.**
