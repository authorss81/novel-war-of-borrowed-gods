# Chapter 17 — The Same Relay

Tomas waited until the hall went quiet, and then he waited some longer, which Elias noticed and did not comment on.

The boiler door at the back of the union hall opened onto a concrete step and a bin store and eleven feet of standing room, and the walls on either side were thick enough that a person inside could not hear a person outside, which is presumably why Tomas had chosen it. He was sitting on the step with his forearms on his knees and no coat on, in the cold, at half past midnight, with a folded sheet of paper in his hand that he had been holding without opening.

“Sit,” he said. “Not in there. Out here.”

Elias sat.

“The east leg signal cabinet,” Tomas said. “At 05:40 yesterday morning, before anything else, you said it was drawing power it should not have. You said it to your sister in the relay room. Do you remember what you told Control about it?”

“I said the cabinet was drawing power it should not have.”

“You did not say where you had heard it. Did you think about it?”

“No.”

“I did,” Tomas said. “I heard it in your voice at about nine o'clock the following evening, over the repeater, when you were telling Joon to log the relay room as a location of interest. And I sat here and did not say anything, and then I went to bed, and I have been carrying it for about a day and a half.”

He unfolded the paper. It was a photocopy of a page from an incident report, and it was very old, and the header read **KESTREL TUNNEL / STRUCTURAL AND ROUTING REVIEW / 2034**, and half the body of it had been blacked out with a marker in a hand that was not Tomas's.

“I was the duty safety officer,” he said. “On the day the roof came in. I had been in the post four months.”

He put a finger on the unblacked top third of the page.

“Same thing. East leg cabinet, unexpected draw, a signal check that returned values for a circuit that had been cut in 2031. My report started the way your morning started. I have the original in a box in my flat and I have not opened it since 2035 because I know exactly what is in it and I did not want to know whether I had been wrong.”

Elias said nothing. It was one of the harder things he had done in a week.

“The pressure cycles,” Tomas went on. “In 2034 we called them *structural breathing* and we were half right. Breathing is what it looked like from the platform. It was not the structure. There is a node under the west apron, paired to the relay you have been living on top of since Saturday, and when the two of them talk to each other through whatever they talk through, the ground under Kestrel moves on a cycle you can set your watch by. In 2034 we lost two hundred metres of platform slab in four minutes. Same cycle. Same interval as the tape on that cabinet, if your bell man is as honest as he sounds.”

“Why did nobody go and look at it?”

“Because it is not in the index,” Tomas said. “It was written off in 2035. There is a line in the report about legacy communications equipment of unknown configuration, and the line is one of the ones somebody marked, and after that the node is not a device, it is a rumour that a man who used to hold a post has told a tunnel. My mark on the 2035 write-off is the second signature. The first is not visible from here.”

Elias felt the sentence arrive and sit down heavily in the middle of everything he had assumed about this week.

“Is that the same credential format,” he said.

“I do not know. I never saw the number. I signed a page with the first line covered up, and I did not ask, and that is a thing I did, and it is on the report, in my own hand, at the bottom, where I wrote that I had not sought clarification of the equipment classification.” He folded the paper again. “You wanted to know what history can be disclosed before a re-entry. That is the history, and that is as far as I can go, and the rest of the report is blacked out by people who were not me.”

---

They sat with that for a while. Inside the hall somebody turned over in a sleep and a cot frame took the weight badly.

“There is a second thing,” Tomas said, “and it is worse, and I have been trying since Friday to write it in a way that is not an excuse.”

“Say it badly.”

“In 2034 there was a rule. There was one instruction, one route, and no contradiction in the running order, and the senior safety officer could close a route and reroute a crew without asking the crew. One voice. It is not an Office rule and it is not a law. It is a safety-panel rule, adopted in 2029 after Meridian, when four crews got two different instructions inside the same minute and eleven people died of it. I was on the panel that wrote it. I was not a junior member of the panel. I was the one who said that a crew that argues with itself at the threshold is a crew that dies at the threshold, and I was right, and I was also the one who had to say what a crew does instead of arguing.”

“You signed the reroute,” Elias said.

“I signed the reroute. The roof was going on the platform level and the rescue service had a passage open on the service level that was not on the plan and was not on anybody's authority, and the rule I had written said the senior officer closes the route and moves the crew, and I closed it, and I moved your crew off the platform and onto the service level, and Samuel was on the service level already, holding a frame, with a passage he had opened that nobody had authorised and could not be closed from the far end.”

“You could not close it from the far end,” Elias said. “You told me that.”

“I told you the second version. The first version, which is what is in the 2034 file, is that a route was closed and a crew was rerouted and a foreman was left holding a frame that the rule had authorised him to hold, because the rule said one voice and the frame was not the voice.” Tomas looked at his hands. “I have given you the second version for eight years because the second version has a place in it where somebody could have done something else. The first version has a rule in it, and the rule is mine.”

The rain came off the awning in a slow, uneven way that neither of them listened to.

“I am not telling you this so that you forgive it,” Tomas said. “I am telling you because we are going to argue about a survey tomorrow morning, and one of the arguments will be somebody saying *the crew needs one voice*, and I need you to hear where that sentence comes from, and I need you to hear it from me before you hear it from a person who wants a route open.”

“All right.”

“All right?”

“You said it. I heard it. I am not going to tell you it is fine and we are not going to argue about it tonight.” Elias braced his right palm against the step edge. “But I am not going to let you be the only person who ever says the word *rule* out loud in this building, because that is how it worked in 2034.”

“That,” Tomas said, “is the first sensible thing you have said to me since Saturday, and I am going to be careful not to reward it.”

---

“There is a part of it I have not earned,” he said, a little later. “There is a sentence. It has to do with how I taught you.”

Elias waited.

“The caution. I gave you caution for eight years. I made you promise before you knew the facts, and I made you carry the promise, and I made it sound like the making was the whole of it.” He rubbed the back of his neck. “I have thought about whether that is why you cannot let a route be undocumented. I do not think I get to say that yet. Not until I am not the only person in the room who is frightened.”

“Fine,” Elias said. “It will keep.”

“I have it written down,” Tomas said. “That is not a figure of speech. It is in a notebook in the same box as the 2034 report, and when this is over and the Office has finished with us, you can read it, and you can do whatever you want with it, and if you want to use it against me in a review I will not fight you on it.”

He held out the folded photocopy, then took it back and put it in his own pocket, because it was evidence and he had spent eight years knowing what happens to evidence that changes hands.

“Not tonight,” Elias agreed. “Tomorrow you put it in the duplicate ledger, with Inez's box, the way we did the credential. Then it does not live in your flat.”

Tomas looked at him sharply, and then almost smiled. “You are better at this than I was at your age, and I am going to have to spend the rest of my life being pleased and appalled about it in the same breath.”

---

“The node,” Elias said. “If it is under the west apron, then it is the one thing in Kestrel that we can look at without going through the door that changed.”

“It is also the one thing in Kestrel that an equipment classification was filed about eight hours before anybody knew a carriage was involved.” Tomas unfolded a different sheet, blank, and squared it on his knee. “So I am not giving you a survey. I am giving you a stop plan, and the stop plan is a separate document, and it is not approved by anybody, and you are allowed to throw it away.”

He wrote as he talked, in a hand that shook slightly and did not slow down for it.

“Before anybody goes below the west apron, these are written. One: I set a displacement limit on the shoring at the apron, and when the instruments say it, it is finished, and I do not get to argue with the instruments. Two: gas, continuously, and if it fails, everybody out, including the instrument operator. Three: no door is opened. Not the service door, not a new one, not a door we find. The survey looks and measures and comes back. Four: the whole team comes out together. Nobody is left holding anything, because in 2034 a man was left holding a frame and it was in a rule and it was my signature at the bottom of it.” He turned the sheet around. “And five. Any one of five people can call the stop. You, me, Mara, Joon, and Inez, and the hall can call it by sending anybody out the door, and the hall does not need to know what the survey is for.”

“You are giving residents a veto on a technical decision.”

“I am giving residents a veto on a decision that will happen in their building, with their current, on a day they voted for.” He set the pen down. “And I am giving you one, because you are the one who will want to continue, and I am the one who wrote the rule that says continue, and neither of us should be allowed to be the only voice in that room.”

Elias read the sheet. It was five lines and it was the first plan in four days that he was not the author of.

“I will take it,” he said. “And I want one change.”

“Name it.”

“Add that I take it on the day it is written, and it expires.” Elias held the sheet out. “If this is in a drawer in six weeks and somebody produces it as a procedure, it becomes a rule with my name on it, and then we are back to 2029. Make it a piece of paper for one survey, and let it die.”

Tomas took it and wrote the expiry in, and initialled it, and gave it back, and then did not move.

“I have one line left,” he said, “and I am going to say it once, and I am not going to soften it, and I would like you to hear it as information and not as an answer, because I have no standing to give you an answer about him and I have told myself that for eight years and tonight I am admitting that telling myself that was a way of keeping the sentence for later.”

The bin store dripped.

“I will not sign another reroute that moves people who did not agree to be moved,” Tomas said. “That is mine to give up and I am giving it up. And I will never tell you that your father had no choice, and I will never tell you that he did. Both of those would be me telling you who your father was, and I was there and you were twenty-seven and crying about a girl you could not name, and neither of us was in a position to do it honestly.”

Elias found that his eyes had gone hot, and that the heat arrived without any memory attached to it at all, which was the one mercy the loan had left him.

“All right,” he said.

Tomas stood up and put his coat on, and at the boiler door he stopped with his hand on the frame.

“For what it is worth,” he said, “I read the Bell file copy this evening. The sister, the run sheet, the missing row.”

“Yes.”

“You left her name out.”

“She asked me to.”

“You did not have to.”

“She asked,” Elias said, “and I did not have to. Those are two different facts and I want the second one in the record as well, because in about a year somebody is going to tell this story as a story about a rescuer who learned the right lesson, and I would like the copy on the wall to say that I did not want to and she made me.”

Tomas looked at him for a moment longer than the sentence warranted.

“Put it in,” he said, “and go to bed. You are going to be on your feet in six hours and you are going to want the sleep more than you want the last word with me, and tonight is the only night you will get it.”
