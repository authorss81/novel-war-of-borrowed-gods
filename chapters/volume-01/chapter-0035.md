# Chapter 35 — The Circuit

The woman who came across on Wednesday morning at ten past four came to the hall at nine on Thursday, because she had slept four hours in a hostel on the east bank and then walked the length of the city to find a building she had read about in a paper, and she was carrying a paper coat and a card nobody had given her.

She said her name was Bess Ferran and that she was thirty-one and that she had been a night-shift phlebotomist at the Meridian hospital since March, and that at ten past four on Wednesday morning a man with a clipboard had offered her a bed and a shift and a place in a queue, and that she had said yes, and that she had gone through a door in Calder Street and come out of one in a place she did not recognize, on the far side, at eleven hundred and six hours, with two other people and no idea at all where she was.

“You came back,” Elias said.

“No,” Bess Ferran said. “I did not come back. I got to a telephone box on Ash Street and I asked the woman in it where the hospital was, and she said *this is Ash Street*, and I said yes, and she said *I know*, and she looked at me for a while, and then she said the hall was at the end of it.” She put a hand on the table, flat, the way people in that hall did. “I got back because somebody had written *Ash Street* on a piece of paper in my handwriting and given it to me at the door, and it is the only reason I am standing here and I do not know whose idea that was.”

Nobody in the hall knew whose idea that was, and Joon wrote *source of the address unknown, subject's own hand* on a card and pinned it, which was the first time in three weeks he had pinned a thing that was not evidence of anything except that a person had been careful.

“And the room,” Bess Ferran said. “You are going to ask about the room and I am going to tell you about the room, and I want it written down that I am telling you and not that somebody told me to and I did not know.”

---

There were nine of them in a room on Calder Street above a shuttered print shop, and the room had two chairs, a bench, a kettle, a portable heater, and nine people standing in a loose ring about two meters across with their feet on a line of tape.

That was the part that ended Elias's first three questions before he asked them. It was not a queue. A queue is a line and a line has a front. This was a ring, and the ring had a man in the middle of it with a clipboard, and the tape on the floor went all the way around, and the nine of them were standing on it.

“They told us we were waiting,” Bess Ferran said. “There is a list. There is a bed at the end of it, on the east bank, with three days of power on it, and I have been in a hostel for five weeks and I have a bad shoulder and a card that is not a card. And there is a woman there who is very kind and there is a man with a clipboard and he writes everybody's name down and he tells you the number.”

“The number of what,” Joon said.

“He calls it *the pair*,” Bess Ferran said. “And I thought he meant people waiting in twos. And on the second day he asked one of us to step out and the rest of us felt it, and I thought it was the heater.”

Mara stood up.

“Two meters across. Nine people. Closed ring, no exits inside the ring. How long.”

“All night. Eleven at night to six in the morning, and then you go and sleep somewhere. Wednesday night is tonight. It is the third night for two of the people in there.”

“Who is the pair for,” Mara said. “What is the load?”

“I do not know,” Bess Ferran said. “I know that a man came in on Wednesday evening and put a hand on the outside of the ring and said *three* and that something in the room got easier, and that two people who had been in it since Monday put their hands on the seats of the chairs and I understood then that they were the other half of it.”

“Two loads,” Elias said, “or one load with a bridge. Either way it is not eight people. It is a number.”

“It is a number,” Bess Ferran said. “And they are not told which number. That is what I came to tell you.”

---

The argument about whether to go in took fifty minutes and nobody won it and everybody's position was on the table afterward.

The route the licensed broker is allowed to offer, Inez said, is a route through a branch, and a route needs a load, and the load is made of people, and the license does not prohibit the load. It requires consent recorded, a witness, and a return, and Bess Ferran had signed a piece of paper at ten past four on Wednesday morning that said something about *voluntary participation in a group stability arrangement* and had not read it because she has never in her life read the second line of anything.

“We go and stand in that hall and explain it,” Elias said, “and four or five of them come out, and the pair drops, and whatever is in the middle of that ring tonight stops being available, and Adare will have a form about it in the morning, and it will say the form was complied with.”

“That is what is going to happen,” Doss said, from the wall, where he had been standing the whole time. “And I want it on the record that I am not arguing for the other thing. I am arguing that the four who come out walk out into a city at one in the morning with no bed, and that this hall has a bad bed situation, and that if the people who come out have nowhere to sleep then the route did not stop and the circuit did not stop, it just moved outside.”

“Then we get them beds,” Mara said.

“We do not have beds,” Mara said, to herself, and everybody heard it.

Elias stood in the middle of a room where the five domains each held a piece of it and started to build a plan, and got about nine words into it, and stopped, and said, out loud, in front of everybody:

“That is a rescue. Ansel can break this and I am not going to make him.”

Ansel Duarte looked up from the mat by the radiator. He had a book open on his knees that he was not reading and had not been reading for some time.

“I am not breaking it,” he said.

“You could have. You had it. You had it in your hand before I finished the sentence.”

“Yes,” Ansel said. “And I am choosing not to, and I want you to notice that I am choosing not to, because in about three weeks there will be a Thursday where I break something, and on that day you are going to look at me and think *I am the reason the building stopped*, and I want you to have had the other version in your head first. Today I did not break it because the four people who want out are adults and they are not in a hole and nobody in this room has standing over any of them, including me. Break my clause the day somebody is in a hole. Do not waste it on a room with a kettle in it.”

He turned the page of a book he had not been reading.

“That is the first time you have used my sentence on yourself,” he said. “I am going to sit with that for a while and I would rather you did not talk to me about it for an hour.”

---

They did not go in.

They went across to Calder Street at half past eleven at night, four of them, and stood on the pavement in the rain in front of a shuttered print shop, and the door of the room above it was open four inches and there was a light on and a kettle going.

Bess Ferran went up and came back down and said, “They will not come down for you. He will not come down. But you can say it up there and he will not stop you, because you are not a customer and he does not want to look at a person who is not a customer.”

So Elias said it from the bottom of the stairs, to nine people and a man with a clipboard, in about six minutes, and he said it plainly, without a plan and without a card.

He said the ring was a load. He said the load was being used to hold a route. He said the people in it had not been told and had signed something about a *group stability arrangement*. He said the number the man with the clipboard was counting was the number of people the route needed and not the number of people who were available. He said the licensed broker was allowed to do this and had a form and had complied with the form. He said the Office could send an observer and had not sent one and was not obliged to on a Thursday, and that the condition in the license is that an observer *may* attend.

He said none of it as an accusation and all of it as information, and he did not once say *get out*, because they were not his and he had learned that in a corridor in May from a boy on a mat.

And the woman who was very kind asked, from the top of the stairs, in a voice that carried perfectly, “What would you do instead, if you were us.”

“I do not know,” Elias said. “I have thought about it all the way here and I do not have an answer and I am not going to invent one for a person standing in a ring at midnight. What I have is a building on Ash Street with a bad bed situation, a kettle, and a woman with a shoulder. It is not better. I am telling you it exists and I am not telling you it is an offer, because it is not one yet.”

Two men came down the stairs inside four minutes and did not go up again. One of them was twenty-six and had a daughter on the other side of the city. The other was a man named Gil Trejo, fifty-four, who had a bus ticket in his hand and who said, on the pavement, to nobody, “I have been standing in that for three nights telling myself it was a queue.”

Nobody else came down, and that was also the finding.

---

The load dropped at twenty past one in the morning, and the circuit was interrupted, and nobody broke anything.

Wendell Pryce came down at one and opened the door of the shop and stood in the rain and said, “It is easier now. Two of them left and it is easier, and in about four minutes it is going to be very much easier, and then in about ten minutes somebody who is standing on a route is going to find out what that means.”

His name was on nothing. He had no card and no hall and no list, and he had told the team, through a door, on Wednesday, exactly what he was standing in, and why he was standing in it.

“I was in one of these in Cordell in thirty-six,” Wendell Pryce said. “I know what the ring is. I have known since Tuesday night. I have a bad hip and a bed at the end of this and I have paid ninety dollars and I know what the ninety dollars is for and I would like it, if I may, to keep paying it.”

“You may,” Elias said.

“I am not asking you for permission, son. I am telling you so you cannot write down later that nobody was asked.”

At twenty-six past one, a man came down the stairs of the shop on Ash Street — not Calder Street — eleven meters past a loading lip, in a light rain, on the wrong side of a wall that had not been there in his life.

Teodor had bought a crossing on Tuesday night for one hundred and forty dollars, with his own name on the paper, because his mother was in a branch apartment on the far side of the Meridian with a bad hip and a landlord who wanted the rent, and the Office had nothing and the hospital had nothing and the only thing anybody had ever offered him was the thing with the form on it.

The route went out at about the same moment that the ring lost two people, and Teodor was halfway across it, and Teodor came back.

He came back the way you come back from a thing that stopped existing, which is on your hands and knees in the dark on a loading lip, and he was on our side and he was not all right and he did not know it for about four minutes.

Bess Ferran and two of the hall's women were there because the hall had a watch on Ash Street that night without anybody calling it a watch. Doss got him inside. Lena Mir looked at him for ninety seconds and said he was going to be sick and gave him a bowl.

When he could speak he said, and Joon wrote it down exactly: “I was on it. I was on it and I could see the other side, and then it went out like a lamp and I was on this side, and I want somebody to tell me what that was.”

“That was a route,” Elias said, “and it was powered by people in a ring who were not told, and the ring lost two people, and that is all I know and it is more than I knew this morning.”

“My mother,” Teodor said.

“Yes,” Elias said.

He sat on the step of the hall in the rain and did not say anything for a long time, and then he said the thing that became the sentence of the week, to nobody, in the flat voice of a man who has just been told yes by an institution and no by his own legs.

“Nobody asked me if I wanted to be a door.”

---

The reason the circuit keeps working is a kettle, a bench, a bed at the end of a list, ninety dollars, and a man who counts.

Elias said it to the board on Thursday night because somebody had to say it out loud before the room built a plan on top of it, and he said it flatly, and Inez wrote it in her chalk and did not soften one word of it.

**THEY ARE NOT CROOKS. THEY ARE CHEAPER THAN WE ARE AND SAFER THAN WE ARE, AND BOTH OF THOSE ARE TRUE, AND WE HAVE BEEN LOSING THAT ARGUMENT SINCE FRIDAY NIGHT AND WE HAVE BEEN LOSING IT WRONG.**

Nobody argued with it. At the bottom of the board, under everything, Petar Lisk had written a line that was not about the circuit at all, and that Inez had let stay, because it was true and because it was the whole of what Thursday had cost.

**WE DID NOT GO ON THURSDAY. WE SAID NOTHING TO ANYBODY. HE WAS NOT THERE AND WE DID NOT TELL HIM ANYTHING, BECAUSE THAT IS WHAT WE SAID WE WOULD DO.**
