# Chapter 34 — The Offer

The letter came at ten past nine on Wednesday morning, on its own, in a plain envelope, with no courier and no clerk, exactly as she had said she would send it, and the fact that she had kept to that on a day when she had broken nothing else made it the most alarming object in the building.

It was two pages. Elias read it standing up and then sat down and read it again.

The Office would conduct a controlled re-entry inside the Kestrel provisional classification. Office instruments, Office survey vehicle, an Office continuity chain, and two certified confined-space technicians dispatched from the regional pool — not borrowed, not lent, assigned, with names on the assignment. The medical standard would be written by Mara Okafor and would be a present standard: no borrowed capacity sought, solicited, or relied on inside the entry window. The chain's resident observer would be the Cinder Quarter's, appointed by the hall, with the right to stop and the right to see every page.

In exchange: Elias Rook's original notebook; the raw audio, including the eleven seconds; and the observer's read line on the sealed data node.

“It is a good offer,” Joon said, about forty seconds in, which was the most alarming sentence anybody had said in that hall since the first week.

“It is a good offer,” Sable's letter agreed, in the fourth paragraph, in the Director's own words rather than the office's, which meant she had written that paragraph at home. “I want to be direct about why, because a reader will assume it is a seizure dressed as help. It is not. We have two hundred and ten meters of migrating void in a contractor survey from 2031 and nobody has looked at it since, and a seam site with an unexamined void under an apron that people walk over is a thing that eventually happens to somebody, and when it does the question in front of every inquiry in this city will be why nobody looked, and the answer will be *because the neighborhood preferred not to have anybody look*, and that answer will be true, and it will be true because of a form you filed yesterday with my own signature on it. I would rather look and be blamed than not look and be thanked.”

Nobody in the room had an answer to that either.

Doss did the arithmetic instead, out loud, because he was in the room and because it was his arithmetic.

“Two technicians,” he said. “A pair. From the regional pool, with names on it, which means they have done it before and they will know what they are doing and they will not be improvising in a hole that ate twenty meters of itself on Tuesday.” He looked at Tomas. “I have been the only ticket in this building since the thirteenth. One ticket and one safety instructor is not a crew. It is a man and a man watching him. If the Service ever puts anybody in a confined space down there off my own assessment, that is the day somebody dies of a thing that was a shortage of rope, and everybody afterwards will say the ground took him.”

“Then it is a good offer,” Elias said.

“It is an offer that solves the one problem I have had for eight days that I cannot solve myself,” Doss said, “and I have been ashamed of that problem for eight days, and I am not going to stand in this hall and pretend the shame is a reason to say no.”

---

Tomas had the hardest part and took it straight.

“The chain names a responsible individual,” he said. “It always does. A continuity chain is a legal document and a legal document needs a person on it who can be sued, and if that person is not a name then the chain is a piece of paper and everybody in it knows it, and the first time it matters it will not be a piece of paper. So somebody in this room is going to put his name on a chain that goes under the apron. I would like it to be mine, and I would like everybody to understand that that is not vanity, it is that I am the only structural person and a chain with somebody else's name on it is a lie of a specific and useful kind.”

“Fine,” Elias said. “And what does it cost.”

“It costs that in five years, when somebody queries the chain, the query comes back with a name on it, and the name is a man whose signature is second on the 2034 write-off of the node under that apron.” He did not raise his voice, and it did not need him to. “I have spent eight years being the man who holds a thing together. I would like the record to be complete instead, and I would like it to be complete in a building I cannot see, and I would like it to be complete before the entry and not after, so that nobody can ever say afterwards that the entry and the disclosure were the same transaction.”

Mara looked at him for a long moment.

“Write the disclosure yourself,” she said. “Not the Office's version, not Vane's, not a summary. Your own words, with the date, and the panel, and the reroute, and the part where you sat on this for eight years while I have been treating the people it killed. I will countersign the medical standard tonight. I am not signing anything next to a summary of you.”

“That is fair,” Tomas said, and looked slightly ill, and signed the disclosure sheet there and then on the back of the letter, in his own hand, in nine lines, and Petar Lisk read it out in the hall twice because that is what happens to documents in that building.

---

Joon found the last line of the exchange at half past ten, and he found it by counting what was left, which is what he did.

“Four things on the table and I want them separated. The notebook, the raw audio, and the read line. Three things. And the entry. One thing. And that is four, and the letter is written as though it is a swap, and it is not a swap, because the three are not a price for the entry, they are a price for the entry being *ours*.”

“Explain it to people who cannot follow you,” Elias said.

“The Office is offering to do the entry we are not allowed to do, on a chain, with a pair, and to hand us the observer's seat so that the residents' file gets every page.” Joon put his finger on the third line. “What we give up for that is the only window this building has ever had into what the Office actually holds about our own ground. After today, when the hall asks a question about Kestrel, the answer comes out of a building we cannot walk into, and the hall can no longer open the file and read it ourselves, and that is not a small thing, because since Saturday the residents' observer condition has been the only reason any of us could check them.”

“So the counter is that they get the entry and we keep the read line,” Elias said.

“The counter is that they get the entry, *and* they get the disclosure, *and* they get the raw audio, *and* we keep the notebook and we keep the read line, and the five-name promise is written onto the chain as a condition the crew works under, and the roster indexing is produced before anybody goes down.” He looked up. “And we do not ask the Office to be bound by the hall's conditions, because that is a thing no institution has ever agreed to and asking for it is how you find out that you were the only ones who thought it was on the table.”

Nobody in the room said anything, and the thing nobody said was: we are the ones who wrote the conditions, and we are the ones asking not to be bound by them, and that is either the most honest document this week has produced or the most convenient.

---

Sable's answer came at ten past four, by the same route, in nine lines, and it was the answer of an administrator who had been given something she wanted and had decided to be exact about the price.

The entry is authorized and dated to Friday, the twentieth, at first light, subject to weather and to the medical standard. The two technicians are named and assigned. The disclosure is received and filed and is in the file before the entry, and she noted with what she called deliberate unkindness that the file now had his name on it and hers and that neither of them could take it back.

The five-name promise is attached as a condition of crew participation. She attached it herself, unasked, and wrote the sentence: *A local undertaking that the Office has no power to enforce is nonetheless a condition the Office will honor, and if it is broken the entry stops, and the Office will not be the party that says it may continue.*

What she refused was the binding clause. One line. *The Office cannot accept, and will not be represented as accepting, any undertaking that binds it to the decisions of a residents' body or a local cooperative, for the reason set out in my answer to Finding 4, and I would ask that nobody in the hall misunderstand the difference between a condition the Office honors and a law the Office obeys.*

“Good,” Elias said, to everybody, immediately, without thinking, and then heard himself, and put a hand flat on the table.

“Good?”

“Good because it is true, and I have watched true things get refused in this room all month for the crime of being convenient.” He took the hand off the table. “And I want it minuted that I said *good* out loud and on the first try, and that four days ago I would have written a page about how that sentence was the whole problem.”

Joon wrote it down and read it back, which he now did without being asked, and which cost everybody in the room a small amount of pride.

---

The discovery was not made by the team.

Inez had demanded the broker's routing log at twenty to nine in the morning, standing in the hall, in front of a witness, using the reporting clause of a license she had not wanted, because she had read the seventy-two hours out loud to herself twice in the bath and had worked out that a duty with a clock on it is a duty that gets met in a hurry, and that a clerk in a hurry at nine in the morning is a clerk who answers the first question and the second.

It came back at eleven. Two events since the license was issued. One at twenty-one fifty on Tuesday, one subject. One at ten past four this morning, three subjects.

She read the second one out loud twice.

```text
Branch routing — licensed
Site: Kestrel provisional 4C
Broker of record: R. ADARE
Subjects: 3
Consent: on file
Witness: on file
Return: on file
```

“Three,” Inez said. “At ten past four this morning, while this hall was reading a letter about a rope pair. Three people went through a branch route inside a designated seam site, on a license issued at nine o'clock last night, and the form says three fields are on file and the form does not say what is in them.”

“Where did they go,” Elias said.

“That is not on it.”

“Where they came *from* is not on it either,” Joon said, “which is worse, because that is the field that would tell us whether any of them is somebody we have ever spoken to, and the form has a box for a time and a box for a subject count and a box for three words about consent and no box at all for a street.”

Doss had been reading over his shoulder.

“Ten past four is shift change at the Meridian hospital,” he said. “And a quarter past five is shift change at the container terminal. And a route that needs a pair of feet on both ends works best when the street is full of people who will not remember a door.”

Inez put the sheet down and did not pick it up again for a while.

“So it is working,” she said. “Not threatening. Not on its way. *Working.* Overnight, lawfully, with a form, on a license, under a woman who has never once lied to this room, and the three people in it are not on our twenty-two and they are not on our twenty-three and they are not on any list anybody in this building has ever read, and if it went wrong tonight there is nobody to notice until the morning, and the form will still say the three words about consent.”

“That is what an offer does,” Sable's letter said, four pages earlier, in the fourth paragraph, in a Director's own hand. *It is a piece of paper. I know exactly what paper is worth, because I am the institution that made paper worth what it is worth.*

Nobody in the hall said the word they were all thinking, which was that on Friday morning the hall was going to be the only building in a mile that knows where three people went at ten past four in the morning, and that the only reason it knows is that the license has a reporting clause, and that the license exists because a woman with a hundred and ninety pages of institutional reasoning sat down and decided that a record was better than nothing.

Petar Lisk wrote the count up on the board at seven, because Petar Lisk wrote everything up on the board at seven, and the count was not a count of people. It was a count of boxes.

**THE FORM HAS A BOX FOR THE NUMBER AND A BOX FOR THREE WORDS. IT DOES NOT HAVE A BOX FOR A STREET. SOMEBODY DECIDED THAT ON PURPOSE, AND THE SOMEBODY WAS A FORM DESIGNER IN AN OFFICE, AND HE NEVER MET ANY OF US.**
