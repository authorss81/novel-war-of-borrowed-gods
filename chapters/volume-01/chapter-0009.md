# Chapter 9 — The People Behind the Wall

The lower junction had changed again.

When Elias and Mara reached it with Tomas, the altered wall stood where the service corridor should have continued. A strip of daylight showed through a horizontal gap near the floor, but the Civic Spine terminal marked the space as solid concrete. Water pushed through the gap in short pulses, carrying dust and the smell of burned cable.

Joon had brought the radio, a paper route sheet, and a second thermal camera. “The relay is still carrying the signal. It points through that gap.”

The terminal gave them a different instruction.

**RECOMMENDED RE-ENTRY: NORTH SERVICE ROUTE. DO NOT ALTER APPROVED ACCESS.**

Tomas read it twice. “That route is not on the floor.”

“It is the only route in the index,” Joon said.

“Then the index is not a survey.”

Mara checked the oxygen cylinder at her hip. “We have enough for careful movement, not a crowd. I am taking the nonwalking patient first, then anyone with a breathing injury. We bring the rest in pairs.”

A weak voice came from behind the wall.

“Please.”

Elias dropped to one knee beside the gap and called, “How many are you?”

“Seven,” a woman answered. “One cannot walk. The air gets bad near the back.”

“Who is speaking?”

“Nadi Bell.”

Elias looked at Joon. Nadi’s name was not on the Car 3 manifest. It was not on the paper count. He wrote it on a fresh line anyway.

“Can you see the source of the water?”

“Yes. There is a service alcove. The wall moved when the train moved. We stayed in the alcove.”

Tomas examined the gap with a probe. The concrete above it was carrying a lateral load, and the seam of light narrowed whenever the trolley motor changed pitch. “We can widen it enough for a person to crawl through, but not enough for a stretcher. We need a frame before the next pressure cycle.”

“I can get one,” Elias said.

“Not alone,” Tomas replied. “You take the rope. I take the brace. Mara takes the patient. Joon keeps the count and the signal.”

The order was sensible. It was also the first plan of the day that Elias had not built around himself.

He set two mechanical wedges at the base of the wall and threaded a line through the old service pipe. The metal was pitted, but it was still fixed to a support column. He tested it with his right hand. His left hand rested against the concrete and reported nothing.

The line went taut.

“Ready,” Tomas said.

Elias pulled. The wedges held as a pressure pulse struck the wall, and the line redirected the settling concrete just enough for a section to shift sideways. The opening was ragged and barely wide enough for a person to pass. Air rushed out carrying smoke and wet fabric. A man crawled through first, coughing. A woman followed, then a teenage boy carrying a fire extinguisher as if it were a tool worth saving.

Nadi Bell came next. She was perhaps forty, with soot across her cheeks and a bloodless cut above one eyebrow. She was carrying the hand of a young man who had collapsed beside her. His legs were trapped under a fallen panel.

“This is Ansel,” she said. “He cannot walk.”

Mara moved to the gap. “Ansel, can you hear me?”

A weak cough answered.

“Keep your head turned. We are going to move the panel, not your leg. If the pressure changes, tell us before you move.”

The young man said, “I can feel the wall.”

“Good. Keep feeling it.”

Elias shifted the rope to a higher anchor. The line was not holding the wall open by itself. It was keeping the panel from sliding when the pressure dropped. Tomas placed a hydraulic brace against the fallen section. The metal frame groaned when he tightened it.

Nadi looked at the brace, then at Elias. “What happens to us if that fails?”

“The water takes the gap. We have a second line, but it is not safe.”

“Is anyone going to be used to hold the wall?”

“No,” Elias said. “We use steel and people who have agreed to move. No one becomes part of the wall.”

Nadi searched his face. Whatever she found made her nod. “Then I will help. I can pass the rope.”

Elias told her what the line could do and where not to stand. She repeated the warning back before touching it. He gave her the middle mark, not the anchor, and kept his right hand on the brake.

Behind the gap, the relay clicked.

Nell’s voice appeared in the radio, close and clear for a second.

“Rook, the east branch is open. Follow the—”

Static swallowed the rest.

The old maintenance channel opened on the terminal. A route appeared in yellow, leading away from the wall toward the north stair. For a moment Elias saw a place where the light was steady and a door waited with Nell’s hand on its frame.

The route was not a map. It was a possibility.

He looked at Ansel, then at the seven people from the alcove. Nadi’s group had one injured passenger, one cylinder, and a wall that was already changing.

Mara called from the gap. “We need the line now.”

Elias released the brake. The north route vanished from the terminal.

The future voice came through the relay in a rough whisper.

Mara Okafor, seventeen years ahead, said, “You need another body.”

It was not an offer. It was a recognition.

Elias closed his eyes. The first loan had ended at the third wheel. The return condition was complete. He had no right to make that completed choice mean more than it was.

“Can you extend?” he asked anyway. “Just for the person behind the wall.”

The answer came after a breath that felt older than the station.

“No.”

The word was plain and unaltered.

“I can keep one living body from failing while another person repairs it. Tarin was that body. The wheel closed this exchange. I will not reopen or stretch that promise for another rescue. I will not hold two lives, and I will not make Ansel’s crossing depend on a promise I cannot keep.”

Elias could have argued. He could have said that the return had been forced by a technical change, that the people were dying, that the future voice had seen a branch where everyone survived. He could have made the case that a future self who offered a gift should understand its purpose.

Instead he said, “Understood.”

The relief in the relay sounded like grief. “Keep the first group together. Bring them out. Do not follow the branch for me.”

The channel closed.

Elias opened his eyes. His left hand had begun to ache again, a deep pain that rose from the wrist to the elbow. The refused extension left no gift in it. Only the memory of asking, and the knowledge that the answer had cost someone else a choice.

Mara saw his face. “What did she say?”

“She will not cover more than one body.”

“Did you accept?”

“No.”

Mara nodded once. “Then keep the line moving.”

Ansel was free enough to crawl. Elias and Tomas raised the fallen panel with the hydraulic brace. The young man came through on his elbows, coughing into his sleeve. The remaining two passengers followed: a woman with a broken wrist and an older man with white hair. He carried a child’s school coat and insisted that they had all been on the train. When he reached the refuge, he pressed the coat into Nadi’s arms.

“There were only seven of us,” Nadi said. “We were not in Car 3.”

“Write that,” Joon said from the line. “Write where you were, what you saw, and who crossed. Do not make the list fit the train.”

Joon’s paper count now held twenty-three names. He read them aloud as the group entered the refuge. Sixteen bodies had come from Car 3 and the maintenance pocket. Seven had been behind the wall. The arithmetic was simple. The terminal’s empty-car report was not.

A medic took Nadi’s contact information: a sister in the Cinder Quarter and a pre-collapse shuttle number from the station’s west apron. Joon wrote both on the same sheet. “The paper manifest has a Bell family,” he said. “The current digital response list does not show the row.”

“Keep the paper,” Nadi said. “Do not let them make our absence tidy.”

The wall behind them folded inward. Water rushed through the new gap, then stopped. The thermal camera displayed two weak heat signatures farther down the junction, separated from Nadi’s group by a distance no one had yet crossed.

“Two more,” Joon said.

Elias looked at them, then at the north branch that had disappeared from the terminal. The cleanest route to Nell’s voice had been there. He had released it with his own hand.

Tomas came alongside him. “You chose the living group.”

“I chose the people we could reach.”

“There is a difference.”

“Yes.”

It was not absolution. Tomas gave him a small nod and turned back to the support frame.

The refuge lights flickered as Inez’s generators took the load. Mara ordered the new patients onto mats. Joon placed the paper count under the battery lamp and wrote the Civic Spine’s response beneath it: **CAR 3 PASSENGER LOAD: 0**.

Twenty-three people were breathing behind the orange mesh. The terminal said none of them had been there.

Elias set his palm beside the number, feeling the heat of the lamp. His left hand still did not know if it was touching anything.

“Keep both,” he said.

Joon closed the recorder and opened a new page.

“Both,” he answered. “Until someone proves the contradiction is wrong.”
