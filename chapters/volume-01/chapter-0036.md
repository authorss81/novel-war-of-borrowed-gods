# Chapter 36 — The Cost of the Good Offer

The entry went at first light on Friday, which meant it began at twenty past five with a truck on Ash Street and finished at nine with a hole in the apron slab and everybody in the neighborhood awake and annoyed about it, which was, as Teodor said at six in the morning to nobody in particular, exactly what he had predicted and nobody had asked him.

He had said it on Thursday night. He had said: *not at first light, because first light is when things happen to people who are on their way to work.* And Inez had written it on the board, and Sable's office had agreed to eight in the morning inside forty minutes without ever once saying no, which nobody in the hall had predicted and which became, by the end of the day, the only evidence anybody had that a condition worked.

At eight they took up the apron slab to a stated depth of sixty centimeters with Office instruments, an Office chain, two named technicians from the regional pool who turned out to be a woman called Ruzicka and a man called Ohno and who were extremely good and who did not once ask the hall for anything, and an entry limit that Tomas had written and the technicians had accepted without a word, which was the first time since the first week that a limit written by anybody in this hall had been accepted by somebody from outside it.

The 2031 repair pour was where the 2038 contractor survey said it was, and it terminated six meters short of where the cameras point, and at thirty-eight centimeters down there was a second pour.

It was dated. Concrete carries a stamp and the stamp was legible and the year was two thousand and thirty-eight, and no drawing in any of the four buildings holds a pour at that depth, and there is a hairline crack running out of the corner of it about a meter and a half long that the technician photographed with a scale in it before anybody in the room had decided they wanted it.

“Nobody goes near that,” Tomas said. “That is my number and my number is *nothing touches it until we know what is under it*.”

Nothing touched it. The photographs went into the residents' file, the hall board and Joon's custody log, and into the node, and the node's copy has a keeper field that does not exist, and Emil Baros's name is in the remarks of every one of them, which nobody in the building found funny and everybody in the building found extremely funny.

---

Inez did the money at eleven and it took four minutes and it was the worst four minutes of the week.

“The entry instruments ran off the clinic’s generator margin,” she said. “Not the hall feeder. The clinic generator, which is the same generator that runs the frame on the east ward, and Lena and I signed that sharing arrangement in the second week and neither of us read the arithmetic past the first month. The instruments needed a two-hour margin and I gave it to them, and I gave it to them because a survey that stops halfway is a survey that comes back in a month and gets done badly, and that was the right call and I would make it again this morning.”

“Then say the part where it is not the right call,” Teodor said, from the wall.

“The part where it is not the right call is that the frame has been off since twenty to ten this morning and the margin is gone until the clinic's diesel is paid for on Monday, and the frame belongs to Comfort Osei, and I did not go and look at her before I gave the generator away, because I was on a slab with a chain and two people from the pool and a crack I have never seen, and I did the generator and I did not do her.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

“The records room also goes on a lamp for a day,” Inez said. “Which is worse than money, because money comes back and the records room does not, and the reason that wall exists is so two hundred pages can be read at one table by four people at once. Tomorrow the four of them read them on a lamp, one at a time, in the cold. That is the cost, I get to name it, and nobody who did not sign the power column is going to argue me out of it.”

Ru Sabar had been standing at the back for the whole of it with a card in her hand.

She put it on the table.

“Mine,” she said. “The machine shop. I have been running on nothing since Wednesday and I have a compressor and a man who wants to be paid Friday, and the second draw is free if you take my card for the week and I am offering it and nobody made me.”

Inez did not touch the card.

“No,” she said.

“I am offering.”

“I know you are offering and I am saying no, and I am going to say why, and you are not going to like it.” Inez's hands were flat on the table. “Six days ago you withdrew your laundrette circuit permanently and I recorded it and nobody thanked you and I made sure nobody thanked you, because a thank-you is a receipt. If I take that card back on Friday for the second draw, then the withdrawal was not a withdrawal. It was a pause, and everybody on that board is going to read the next withdrawal as a pause, and the whole of the plan is that a card is a card and a withdrawal is a withdrawal and neither of them is a conversation we can reopen on Thursday because we are short.”

Ru Sabar looked at the card on the table for a long moment.

“You have been telling me for six days that a card is a card,” she said. “You are telling me now that my card is a card that only works one way.” She did not pick it up. “I am not angry. I want that in the room. I am not angry, and I am going to go and close up, and I am not going to ask you again, and that is not a punishment, it is me taking the words back that I had already offered.”

She left the card on the table. Nobody picked it up for about a minute, and then Inez did, and read it out loud — Ru Sabar, machine shop, second draw, consent, with an expiry on it — and put it in the envelope with the other cards, and the room understood, and nobody said it, that the consent had been asked for twice.

---

Mara made the triage at half past ten in the morning, in the open, with the ledger on a cart and with Osei Kwarteng standing eight feet away because nobody had asked him to leave and nobody was going to.

The medical bank had gone to reserve at nine and the reserve had gone at ten. There was one cylinder of powered time left in that ward and it was in a frame, and there were two people who needed it, and Mara had refused since last Wednesday to write down a rule about that.

She did not write down a rule. She wrote down one decision, with a time, and her own name on it, and she read it out.

“Ansel Duarte. Nineteen, nonwalking since the second day, sacral lesion that has gone past what a dressing will hold, and it is going to take a week of a powered frame and if it does not take it, it will take something else. Comfort Osei, fifty-one, four days after a fusion, on a frame that has been off since twenty to ten this morning, with a back that has been going for a week because the ward it is on is dark every night from two until four and has been for a fortnight.” She put the pen down. “I am not writing a rule. I am writing a decision, with a time, and my name, and I am going to say it out loud with you in the room, Osei, and I am not going to look away while I say it.”

She said it: Ansel gets the frame.

“You are not wrong to be angry,” Mara said. “You are going to be angry for about a week and Lena is going to sit with you while you are, and I am not going to explain it, because there is nothing here that explains it. I want to say three things instead, and then I am going to go and do the work and I would like you to hold me to all three.”

“All right,” Osei said.

“One. I have not chosen between a nineteen-year-old and your wife. I have chosen between a wound that has a week in it and a back that has a year in it, and if the week does not work I will lose that boy and I will have chosen correctly, and I want you to hear me say that I know how that sounds at eleven in the morning with your wife lying on a dark frame.”

Osei said nothing.

“Two. The frame went dark because I gave a generator to a survey, and it was me who gave it, and I would give it again. That is not an excuse and it is the truth and I am not going to soften it for you.”

“Three. If I had been in that room at seven this morning with the choice in front of me instead of on a slab, I would still have given the generator away, because a seam over a school is worse than a back. And that is the sentence you are going to hate most, and I am saying it now while it is cheap, because it will not be cheap at four o'clock this afternoon when she is in pain and I am not the one in pain.”

Osei sat down on the end of a bench, and Lena Mir sat down beside him, and neither of them said anything for a long time, and then Osei said, “Give it to the boy,” and Mara said, “Thank you,” and neither of those two sentences was the whole conversation and everybody in the room knew it.

---

Tomas got the third one and he got it last, and he got it in the post, which is how it happened.

It was nine lines, and Joon read it out, and then read the covering note.

It was a letter from the Havenport Continuity Office, addressed to him, and it thanked him for his disclosure and confirmed that the two thousand and thirty-four write-off of the buried paired node, to which his signature is second on the page, had been cross-indexed to the current chain that places him under the apron at sixty centimeters depth on the date and hour of the entry.

The note also asked him, in one line, to confirm whether he intended to be available to give evidence at any future inquiry into the site.

“That is a form asking you to book yourself in for your own hearing,” Elias said.

“It is a form asking me that,” Tomas said. “And I cannot answer it today, and the reason I cannot answer it today is that I do not know, and the reason I do not know is that until about an hour ago I did not have any reason to imagine there would be an inquiry, and now there is a stamp in a slab in 2038 that nobody has a drawing for, and a stamp is a question, and a question under my feet is an inquiry, and I am the structural author of the chain that stood over it.”

He put the letter down on the table and looked at it the way he had looked at the eleven-meter mark on Tuesday morning.

“Nineteen years,” he said. “Nineteen years I have been a man in a room that makes things not happen, and the way I have done it is by being careful and by not writing anything down, and the one time I wrote something down, in 2035, with a pen, about a node I had not examined, in a hurry, because somebody wanted a form closed — that is now the only line in any file in this city that anybody can find me on. And I signed it because I was asked to, and I would sign it again, and I have not told anybody that until this second.”

“You told us about the 2034 collapse in the second week,” Elias said.

“I told you the collapse. I did not tell you that I signed off the node that the collapse is about. Those are different sentences and I gave you the first one for eight days.”

“Whose fault,” Joon said, from the table, without looking up, “is that somebody could put those two sentences in different paragraphs?”

Nobody answered, and Joon wrote it down, because it was a question and it belonged in the room.

Then he said the rest of it, which was the part he had come into the room for.

“We made him do this. That is the thing to say. Nobody in this room made Tomas Vale disclose his own history to a building he does not control, and we did not do it by lying, and we did not do it by threatening him, and we did it by promising a Director that the structural author of a chain would be disclosed before the chain went down, and he kept the promise because that is the sort of man he is, and so his name is now in a file where it was not, and it is there next to his own handwriting, and in five years the first thing a person in that building can say about the ground under this street is *Vale*.” He looked up. “And we do not know whether that was right, and we are not going to find out this week, and I am not going to let anybody in this hall be comforted about it by me.”

---

The last thing of Friday happened at half past six in the evening, at the gate, where the scarecrow with the form on it was still standing because Osei had voted for it to stay up and nobody had ever voted it down.

Teodor was not on the board. Teodor was not a cardholder. Teodor was a man whose mother was on the far side of a Meridian in an apartment with a bad hip and a landlord, and who had been on a route and had come back on his hands and knees, and who had spent the day outside because inside there were too many people being careful at him.

Elias found him at the fence with a stop page in his hand, because a stop page is a printed thing and Teodor had been reading one.

“I am going to ask for a stop,” Teodor said, “and Inez is going to say no, because I have not got a card and I do not live here and I have known you five minutes of conversation. So I am not asking for a card. I am asking for a stop, and a stop is not a card, and you do not get to make me take a card to have one.”

“Name it,” Inez said, from the top of the step.

“Friday. First light. And I do not want to have to be the one who says it, because I have spent all day being the one who says things, and I am tired.” Teodor put the page down on the rail. “But somebody has to, and it should be somebody who does not have to be here. That is the whole of my argument and it is not a good one and I do not care.”

“Granted,” Inez said. “Sixth name. Same power as the other five, including the hall, including you, and you can stop a survey, an entry, a chain or a draw, and nobody can overrule you, and the only thing I am asking is that you have to be in the building to use it, because I am not paying for a telephone.”

Petar Lisk read the whole thing out at seven and wrote it up, and the new line on the board, above everything else, in the nine-year-old's capitals, was not a rule and was not a plan and was the sentence of the week and stayed on the wall after that, through the fourth column and the four sheets and everything else that has been put up since.
