# Chapter 40 — The Route That Is Not Home

On Monday morning the notice was still on the terminal, patient, four fields, and nobody in the hall had touched it since eleven o'clock the night before, and by nine in the morning there were two arguments against using it and one for it, and the one for it was made by the person in the room with the least right to make it, and everybody knew that, and she made it anyway.

Mara stood up with the ledger closed.

“I am going to say the bad thing first and I am going to say it about a person I am going to refuse to move. There is a second thermal signature at roughly nine meters that has been unconfirmed since the second week and it may be a person and it may be a hot pipe, and the last thermal survey is eight days old, and a person who is alive at nine meters today has had eight days of this.” She put her hand on the table. “There is a road. It is offered by somebody who has walked it. If we do not use it, then at some point I am going to be the physician who watched a road go past a window because of a rule, and I would like that on the record in my own words before anybody else puts it in theirs.”

“That is the case for,” Elias said.

“That is the case for and I will not soften it and I will not withdraw it.” She sat down and then stood up again. “Now the other half, which is harder. There is a version of my sister, or a version of somebody who is a physician in a branch, and she has offered a thing, and if I use *her* to argue that a present patient should go through a door, then I have used a future person's capacity to authorize a present decision, and she is not here, and she cannot be asked, and I have spent three weeks saying that no present person may be required to authorize a future self's capacity. It is not a rule for them. It is a rule for us. It goes both ways and the both ways is the only part of it that means anything.”

Nadi Bell had the school coat on her knees and she had not said anything for an hour.

“Add the other one,” she said. “The road is made of people holding a door, and the price of it is that the door stops being held for the people it is being held for, and every one of those people is off every list in this city, which means the cost of using it is invisible, which means it is free, which is what makes it look like a gift.” She looked at the terminal. “My sister is one of the invisible. I have wanted a road since the second day. That is not an argument, it is a reason, and I am putting the reason on the table so that nobody can use it later without admitting they used it.”

The room was quiet, and then Elias Rook stood up and said the thing that was going on the board, and he said it before anybody else had finished arguing.

“That is a rescue.”

He said it about himself, out loud, in a hall with forty people in it, and then he put his hand flat on the terminal and kept talking.

“Her sentence, not mine. I am not going down that road to find out what is at the end of it, and I am not putting a name on a document that describes it, and I am not calling it a survey, and I am not accepting it as a loan, and I am not asking her for anything else, and I want all five of those written down separately because four of them are the same thing and one of them is different.”

Petar Lisk wrote, in his own hand: **HE USED ANSEL'S SENTENCE ON HIMSELF BEFORE ANYBODY SAID IT.**

Ansel Duarte did not look up from the book.

“Second time,” he said. “I am keeping count of a thing that is mine, and nobody has to do anything about it.”

Joon wrote the refusal at ten past ten, and it took four minutes, and then it took twenty because the room would not let him finish it.

**ROUTE DECLINED. 23:14 SUNDAY, OFFER RECEIVED ON A LICENSED BROKER'S BRANCH CIRCUIT, FOUR FIELDS, ONE GIFT NAMED, ONE BOUNDARY, ONE RETURN.**

**NO LOAN OFFERED BY THIS HALL. NO CAPACITY TAKEN. NO BOUNDARY STRETCHED, AMENDED, OR ASKED ABOUT. NOTHING SIGNED BY ANYBODY WHO WAS NOT ASKED.**

**THE GIFT WAS NOT ACCEPTED AS A LOAN AND THE ROUTE IS NOT ACCEPTED AS A ROUTE.**

And then, because the room had made him say it out loud, the line that Joon Park had to read twice before he wrote it down.

**THE RETURN IN THE OFFER WAS *WHEN YOU HAVE WRITTEN DOWN THAT YOU DID NOT USE IT.* THAT RETURN IS NOT INVOKED. IT IS PERFORMED. THOSE ARE NOT THE SAME ACT AND I WANT THE DIFFERENCE ON THE PAGE, BECAUSE IN TEN YEARS SOMEBODY WILL READ *THE RETURN WAS SATISFIED* AND CONCLUDE THAT A DEAL WAS DONE.**

“Read it to Niko Senn's line,” Elias said, and Joon understood him, because Niko Senn had got the wording he dictated on two pages in the second week and it was the only precedent in the building for a person insisting on the exact form of a thing about himself.

Niko Senn was not there. He was on a bus to the east bank with a ticket, on an ordinary road, and he came back at nine in the evening and read it in the dry end standing up, and he said one sentence, which was, “Say *declined* and not *not used*,” and Joon changed it, because Niko Senn had earned that in the second week and the point of a precedent is that it holds when it is inconvenient.

And the envelope was still in the notebook, and on Monday morning a man in this city would have carried a message to his sister for four hundred dollars, and nobody in that hall said so out loud after nine o'clock, because it had been said out loud once, at eleven the night before, in front of witnesses, and it did not need saying again to be true.

The loading lip at four o'clock on Monday was the last appointment on a board that had been written by hand since the first week, and Elias walked the twenty-two meters in ordinary wind with no instrument and no chain and nobody behind him, because there was nothing to measure.

The handset went eleven meters into a corridor and a man picked it up.

“It is still eleven,” Elias said. “Nothing has changed and nobody came up the ramp to tell you anything, because nobody came up the ramp.”

“You said you would come Mondays and Thursdays.”

“I did. I did not come Thursday. I did not tell you I was not coming, because that is what we agreed, and because you would have known anyway at four o'clock and you did not have to be told, and I have thought about that sentence every day since and it is the correct sentence and I still do not enjoy it.”

“That is the correct sentence,” the man said. “It is also the reason I am still on this wire, so do not get used to it.”

Elias stood in the cold with his left hand in his pocket and his right one on the concrete.

“There is a road,” he said. “It is on the other side of your wall. It came from my sister and it would have let somebody through and it is not going to be used, and I am telling you because you are the only person I owe that sentence to, and because you told me on the first day that this wall is a thing that gets closed by people who never had to walk it, and I would like you to know that one person has now declined to walk it.”

The line hissed for a long time.

“That is a waste,” the man said. “Do not argue with me about that, I have had a long week and I am not going to be talked out of a waste. But you came down and said it, and nobody has ever said one to me before, so I am going to say the thing I have been not saying.”

“All right.”

“I have never been past the wall on this side,” the man said. “I have been on the other side of the wall, which is the same wall, and I have been next to the thing you are talking about, and I have never once asked anybody to come and look at it, and I am not going to now.” A pause, and then, almost as an afterthought, in a voice that was not the voice of a man discussing ground: “It is warm on that side. In February. Under the water. If anybody ever wants to know what it is like on the other side of something, that is what it is like on the other side of something.”

He put the handset down mid-sentence, the way a man does when he has said one thing more than he meant to, and the line went to a flat carrier, and Elias stood at eleven meters in the cold with a piece of information he could not use, and did not move, and did not go and look for the man, and went home.

The board at seven on Monday had six new lines and Petar Lisk read all of them, and the last two were the ones that the next ten days are made of, written in Inez's chalk because the hall had voted at seven and had voted right.

**THERMAL SURVEY, WEDNESDAY MORNING, FOR THE SECOND READING AT NINE METERS. A SURVEY, WITH A LIMIT, WRITTEN BEFORE ANYBODY GOES, WITH A DATE ON IT AND SOMEBODY ELSE'S NAME AT THE TOP.**

**AND THEN WE GO TO THE RELAY. NOT BECAUSE A WOMAN TOLD US WHERE IT WAS AND NOT BECAUSE OF WHAT IS ON THE OTHER SIDE. BECAUSE THERE ARE PEOPLE DEEPER IN AND A RELAY UNDER WATER THAT WE HAVE NEVER LOOKED AT, AND THOSE ARE TWO SEPARATE REASONS AND WE ARE ONLY ALLOWED TO USE THE SECOND ONE UNTIL THE FIRST ONE IS DEAD.**

And underneath them, in Petar's capitals, because he had been told to write what he thought and had been honest for once:

**SHE SAID DO NOT MAKE IT A DOCUMENT. WE ARE GOING TO HAVE A DOCUMENT ANYWAY BECAUSE WE ARE NOT BRAVE ENOUGH NOT TO, AND WHEN WE DO, WE OWE HER A LINE THAT SAYS WE KNOW IT.**

The three live questions, which nobody in that hall could answer and which Petar wrote out last, in the order Inez told him to:

**WHO HELD `MNT-4C-17`, AND WHO SIGNED THE SECOND LINE OF A SHEET THAT ASKS FOR A PERSON — AND WE STILL CANNOT REVISE ANYTHING THAT DEPENDS ON IT.**

**IS THE OLD RELAY A WAY TO REACH HER, OR IS IT THE THING THAT CLOSES HER SIDE WHEN SOMEBODY USES IT.**

**AND WHAT DOES THE CONTINUITY OFFICE ACTUALLY BELIEVE A SINGLE APPROVED OUTCOME PREVENTS. THERE IS A CONTRACTOR'S NUMBER ON A SHEET IN THAT BUILDING, TWO HUNDRED AND TEN METERS, AND A WOMAN WHO HAS NEVER LIED TO US SAYS IT IS REAL, AND SHE IS STILL WAITING TO BE BELIEVED ABOUT SOMETHING.**
