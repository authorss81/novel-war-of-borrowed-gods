# Chapter 33 — The License

She came at nine at night with a courier's envelope and a man in a gray coat who turned out to be the licensing clerk, and she sent the man to sit in the front room with Petar Lisk and a glass of water and a job he was very good at, which was being somewhere he was not needed.

She put four sheets on the table. The first was her answers to Hollis Vane's four findings, and she said, before anybody sat down, that she was answering them as the Director and not as the chair of the board that had ordered them, because a Director who answers in the chair is answering herself.

**Finding 1, the classification is not supported by the Office's own evidence.** “Accepted, and I will put my name to the mechanism and not only to the conclusion. The cause box arrived pre-ticked from the standing site pack at zero five two four, before the first alarm, and nothing in the field overwrote it. From today a provisional classification cannot carry a cause no observer entered. That is a change to a form, it will take nine weeks to get through every desk in this office, and I am telling you the nine weeks so that nobody in here thinks it is fixed.”

**Finding 2, the file cannot be revised in any respect depending on credential attribution.** “I have had it done. I did not have it done this morning, because it was not on my desk until Saturday, and I want that on the record as a fact about how this office works and not as an excuse. It took a records clerk two days.” She turned the second sheet around. “Sector four C, class three, early sequence, issued against the two thousand and thirty-five renumbering. Nine credentials. Nine holder fields not carried forward. Nine.”

Joon read the numbers twice.

“Nine is not a name.”

“Nine is not a name,” Sable said. “It is nine, and the mapping that would put nine names next to nine credentials was consolidated in two thousand and thirty-nine into a regional archive in Cordell, and Cordell does not answer to me, and I have written to them and the letter is dated Tuesday and it will be read on Wednesday by somebody with a queue. You have moved the answer one building over. I am not going to stand in a union hall and pretend that is a result.”

“Nobody said it was,” Elias said. “Say the next part.”

“The next part is that I would have done it anyway, and that I would have preferred to have been asked, and that in six years nobody has asked me for anything with a date on it.” She said it flatly, without self-pity, the way she said everything. “That is the whole of what this office has learned from your four findings and it is not a large amount.”

**Finding 3, that a named subject may withdraw the named field of a record without withdrawing the event.** “Adopted today, in writing, as office practice, and I would like the room to hear what it costs me before it costs you anything. Every form this office issues for five years has a withdrawal line and an instruction to the subject, and a clerk handed a withdrawal has to enter it as a finding rather than as an absence, and about a third of my clerks will get that wrong for a year because the system rewards them for the other thing.” She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed. “It is the best thing in your four pages and I am not going to be thanked for it in a room where I am about to do something else.”

Nobody thanked her for it in a room where she was about to do something else.

**Finding 4, that the classification must not become the standing site default by silence.**

“No,” Sable Arden said. “By that name, again, and I will give you the same reason and then I will give you the other reason, which is worse. The first is procedural: a request is not a finding, and the day an institution takes instructions about its own findings from a union hall it has stopped being an institution and started being a faction, and I have watched two of those and one of them was mine. The other reason is that I am not going to let a category expire by nobody opposing it, because when a thing becomes the standing default it becomes the answer to the next question without anybody having to say it, and the next question is always about somebody who is not in this room.”

She squared the fourth sheet.

“Here is what I will do instead, and you should both be suspicious of it. A provisional classification may not stand ninety days without either a seal or a finding. In eighty-nine days this site has to be sealed or found about, and the only instrument I have for finding about is a joint inspection, and that needs a structural person with a ticket and a rope pair, and two of your three tickets went to a gas main on the thirteenth.” She looked up. “You have spent four days refusing me that inspection. What you have actually done is set a timer on the thing you are afraid of. I think it is a mistake and I think you made it for a good reason, and I have watched competent people make that exact mistake.”

Nobody in the room had an answer to that, and the fact that Elias found it *correct* rather than merely unavoidable was the part he was going to be thinking about at four in the morning.

---

The third sheet was the license, and she put it on the table face up and did not soften it by putting anything on top of it.

**R. ADARE — BRANCH ROUTING, LICENSED. SITE: KESTREL PROVISIONAL CLASSIFICATION 4C. EFFECTIVE TUESDAY, THIS DATE, ON THE PRODUCTION OF A ROUTING LOG AND A CONTINUITY CHAIN THAT EXISTED BEFORE THE DATE OF APPLICATION, WHICH HE HAS HAD SINCE FRIDAY EVENING AND DID NOT DECLARE.**

“You are going to ask me about that,” Sable said, “so I will answer it. He lodged on Friday night. He had the log and the chain by Saturday morning. I could have refused the application for a defect in the declaration and I did not, and the reason is that the log is the best record of that ground anybody will ever have, and it is in his hands, and it is now in a file with my name on the top of it. If that is corruption I would like to be told which part, and I would like to be told it before the fortieth page, because I have read the first thirty-nine and I have approved all of them.”

She turned the fourth sheet over.

“I cannot stop him. I want that understood first, because everything after it depends on it. I cannot stop a man from routing people through bad ground. Not with a refusal, because a refusal is a thing a private person does and he is not a private person. Not with a closure, because a closure is a thing the site authority does and the site authority is me, and if I close the classification to a man with a legal right to work in it, I have to close it to the electricians and the gas crew and the four hundred people who use that apron every day, and the classification does not have a shape that lets me do both. So I have done the only instrument available, which is the one Adare told you about on Friday, in a laundrette's worth of better manners than he needed.”

She read the conditions out loud, because she had written them and wanted the room to have her wording and not a summary.

Consent recorded. Witness recorded. Return recorded. A routing log with a time and a place and a broker of record for every event. A continuity chain that must have existed before the application. **Notification to the local residents' body within seventy-two hours of any routing event involving a resident.** An Office observer entitled to attend any routing event inside a provisional classification, unannounced, with a hand on the log.

“Four conditions and a reporting duty,” she said. “Every one of them is better than what he was doing on Friday night, and every one of them is a piece of paper, and I know exactly what paper is worth, because I am the institution that made paper worth what it is worth.”

“And the seventy-two hours,” Inez said.

“The seventy-two hours is the only part I am ashamed of and I am going to tell you why I kept it anyway, and then you can decide what to do with it.” Sable did not look away from her. “A routing event is somebody crossing a seam. It is short. It is often over before a residents' body could be convened, and a notification clause that can be broken by a clock is a notification clause that says *tell them afterwards, if you can*, which is the entire history of this office's relationship with neighborhoods. And the time-critical condition is the honest one: if a person is being routed out of ground that is moving, an hour spent in a hall deciding whether to object is an hour the person does not have.”

“Or it is a hall full of people who were not told one of their neighbors went through a wall last night,” Inez said. “You have described a duty and you have described an excuse, and they are the same sentence, and the sentence runs toward whoever is in power.”

“Yes,” Sable Arden said. “That is what authority is. I have spent nineteen years learning to say yes to that sentence early so that I can be the one saying it.”

---

The room went at it for an hour and a half, and the hall did not behave like a room that had been told a no.

“Say the thing you actually want to ask for,” Nadi Bell said. “Because you have not asked for anything. In seventeen days you have asked for a notebook, a re-entry, a classification, a signature and a seal, and tonight you have brought me a license I do not want in a building I live in, and nobody hands those out for free at nine o'clock on a Tuesday. So ask.”

Sable put both hands flat on the table, and Elias watched her decide to be honest and thought it might be the first time.

“The classification carries a population of record,” she said. “I have to report a population. Every designated site has one, and it is a number, and it is a number because a number is the only thing that can be reconciled between four agencies in an afternoon. Yours is currently twenty-two or twenty-three, depending on which of my clerks you ask, and I would like the number you use to be the number in my file, so that when the number is wrong, it is wrong in one place instead of four.”

“Which number is yours,” Nadi said.

“Twenty-three. It has been twenty-three since the second day and it has not moved because no person has ever left your building in fifteen days, which I am aware is a sentence with two readings.”

“Twenty-two the first time somebody walks out of that door,” Nadi said. “Twenty-two, and it will keep moving, and it is going to move down, because people who get better go and live somewhere, and if your number is the number in your file then every person who walks out of that hall takes a piece of the Kestrel event out of your report, and in ten years this site will read as an event that killed nobody and housed nobody, because the count went down every time somebody got well.”

Nobody in the hall said anything for about a minute.

“That is a better answer than mine,” Sable said.

“It is not an answer, it is a refusal, and I would like it recorded that I made it.” Nadi Bell put her hands in her lap. “And I would like the other thing recorded too, because I have been wanting to say it since Friday and Adare was standing in this room with a laminated card. A license is a promise that a bad thing will at least be written down. That is the only reason in the world to want one, and I want one, and I have wanted one since Friday night, and it is the most upsetting thing I have said in this hall, and I would like somebody to make me regret saying it and nobody is going to, so write it down instead.”

Joon wrote it down, in full, in his own hand, and read it back to her before he closed the notebook, because that is what he does.

---

The last thing was the thing the room did not have a name for, and Sable put it on the table last on purpose, the way she put everything.

“I will put an observer in the Quarter,” she said. “Not in the hall. In the Quarter — at the gate, on the street, on any carded draw, on any event involving a resident who holds a card in that building. Unannounced, quarterly at minimum, with a hand on the log. And I will name the observer, and the name goes on your board, and you may publish it.”

Inez put both hands on the table too, which was the first time anybody had copied her.

“And in exchange,” Inez said.

“In exchange the number you use is the number in my file.”

It sat there.

It was, on the face of it, an honest offer from a person who had an institutional reason and had stated it and had not dressed it up. An unannounced Office observer on a residential street is not nothing. It is the first time anybody in this building had been offered a standing presence by the institution that holds the only copy of the ground underneath it, and the reason it was on the table was that the institution wanted a number.

And the number was the exact instrument that had taken a woman off a list on the second day and left her as a subtraction, and Nadi Bell had just spent fourteen days explaining precisely that to a room that listened.

“Take it back upstairs,” Elias said.

“I cannot,” Sable said. “It is an offer and it stays open for seventy-two hours, which is a number I chose and I chose it because it is long enough to be a real offer and short enough to be pressure. I am telling you that it is pressure. I am telling you that I know it is pressure, and I am telling you that I am making it anyway, because the alternative is a population of record that this office fills in on its own, and I do not trust myself with a blank field, and neither should you.”

The courier got his coat on. At the door she stopped and turned round, and she said the last thing of the night to Elias and not to the room, in the low voice of somebody who has been in this job for two decades and has learned which sentences are heard.

“Elias. I have read your board. Monday and Thursday, four to six, at the loading lip, for a man who cannot sign anything.” She did not ask who had authorized it. “If there is ever going to be an entry under the ground, this office is the only body that can send two people who do this for a living, and I have been holding them, and I will keep holding them, until somebody tells me in writing that they do not want them. I am not going to offer that in a hall at nine o'clock. I am going to offer it in a letter, on its own, on a day when nobody has just been threatened by a piece of paper, so that you can read it without an audience.”

“Write it on a Tuesday,” Elias said.

She almost smiled, which was not the same thing, and went out into the wind.

Petar Lisk read the board at seven with a gray coat's worth of questions still in it, and the last line was Nadi Bell's, and Inez wrote it up in chalk because Petar had to go home and his mother was collecting him and it was not that kind of night.

**WE ARE GOING TO HAVE TO DECIDE WHETHER WE WANT THE ONLY THING THAT KEEPS A RECORD OF WHAT HE DOES. I WANT IT. THAT IS THE WHOLE OF MY ARGUMENT AND IT IS NOT ENOUGH.**
