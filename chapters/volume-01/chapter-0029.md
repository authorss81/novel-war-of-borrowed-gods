# Chapter 29 — What the Records Can Bear

The three paper copies of Saturday's corridor survey were on the hall table at ten on Saturday morning, and two of them agreed with each other and the third one did not, and the third one was the one in a building the team could not walk into.

Joon had the node's query printed, because he had learned on Friday to print everything.

“Field: survey record, west apron baseline, this week,” he said. “Field: route identifier. Field: length. Field: date. Field: witnesses. Field: keeper.”

He put the last one down on the table.

“The node returns the first five. The keeper field comes back empty, and it comes back empty the way a field that does not exist comes back empty, which is to say it comes back with a dash where a form would print a line if a form had one.”

“There is no keeper field,” Elias said.

“There is no keeper field,” Joon agreed, “and there is no witness field either, because the Office's baseline form has a single line for *person present* and it is filled with the Office's own person, who is Ilse Brauer, and not with the resident who walked it or the boy who held the far end of the tape. And Emil Baros's name is not in the record. It is in the record's *remarks*, in free text, in a line a machine does not read, and it was there on Saturday and it is there now, and I can pull it and read it to you.”

He read it. It was there, in Brauer's hand, with her initials twice.

“So the name is in the copy and not in the index,” Mara said.

“The name is in the copy and not in the index,” Joon said, “and those are different buildings, and I have now got three of them. A holder field that was never carried forward in 2035. A subject field on an aisle log that somebody chose to leave blank. And now a form that has no place to put a resident's name, so a man who walked thirty-one meters of concrete with a clipboard on Saturday and signed for it is, to any system that asks by field, nobody at all.”

Nobody said anything for a while.

“Write it as a row,” Elias said. “Three things, one row. Do not write it as a theory. Write the three cases and then write the sentence that they have in common and let the sentence be as small as the evidence allows.”

Joon wrote it, and read it back:

**A FIELD THAT DOES NOT EXIST. A FIELD THAT WAS LEFT BLANK. A FIELD THAT WAS NEVER DESIGNED FOR THE THING IN QUESTION. IN ALL THREE, THE NAME IS NOT ERASED. THE NAME IS NOT PRESENT.**

“The third one is different,” Tomas said.

“The third one is the one nobody did to anybody,” Joon said, “and it is the one that will still be there in twenty years, and it is the one I am going to think about at two in the morning for the rest of my life.”

---

The second disagreement was worse, and it was an accident, and nobody could prove it was an accident, and everybody in the room believed it.

“The aisle log page,” Joon said. “Brauer scanned it on Monday. Single pass, bound ledger, which is single-sided, so the scan has three of the four columns and the fourth is off the right edge of the frame.”

“So the Office's copy of the log has no subject column.”

“The Office's copy of the log has no subject column,” Joon said. “Which means that in a building I cannot get into, there is a record of a box being opened on the day of the collapse with a reason and a time and no place where a person could have been written. And there is no way for anybody in that building to see that, because a scan looks like a page, and nobody is going to open a bound ledger at aisle nine on a Saturday to check a column that is not in the picture.”

“Brauer will redo it.”

“Brauer will redo it on Tuesday,” Joon said, “and Tuesday is the day the shuttle confirmation is due, and Tuesday is the eleventh day, and by the time anybody at the Office has looked at that ledger again somebody will have read a document in which the blank was structural.” He looked up. “A field that does not exist is honest. A field that can be blank is dangerous. And a field that is not in a photograph is invisible, and we are the only people on this street who know it is missing, and in about a year I will be a man with a story instead of a document, and the story will not survive contact with a building I cannot get into.”

He wrote it down and initialled it, which he had never done for a sentence of his own before.

---

The signal came at two in the morning, and Joon was awake for it, because Joon was always awake for it, and he came and knocked on the door of the room four people were sleeping in and said the four words that meant somebody had to be awake.

“Do not put the light on,” he said. “I want everybody in this room to hear this exactly once with their eyes shut, and then I am going to type it and read it back tomorrow morning and you can all doubt it together.”

It was four words on the same carrier, the same compression, the same present voice, and the compression was worse than the last one, which meant either a worse line or a longer distance, and neither of those were good.

“Do not use the west run.”

Nobody spoke for a while.

“What time,” Elias said.

“Two oh four. My time and the relay's time, and the relay and I agree inside a second, which is the only agreement in this hall that has held for fourteen days.”

“Read it back.”

“Nell Rook signal fragment, Saturday, 02:04, four words, present voice, branch-crossed, carrier is the 4C local route line, which is the same physical path as the 05:17 maintenance entry, and which runs through the twenty meters of the lower service run that were there on Monday and were not there on Tuesday morning.” He said the last part without any editorial at all. “I am not going to interpret that and I would like nobody in this room to interpret it tonight. It goes on the page as a carrier fact and a message, and whatever anybody feels about the two things sitting next to each other in one sentence is a thing they can write down under their own name in the morning.”

Elias sat on the edge of a cot in the dark for a long time and did not turn the light on either.

“Write this too,” he said. “I am not going to sleep, and I want it in the record that I did not sleep and that what I did instead was sit here and understand that a person has just told us not to use a road that is not there, and that she sent it after it stopped being there, which means either she is late, or it is a different road, or the thing she is protecting is not the concrete.”

“That is three readings and none of them is a finding,” Joon said.

“Write that down too.”

---

He did not sleep, and at ten in the morning he came into the dry end of the hall with the notebook and put it on the table next to the new records wall, and the notebook had a bulge in the front where the envelope was.

“I want to file it,” he said.

Mara put down the ledger. Joon put down his pencil.

“Say all of that,” Joon said.

“I want to file the envelope. Not send it. File it, with a reference number and a date, so that it is in the record, so that it exists in more than my coat, so that if a person who wants to answer me finds it there is something to answer. And I have wanted to do that since Tuesday and I have not done it because every time I decided it was a good idea I could hear somebody explaining to me why it was not.”

“You can,” Joon said. “Go on. You are in a room with witnesses and it is Saturday and Inez reads the board at seven, so whatever you say now gets said in this building by seven.”

Elias put his right hand flat on the notebook.

“Reason one,” he said. “And this is the one I am least proud of. I am afraid of a survey. The board gave us eleven days and a joint inspection and there is a genuine possibility that somebody in this hall goes into the run in ten days' time and does not come out, and if that happens, then a woman with a voice and a carrier and no address has had no word from her brother in ten days. I know that is not her problem. I know it is mine. But it is why I have had the notebook open three times this week.”

Nobody said anything.

“Reason two,” he said. “If I file it, it becomes a record. And a record can be answered. And I have spent fourteen days watching what a record does to the people inside it, and the answer to this record will not come from my sister. It will come from a building, and a building can answer a record with a form, and the form will be *no response available*, and then I will have a document that says I asked, and I will have nothing.”

Joon said, “No.”

He said it flatly, in front of eleven people, and he did not soften it afterwards, which is what Elias had been half hoping for and entirely knew was not going to happen.

“No,” Joon said. “You are not filing an envelope to a person who has no address on paper in a system that has already lost one woman. The moment it is filed it becomes a record, and the record has an owner, and the owner is not you, and the owner is not her. And then in a year somebody in a warm building with a sealed node will produce a file that contains a request for contact from Elias Rook, and they will be required to answer it, and they will answer it with the truth, which is that the subject is not locatable. And you will be a man who has proof that he asked.”

He put the pencil down.

“That is a real sentence and I wrote the same one about Yolanda Bell’s row in the first week, and I do not think you noticed, and I have decided that I do not care.”

---

Mara was slower and colder and she took longer.

“I am not going to put a present voice into a document as though it were a future one,” she said. “Do you understand the difference I am drawing? Every piece of paper in this building that has a *future* in it has a boundary and a return and a lender. They are people, and they can be refused, and we have written the refusals down, and a refusal is a complete action. What you want to file has none of that. It is a message from a woman in the present, in a place we cannot describe, about a road she has just told us not to use. The moment it is filed, it becomes a thing that is being *looked for*. And a search is a promise to somebody behind a wall. It is a promise made in a building, by people who are not there, to a person who has not asked.”

“I have not asked anybody to look for her.”

“You have been sitting in a hall for fourteen days describing a carrier to people who are not you,” Mara said, “and last Sunday a man told you the far run is the only road he has, and on Saturday at two in the morning the road he has was named in a voice from the other side of it. File it. Then read your own notebook back to yourself and tell me which of those three facts is about you.”

---

Elias did not file it.

He took the envelope out of the notebook in front of them, and he did not open it, and he said the reason out loud with his right hand flat on the table and his left one lying useless beside it, which was a habit now and not a choice.

“I am not sending it and I am not filing it, and the honest reason is the worst one, which is this. The only line in the world that could carry a reply is the one she just told me in her own voice not to use. And this envelope is the only version of me that can still get to her without a crew, without a route, and without breaking a boundary that she stated. If I file it, then the way it reaches her becomes somebody else's decision, and somebody else's decision will involve a search, and I have spent fourteen days learning that a search is a thing that happens to people.”

He put it back in the notebook and closed the cover with his right hand.

“Also I am not brave enough, and if you want that in a document you may have it, because I have found out this week that I would rather be told the true reason than be praised for a noble one.”

Inez read the board at seven that evening, and the second-to-last line of it was his.

**ENVELOPE UNSENT. NOT FILED. THE REASON WAS GIVEN AND IT WAS NOT THE BEST OF THE REASONS AND IT WAS RECORDED ANYWAY.**

The last line was Joon's and it was a question nobody in the room could answer and nobody was willing to write an answer to until they had more.

Elias stood at the long table after the hall had gone and put the notebook in his coat, and his right hand closed the cover, and his left one lay open on the table beside the page where he had written three lines in a tunnel: **WHO USED THE CREDENTIAL BEFORE THE COLLAPSE / AND WHO WAS THE SECOND NAME ON THE 05:02 DISPATCH SHEET / AND WHO DECIDED AT 05:24 THAT THIS WAS AN EQUIPMENT FAILURE — AND WAS IT A PERSON, OR A TEMPLATE THAT A PERSON OPENED EARLY?**

The second answer had turned out to be a credential, and the envelope was still in the same notebook, and Joon's question was still on the wall upstairs, and Elias Rook, who had spent two weeks learning that a promise made in good faith is not a license to choose another person's outcome, went up the stairs without turning the light on.
