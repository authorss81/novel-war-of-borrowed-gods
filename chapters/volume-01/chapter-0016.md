# Chapter 16 — The Bell Row

Lena Mir lent them the clinic's day line between nine and ten, and she lent it with a condition that she was in the room for the whole of it.

“I am not lending you the clinic,” she said, keying the wall phone through to the side room and pulling the door half shut. “I am lending you the wall. I have a man in the back with a dislocated shoulder who has been waiting since seven, and if I stand up, this call is over and I do not apologise to anybody for it.”

They took the handset into the corridor. Nadi Bell went first, because a call made by somebody else would be a call about somebody else.

Elias stood with his back against the tile and his right hand in his pocket and did not listen to the first minute, which was the part where a woman who had not heard from her sister in two days said her sister’s name twice before she said anything else.

Then he listened, because after the first minute there was nothing left to protect anyone from.

“Tell me about the wall,” Nadi said. “Tell me what was on it.”

“Tell me about shuttle six,” Yolanda said.

“That is what I am asking.”

“You are asking me to describe a wall.”

“I am asking you to look at it and tell me what is on it, because in about ten minutes somebody is going to tell you there is nothing on it, and I would like you to have looked at it first.”

The line hissed. Somewhere below, a truck changed gear.

“Six ran the apron,” Yolanda said. “Five forty-two, five fifty, six o’clock, the relief runs. I signed the sheet. I signed every one of them, it is the whole job, you stand at the desk and you sign and you go home. I signed the four fifty-eight one because the four fifty-two ran short and they sent a car instead. And I remember that because the car driver would not put his coat on and I wrote *coat* on the sheet in the box so they would stop sending him.”

Elias closed his eyes for a second. Beside him, Joon's pencil had stopped moving.

“What number was on that sheet?”

“Why?”

“Because I think my friend has a piece of it.”

A pause. Then: “Nine-one-seven-four. It is the sheet series. The last four. I have signed about nine thousand of those and I would know the sound of one by now.”

“I will not tell you the number out loud,” Nadi said. “I will tell you later, in person, and I will tell you who I got it from and I will not tell you their name.”

“That is fair,” said Yolanda. “I have been on the receiving end of a fair.”

“It is not from the official list,” Nadi said, and then stopped, because her voice had done something she had not authorised.

“You are not on the official list,” Yolanda said.

“No.”

“The list came out yesterday afternoon. It says four recovered from Marrow Street building. There were five of us in that building. My mother, my aunt, two men off the night shift, and you. I have read that list four times and I did the arithmetic on a receipt because I did not trust the arithmetic in my head.” Yolanda’s voice did not rise. It got quieter, which was worse. “So the list has put four people in a building where there were five, and it has kept the fifth somewhere, and my sister is the fifth, and now I have to decide which of two bad sentences is true: that you are somewhere in a tunnel, or that you have been somewhere this whole time and the paper does not know.”

The clinic room door opened. Lena said, “Two minutes.”

Nadi did not use them for herself.

“Do you still have the run sheet,” she said. “The carbon. Not the copy, the sheet.”

“In the desk drawer at the depot. Nobody has cleaned the depot desk since the year your father died.”

“Good. Do not go and get it. Do not go and get it this week. If you go and get it, somebody will ask you why, and you will have to say what our building did.”

“I am not going to the depot,” Yolanda said. “I am going to work. I have a shift at eleven and I have not slept and I am going anyway, because if I do not go I will sit here and wait for a list to change.”

“That is the correct decision,” Nadi said. “And I want you to know that when I know something, you will hear it from me, and not from a form.”

The line went dead at Lena's shoulder. Nadi put the handset back on its cradle with both hands and stood there.

In the corridor nobody said anything for a moment.

“Her shift is at eleven,” Nadi said. “She has not slept and she is going to work. That is the piece of this that I am going to keep.”

---

The transit operations desk for the west apron was in a prefab building at the edge of the Meridian, and it was open, and it was staffed by a young man called **Bex Amadi** who had been on the counter for two years and who answered routine questions with the flat cheerfulness of somebody who had been asked the same four things all week.

They went in the afternoon, Joon and Elias and Nadi, because Nadi refused to stay behind and Elias understood that this was the point.

The paper first. The stub was real: a carbon of a west apron relief run sheet, soft at the folds, with the depot's stamp on it and a tear at the corner where it had been pulled out of a book. It carried a serial in the corner.

“Read me the last four,” Nadi said.

Joon did not read it out in the depot. He turned the sheet so she could see it, and she looked at it for a long moment and then put her finger on the corner, and Elias watched her decide something.

“It is hers,” she said.

Joon photographed the stub with the stamp and the tear in frame, and wrote Bex down as a witness to the physical object, and Bex wrote his own name and his shift number on the copy without being asked, because he had worked out what the room was.

Then Joon asked the question that mattered.

“Shuttle six on the incident morning. Was it dispatched from the west apron before the rail alarm?”

Bex turned to the screen. “Give me the date and the service.”

“The date is fine. The service is fine. I want a routine dispatch confirmation without an incident reference.”

Bex's fingers stopped.

“I can do routine,” he said. “I cannot do it with a Kestrel reference anywhere near the query, because the moment the two touch, the enquiry attaches to the incident file and locks for review, and then it is not mine to answer, and you get a letter.”

“Then do it without.”

“Without a reference, I am asking whether shuttle six ran on a Tuesday in the ordinary way. That is not a false question. It is a smaller one.”

“It is a smaller one and it is the same one,” Elias said, “and I will not do it, and neither will you.”

Bex looked at him. “Then you want the record and you want it attached to an incident that is under review by an office that has already classified it as equipment failure. So you want the record and the letter. Take both.”

They took both. It would take nine days and it would come from a different building.

---

The man at the counter was not new. He had been standing by the timetable poster with a laminated card and a paper cup of coffee since they came in, and now he set the cup down.

He was perhaps fifty, well dressed in a way that had taken money rather than effort, and he was extremely pleasant.

“Ms. Bell,” he said. “Ruta Amsel’s group. Nadi. Seven from the alcove.” He said it lightly, the way a man says a name he has looked up.

Nadi went very still.

“I did not give you my name.”

“You did not. There were four hundred and nine people through the Quarter yesterday afternoon and somebody on my side takes an interest in the Kestrel count, because the count is the only interesting thing anybody has done on this line in a decade. That is not sinister. It is how I make a living.” He turned the laminated card around. It was not an advertisement. It listed services in plain type: **BRANCH ROUTING — OFF-EXCHANGE — FAMILY MOVES — 24 HOURS.**

“Let me be clear about what I do,” he said. “I move people who need to move and cannot. Off-exchange calls so a family is not a row in somebody's file. Branch routing when the ground is bad and the institutions are worse. And records, when a record is wrong and the office that owns it will not correct it.”

“Which of those is this?” Joon said.

“All three, this morning, and I would be glad to do them in any order you like.” He looked at Nadi. “The row. Your sister's name came off the response list and your family address came off the shelter roll, and I can put both back in by six this evening. It is not difficult work. It is work somebody has to be paid to do and somebody has to be paid not to mention it.”

Elias felt the offer land in him as something uncomfortably close to relief. Six hours. It would have solved the thing they had come for, and nobody at the hall would ever know, and the woman on the counter would have her family back in a system that could see her.

“How much,” Nadi said.

“Four hundred for the list work. Sixty a month for the line. And nothing for the rest, because the rest is not a sale, it is a favour.” He said it kindly. “The favour is: I do not report this conversation to a Continuity Office that will write your address somewhere. You are not interesting to them. You are a footnote with an allergy. I am the only person in this city who will look at a wrong record and be able to leave it alone.”

“Why would you do that for free?” Elias said.

“Because in four years you will need me and I would like you to have had a free afternoon.”

Then he set the card on the counter, and the offer underneath the offer came out, and it came out because the polite one had been accepted by nobody and he had done this before.

“There is a third thing,” he said. “Seven of you came out of that alcove, and the ground you came out of is still bad, and the Office has a truck on your street and a form with your count on it. There is a route. Not the Spine, not the tunnel, not the north stair. It goes out through the basin and it puts a group of seven on firm ground in about four hours with no exposure and no daylight and no form. It works tonight.”

“Nobody goes anywhere tonight,” Elias said.

“I have not finished. It works because somebody is holding it. A branch route is not a door, it is a tension, and a tension needs a line of people standing in it who cannot be moved until it finishes. Seven is a good number. Six is workable. I would take seven, because seven means nobody has to be left on the near side.”

He said it the way a man explains a seat-belt.

Nadi's voice came out flat and level. “You mean us.”

“I mean people who have already been through the worst part and are standing in a building with a truck outside the door. I mean seven of you and twenty minutes, and a man on each end, and the far end is solid ground.”

“No,” Nadi said.

The pleasantness did not leave the man's face, but something behind it rearranged itself. “Ma'am, in four months you will be glad to have been in the room when it was possible.”

“No. Not in four months, not in four years, and not if you came back for it twice.” She stepped away from the counter, and then turned back, and Elias watched her make a decision she was not making for his case. “And I want you to write something down for me. Write that the offer was made, and write that I said no, and write the date, and put your name on it, and give it to him.” She pointed at Joon without looking at him. “Because in four years somebody is going to tell a story about this afternoon, and I want a piece of paper that says the woman said no on her own feet.”

The man looked at her for a moment. Then he laughed, delighted, as though she had said something extremely good.

“She is the real thing,” he said to Elias, half-jokingly. “You should keep her.”

Then he wrote it. Name, date, time, the three offers, the refusal, the word *anchors* where it belonged, and signed it **R. ADARE**, and tore it off and handed it across, and did it willingly, which was the detail that stayed with Joon for three days.

---

They walked back through the Meridian with the stub photographed, the letter pending, the refusal signed, and nothing on any screen anywhere that said a woman named Yolanda Bell was the sister of a woman who was breathing in the union hall.

At the corner of Ash Street the light went strange, the way the light had gone strange over Kestrel, and Elias stopped, and Nadi stopped, and neither of them said anything for a while.

Then Nadi said, “You did not ask her name. When you could have. You could have got the whole thing in one call — you could have asked Yolanda Bell to confirm a statement, put her in a document, and had a living relative on the record by lunch, and instead she is a person on a telephone who worked out the arithmetic on a receipt.”

“Yes,” Elias said.

“Why.”

“Because the moment her name is in a document, she is in the record, and the record is the thing that lost her.” He heard how it sounded, coming out of him, and it sounded like a rule rather than a decision, which was the direction he was trying to stop travelling in. He tried again. “I will not put her in. If that costs us the argument, it costs us the argument.”

Nadi looked at him. “It is not only the argument. It is her.”

“I know it is her,” Elias said. “That is why I am not doing it, and I would have done it this morning, and I want you to know that it cost me something, because you will not believe me otherwise.”

She nodded and kept walking, and after a while she said, “When somebody finally asks her, in a room I am not in, you are not the one who asks. Joon is the one who asks. You will be in the room and you will say nothing, because you will want to say something, and I do not want to hear it.”

“Agreed.”

“You will hate it.”

“Yes,” Elias said.

---

In the hall that evening, Joon built the fifth copy of the Bell file, which was more copies than any other item in the incident had, and Elias understood as he watched that this was what Nadi had actually asked for.

It went to Joon. It went to Inez's lock-box. It went into the cooperative's residents' file, where Ru Sabar initialed the receipt because that was how Ru Sabar treated every document. It went to the Office under cover of the exchange, because a discrepancy that only exists in one building is a discrepancy that can be ended by losing a building. It went into Elias's notebook, and he wrote the whole afternoon in his right hand, and then wrote one line underneath it and stopped:

**A row missing from a list is not an error. It is a person who cannot be found by the people who are looking for her. We have a sister who remembers a run sheet and a family of five in a building the list calls four. That is a civic injury and not a puzzle, and every hour we spend on the route we do not spend on it is a choice.**

He closed the notebook and put it away, and Nadi Bell was at the long table teaching Petar Lisk to spell Yolanda, and neither of them was waiting for anybody to approve it.
