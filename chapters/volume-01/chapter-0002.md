# Chapter 2 — Keep One Breath

Three knocks meant someone was using the emergency code for *route unsafe*. Elias had taught it to school groups during the Cinder Quarter’s annual safety week: three for stop, two for hold, one for withdraw. It was not a request. It was a fact being passed through whatever remained between one place and another.

He kept one hand on the rope and reached the service landing.

The carriage had stopped being behind a wall. It now sat six feet below him at the end of the landing, buckled inward around a concrete support column. Fifteen people were visible through its broken windows. Some crouched in the aisle. Some lay across seats. None of them moved toward the doors.

The landing dropped half an inch under Elias’s boot, then rose again.

“Structural breathing,” Tomas Vale said behind him. “Don’t step twice in the same rhythm.”

Tomas had reached the apron with the spreader team. He secured his own line to Elias’s, then looked at the carriage without pretending the safety report had explained anything.

“Move the spreaders to the column base,” Elias said.

“They’re already moving.”

“You beat me to it.”

“Your radio called for them.” Tomas crouched over the track bed. “That’s not a complaint.”

Elias descended. The first landing step took his weight. The second dropped a fraction lower, then rose against his boot. He moved sideways along the wall, testing each surface with the flat of his sole. Ordinary concrete flexed under the repeating pressure. The carriage roof ticked above him.

At the carriage doors, a woman in an orange maintenance coat lay across the threshold. The impact had driven a steel handrail through the lower left side of a man’s chest. He was pinned beneath it, one boot hooked around a seat brace. Blood had spread through the torn fabric of his shirt in a black-red oval. His eyes were open, but they did not follow Elias when he called.

“Mara!” Elias called. “Triage pocket!”

The physician came through the platform stair with two medics behind her. Mara Okafor wore her black hair under an orange strip of rescue tape and carried a trauma kit as if she had been born holding it. She took one look at the carriage, at the man beneath the rail, and moved past the first responder who had begun cutting his coat.

“Stop there,” she said. “The rail is tamponading something. We remove it wrong, he bleeds out.”

A medic found the passenger’s triage band and read the name. “Tarin Voss. Car 3.”

Mara dropped to her knees beside the injury. Tarin dragged in air, but he did not answer when she said his name. His lips had the blue-gray color Elias had learned to fear in the old first-aid courses.

“Keep his airway clear,” Mara said. “He is not responding.”

The carriage groaned. The landing fell again, and a bright crack raced across Mara’s light. She caught herself on the threshold.

Tomas shouted from the rail bed. “The pressure cycle is shortening. Forty seconds now.”

Elias saw the maintenance trolley where it had been staged for the relay crew: a low motorized cart with two rubber tires and a smaller orange drive wheel at the rear. The offer on Mara’s terminal had not appeared yet. He saw the trolley because it was the only thing in the landing that could hold the hydraulic spreader and oxygen tank level.

A Civic Spine display flickered awake at the triage station. Plain text assembled itself from the relay’s broken translation layer.

```text
Loan offered: Mara Okafor, seventeen years ahead
Gift: Keep one breath from failing while the body is repaired.
Boundary: Do not use my hand to kill. Do not use my hand to abandon a person.
Return: When the third wheel stops turning.
```

The words did not appear on every screen. They appeared on Elias’s wrist display, on Mara’s, and on the nearest emergency repeater. Joon’s voice came through the speaker beneath them.

“I only have half the offer,” he said. “Relay’s cutting it apart.”

“That is the whole offer.” Elias read every line aloud. His voice sounded strange in his own ears. “It names Mara Okafor as the lender. The gift is limited to keeping one body from failing while the body is repaired. The boundary forbids killing or abandonment. The return is when the third wheel on that maintenance trolley stops turning.”

Mara looked at him. “Repeat the last part.”

“The third wheel stops, the loan ends. I do not get to decide when.”

Beneath the words came a woman’s voice, older and roughened by static. “In my branch, we found a living person behind the cleaner route. We left him while we chased the carriage signal. I remember reaching him too late.”

Mara’s hands stopped for one beat over Tarin’s wound. It was the only sign that she had heard the voice beneath her own name.

“Present emergency consent,” Elias said. “Tarin is unresponsive. Do we have emergency treatment for him?”

Mara looked at Tarin, then at the blood around the handrail. “Yes. I am using emergency treatment rules. I authorize the treatment I judge necessary. I do not consent on behalf of any future version of me.”

“State that for the record,” Elias called to Joon.

Joon’s keyboard clattered. Mara’s face tightened at the reminder that every word was being entered, but she kept working.

“If that voice is mine,” she said, “she has given permission for one repair, not for a promise. Do you understand?”

“I understand.”

“Repeat it.”

“The gift is for Tarin Voss only. We use it while you repair him. We do not move it to another person, force treatment, kill anyone, or leave anyone. When the third wheel stops, it ends.”

Mara checked Tarin’s pulse by touch when the portable monitor flickered out. “Then ask for it.”

Elias had never borrowed anything from the future. He had borrowed strength from sleep he did not have, experience from instructors who no longer taught, and certainty from people who trusted him enough to let him speak first. This was different. The offer sat between his hands like a tool someone else had decided to place there.

He looked at the steel rail through Tarin’s chest. At the living man beneath it. At the route he could not promise to keep open.

“I accept the loan for Tarin Voss,” he said. “Give me one breath while Mara repairs him. Nothing beyond that.”

The text on his wrist display vanished. The temperature in his left hand dropped.

For an instant, his fingers did not belong to him. He felt a memory that had never belonged to his life: standing in a brighter station with orange tape around a white column, hearing Mara say a number he did not recognize, and feeling blood between two fingers while someone counted down. The memory held more fear than his own because it had been lived longer.

Elias clenched his jaw and forced his hand to close on the rope. The sensation came back as needles.

“How long do you need?” he asked Mara.

“The pressure cycles are making this worse. Two minutes without another collapse, maybe. Thirty seconds if the next one comes early.”

“Then we give you thirty.”

He turned to Tomas. “Move the powered trolley under the oxygen. Keep its rear wheel turning. I need it moving.”

“Keep it turning how long?” Tomas asked.

“Until the offer says it stops. I won’t be the one deciding.”

Tomas studied him for one second, then nodded. “You heard the field conditions.”

“I read them aloud.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“It has to be enough for now.”

Tomas sent two responders down with the trolley. Elias clipped a sling beneath Tarin’s shoulders and placed a broad steel plate beside the wound, ready to support the rail as it came free. Mara threaded packing gauze beneath the metal and clamped the bleeding point. Her movements were exact. Each breath she took seemed to measure the same distance as Tarin’s.

Elias put his numb hand around the carriage frame instead of the rope.

“Two,” he called. “One. Lift.”

The rail did not move at first. He felt the wrongness before the metal began to rise: a cold pressure behind his wrist, as if the carriage and the wall were pulling in different directions. His hand held. His shoulder took the weight when the steel shifted.

Mara drew the rail free in a long scrape. Tarin’s body arched; the monitor stayed black; air rushed wetly through the wound. Mara pressed a seal over it and worked with both hands.

“Keep the hand away from him,” she said. “No matter what you feel.”

Elias obeyed. It was the first time obeying felt more useful than deciding.

Behind them, the trolley crossed a joint. Its small orange wheel spun against the rail.

They held.

The landing dropped again on the next cycle. This time the powered wheel kept turning, and the offer remained present in the pressure of Elias’s left hand. Tarin’s body remained terribly still, but Mara’s seal held. Color crept back to his lips one shade at a time.

“Pressure is stable,” she said. “I can move him if the next landing is open.”

Elias turned. Where the carriage had blocked the service corridor, a narrow door now stood in the end wall. Paint had peeled around its frame. Water marked the lower panels. A brass plate above it read **CAR 3 SERVICE**.

No such door appeared in his paper map.

“Keep your hand on the rail,” Tomas called from the trolley. “That frame is carrying the wall.”

Elias shifted the mechanical anchor across the threshold and saw fresh tool marks on the concrete: bright metal scrapes, a line of wet footprints, and a handle polished from the inside.

The door was real enough to leave marks. It was not real enough to trust.

Mara looked at the new opening, then at Tarin. “I need thirty more seconds.”

Elias faced the door and raised his soundless, aching hand.
