# Chapter 7 — No Answer

By late afternoon, the Kestrel rescue had acquired the exhausted shape of a normal disaster.

Stretchers stood in rows beneath a canvas shelter. Medics warmed saline bags over a rescued heater. Outside the yellow line, a parent who had arrived separately from the station demanded that every rescuer repeat a list, while the child’s name was written on a paper card in Joon’s hand. The parent and child were not part of Kestrel’s rescued count. Someone had brought coffee in a metal thermos. It tasted burnt, but Mara drank half a cup before returning to the airway kit.

The Civic Spine team had set up a portable terminal beside the refuge. Its screen displayed a blank count field and the instruction **AWAIT CONSOLIDATED RESPONSE LIST**. The count should have been sixteen. The terminal offered a space for one number and no place to write that the route had changed during the answer.

Elias sat on an overturned crate with his left hand in a warming pack. The physician had checked his nerves, circulation, and grip. The hand responded to pressure and temperature, but the fine movements had not returned. He could hold a rope if he watched it. He could not trust it to tell him when a knot had slipped.

Mara crouched beside a patient whose breathing had turned shallow after the journey. “No loan,” she said without looking at him. “We use the oxygen, the monitor, and the hands we have.”

“I was going to ask for the old offer.”

“Your wanting is not an emergency.”

Elias watched the sealed tunnel entrance from under the shelter. The air above the ramp had a faint shimmer, as if heat rose from pavement that remained wet. The changed door was below. The north stair was gone from the index. The signal remained.

He rose and walked to the old maintenance relay with Joon.

The relay was mounted on a steel frame outside the yellow line, its battery pack surrounded by a ring of charged lamps. The lamps were not part of the Civic Spine kit. A neighborhood repair crew had lent them after hearing Joon’s request for light that did not depend on a live network. One had a cracked lens and a switch tied with orange cord. It could show whether a nearby future voice had consented to be heard. It could not make a seam open.

Joon had the raw recorder beside him. “If you try the offer, the relay may carry the refusal.”

“Or it may fail.”

“Then we record the difference.”

Elias placed two fingers against the relay frame. It was warm from the battery, not from a future hand. He stated the concrete goal aloud, as he had learned to do with ordinary tools.

“Mara Okafor’s future self, this is Elias Rook. We do not need the Still Hand. I am testing whether the return is complete. I will not ask you to hold another body or extend the exchange.”

For a moment, only the battery hum answered. Then the terminal showed one short notice.

```text
No answer
```

It was not the same blank as a dead relay. The letters appeared cleanly, held for three seconds, and vanished. Joon wrote the time, the battery level, and the word refusal in his paper log.

“That was deliberate,” Elias said.

“Or someone built a very convincing machine.”

“The battery was fine. The relay was fine. The offer was not answered.”

Joon looked toward the tunnel. “You do not know that.”

“No. I know the difference between a missing signal and a no.”

A burst of static came from the recorder. Joon had left the Kestrel maintenance channel open for the unknown voice. He turned the gain down, then back up when a human shape emerged in the waveform.

A man said, “If the map is clean, someone is standing on the missing people.”

Elias’s first thought was that the voice belonged to the Office. His second was that it belonged to an echo. His third was less certain and more frightening: it was neither. It was a recording from a relay that should not have been active, carrying a man who knew about the contradiction before Elias had found the words for it.

“Who is this?” Elias asked.

The speaker did not answer immediately. A relay clicked. Water or wind moved through the channel. When the voice returned, it was closer in tone, though still too damaged to place.

“Keep the paper. Keep the raw audio. Do not make the map agree.”

Elias looked at Joon. Joon’s thumb hovered over the recorder’s save button.

“I am not looking for a faction,” Elias said to the speaker. “I am not treating you as proof of anything. I am keeping the evidence because people crossed that route.”

The voice gave a sound that might have been a breath or might have been relay noise. “Then it is not useless.”

The channel went quiet. It did not offer a route, a gift, or an identity. It did not promise that keeping the records would produce a safe answer.

Joon pressed save. “Raw file copied. The voice is still there.”

“Keep the speaker, not just the file,” Elias said. “I want the next person to hear the actual sound.”

Mara came to the relay, wiping gloves between her fingers. “Did you ask for more power?”

“No.”

“Did you ask to reopen the loan?”

“I asked whether the return was complete. The answer was no.”

“Thank you for saying no to yourself before I had to.”

“It was not a victory.”

“No.” She looked at the tunnel. “But we can still work.”

Tomas came from the stairwell with a folded map and a red grease pencil. “The Office wants a consolidated headcount. They want one number, one route status, and one explanation for the seam.”

“We can give them sixteen and unknown,” Elias said.

“They will call that a failure.”

“They can call it what they need to call it. We will not invent the missing people.”

Tomas’s expression tightened. “They are not missing. They are here.”

“That is the distinction they do not want.”

The phrase came out harder than Elias intended. Tomas looked at him, then at the paper tally.

“Your father would have wanted them out,” Tomas said.

Elias’s mouth tightened. “My father stayed in a route everyone else called temporary. I am not going to argue about him with a station full of people who need a number.”

“No,” Tomas said. “You are going to do what he could not. You are going to admit you do not know the route is safe.”

The argument stayed between them without becoming a fight. Mara set a medical ledger on the crate and opened it to the page where the first offer had been disclosed.

“Write the names first,” she said. “Then the measurements. Then what each person witnessed. If the Office wants a single number, let them have a number attached to the truth around it.”

Joon added a small line to the paper map: **Route observed. Current index contradictory.** He drew a box around the old service door and left a gap in the line instead of connecting it to a wall.

The temporary anchor lamp beside the relay flickered once, then went dark. No panel appeared. No loan began. The lamp had recorded no consent because none was offered.

Elias watched the dark lens and felt the absence of a future hand as a physical weight. He had wanted the power to remain available, even if he did not use it. The refusal denied him that comfort.

A vehicle stopped outside the yellow line. Its doors opened before anyone had called the Office. A woman in a dark gray coat stepped into the triage yard carrying a document case. Behind her came two staff members in white identification bands.

Joon closed the recorder but did not switch it off.

“Office,” he said.

Elias looked at the tunnel, the ledger, and the paper map. The route had changed, the future had refused, and the next authority had arrived before the records could be compared.

The gray-coated woman looked at the altered wall, then at Elias.

“Director Arden,” Tomas said quietly. “Now we find out whether safety means protecting the living or protecting the report.”

Elias set his pen down.

“Both,” he said. “That is the part they keep leaving out.”
