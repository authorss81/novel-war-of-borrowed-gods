# Chapter 5 — The North Stair

The north stair was not a place they could reach by going north.

Joon had the old maintenance map spread across the canopy table, weighted with a wrench and two batteries. The signal came through a recorder balanced on an overturned crate, thin and uneven beneath the static.

“North stair is a branch, not an exit,” Nell’s voice said. “Don’t let the green lamp choose.”

The recording stopped.

Joon rewound it and played it again. “The carrier is live. It entered the station network after the collapse, and it’s being pulled through the north maintenance relay. I can’t get a clean location, but I can get direction.”

“Direction to what?” Elias asked.

“The stair. Or a version of it.”

Tomas pointed to the paper map. “The north stair is two levels down and then east. If the relay is feeding that packet, the route may be intact for a few seconds.”

“May,” Mara said.

She had moved Tarin to the first medical tent and returned with a compact oxygen cylinder, a trauma pouch, and the expression of someone who had already heard a future version of herself set a boundary. Tarin was stable. The seal held. The medics had him on a monitor powered by a battery, with someone counting his respirations by hand.

“His pressure is holding,” Mara said. “The hand is not needed for him now.”

Elias looked at the fading text on his wrist. “I heard.”

“I know. I’m making sure you heard the other part.”

She lowered her voice. “I will not ask that voice to prolong the loan for the person we have not found. If the gift is for one body, it stays with that body.”

“She could refuse,” Elias said.

“The terms already refuse any larger use. I’m not asking her to cross a boundary to make our search easier.”

The words were sensible. They were also a door closing in front of him.

Joon handed Elias a thermal camera. “The signal is strongest at the maintenance landing. Oren Pike is listed on the Car 3 manifest, seat 3C-18. He was not in the carriage count. If he is alive, he is below the old platform or inside a service pocket.”

“Could the signal be bait?” Tomas asked.

“Could the collapse be bait?” Joon replied. “We work with what is there.”

Elias shouldered a short length of rope and checked the knots. The powered maintenance trolley sat on the old service line, its orange rear wheel turning in a smooth circle. A cable ran from the cart to a hydraulic brace they had positioned beside the carriage. The line was the same one that had held the changed door open. When the wheel turned, the brace tightened by a fraction. When it paused, the pressure in the landing changed.

The first offer had become a practical tool as well as a debt. Elias disliked the comfort of that.

“Move in pairs,” he said. “Joon, keep the recorder with us. Tomas, stay on the main line. If the stair changes, we come back to the trolley, not toward the signal.”

Mara fell into step beside him. “I am coming because you may find someone who cannot move.”

“I know.”

“Do not turn that into gratitude.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

They descended by the route the first group had used, past the service door’s black gap and into a lower landing where the walls were older and the pipes had been painted so many times their edges looked soft. The air smelled of wet iron. Water moved in a thin channel along the floor, carrying dust from the old rail bed.

The north stair appeared ahead of them.

It had a steel handrail, six concrete steps, and a sign fixed to the wall. The sign said **NORTH SERVICE / NO PUBLIC ACCESS**. The stair rose into a darkness that seemed deeper than the building allowed.

Elias pointed his thermal camera down it. The first steps were warm. The sixth was cold. A heat bloom hovered beyond the final turn, too small for a person and too steady for a flame.

The future voice came through the wrist display without a new notice. It was only a thread of sound, folded beneath the relay’s hum.

“In my branch, the stair held,” the older Mara said. “That does not mean it holds here.”

Elias stopped.

“Did you hear that?” Mara asked.

“Future Mara. She’s answering the stair, not offering a new loan.”

“Does she know whether Oren is there?”

“She doesn’t say.”

The lack of an answer was almost worse than a warning. A warning could be obeyed. This left a space where a person might be trapped, a route that might work, and a future version of Mara who would not pretend certainty.

He looked at the stair. A narrow signal line ran from the relay box beside the sign. The light above the landing was green. The green lamp in Nell’s transmission had been a warning, or a trap, or both.

“We go to the sixth step,” Elias said. “No farther until the structure answers.”

He placed a mechanical anchor on the handrail and ran the rope through it. Mara checked the oxygen cylinder. They moved together, one person testing the floor before the other committed weight.

The third step dropped.

Elias caught the rope, not the rail. The future pressure in his left hand flared, and for one breath he felt the stair as it had been in the branch that had offered the loan: solid beneath his boots, a door at the top, Mara calling from the other side. Then the sensation vanished. His hand went numb again.

“Do not follow the memory,” Mara said.

“I wasn’t.”

“You looked at the stair as if it had betrayed you.”

“It did.”

“It changed. That is not betrayal.”

He disliked the distinction when she was right.

At the sixth step, the thermal camera found a body behind a maintenance cage. The cage was full of dead relays and coiled cable. A man lay on his side behind it, one arm threaded through a broken shelf, his legs pressed against the wall. He was alive. His jacket bore the reflective strip of a Car 3 passenger.

Elias crouched. “Oren Pike?”

The man lifted his head. “You’re the man with the old radio.”

“Can you move your legs?”

“Ask them.”

The answer came from the legs themselves: a tremor, then a spasm. Oren’s ankle was turned at an angle that made Elias’s stomach tighten. The shelf had pinned his left arm. He had crawled there when the carriage vanished, following a wall that was not in the same place twice.

Mara crouched beside him. “I need to free the arm before the ankle. The shelf is holding pressure on a vessel.”

“I can cut the shelf,” Elias said.

“If you cut it wrong, the pressure goes into his chest.” Mara looked at the route behind them. “I can work here, but we need a line back to the trolley. If the pressure changes, we move him before the stair changes again.”

Elias looked toward the relay box. The green lamp blinked. The signal in Joon’s recorder sharpened, and Nell’s voice came through again, broken by the cage.

“Rook—don’t—”

The rest disappeared.

The stair behind them shifted. A wall of dark water appeared across the landing, then drained away to reveal concrete. The route to the north was no longer continuous. The relay line remained.

Elias put his hand on the rope. “Mara, how long?”

“Two minutes if the shelf behaves. Five if it doesn’t.”

“I can ask the voice for more time.”

“No.”

The refusal was immediate. Mara did not soften it with explanation.

Elias turned to the mechanical brace and began placing wedges along the floor. He did not use the borrowed hand. He set the first wedge with his boot, the second with his right palm, and the third by shoulder. Each piece of steel was ordinary. Each was chosen because it could fail in a way he could measure.

“Tomas, I need the trolley line forward,” he called. “No more power to the door. Bring the line to the sixth step and keep the rear wheel turning.”

A pause came over the radio. Then Tomas said, “The wheel is turning. The door is moving.”

“Leave it. The line is more important.”

“If the line fails, the door may be the only route back.”

“Then we will have learned that too late.” Elias tightened the rope around the anchor. “Bring the line.”

He left a breadcrumb at every turn: a strip of orange tape, a loop of white cord, a smear of thermal paste on the wall. It was slower than following the signal. It was also the only way he could give Mara a physical return route instead of a guess.

When the line arrived, they transferred Oren’s arm and moved him toward the sixth step. The future voice whispered once more, “One body.”

Elias closed his eyes. “I know.”

He waited until Mara finished with the shelf. He waited through the pressure change, the trolley’s motor, and the sound of Oren biting down on a strap. When they finally moved the man onto the stretcher, Elias took the rear line and kept his numb hand open rather than gripping it.

The north stair opened for an instant. Beyond it was a low chamber with a second wall and a door he had never seen. Nell’s signal came from that direction, close enough to make his chest hurt.

He looked at Mara. “The signal is there.”

“I heard it.”

“We could mark it.”

“We could. We also have one injured passenger, a changing stair, and a line that does not come back if we lose the wall.”

Oren raised his head from the stretcher. “The stair appears when the map is wrong,” he said. “I saw it twice. Both times the map said there was nothing.”

“Did you see the person who sent the signal?”

Oren looked toward the dark. “No. I saw someone keeping a door open.”

The wall shifted. The second door vanished before Elias could reach it. A slab of concrete settled across the opening, leaving only a narrow black seam. He could still hear the relay humming on the other side.

Joon’s voice came through the radio, strained. “Elias, the route index just removed the north stair. The paper map still has it.”

Elias looked at the line in his hands, then at Mara.

“Take Oren back,” he said. “We can mark the wall. We do not follow it tonight.”

Mara nodded. “For now.”

They moved the stretcher toward the maintenance trolley. The black seam stayed behind them. By the time they reached the refuge ramp, the stair had become a blank stretch of wall, and the only proof it had ever been there was the orange tape on Elias’s glove and a signal still repeating beneath the relay.
