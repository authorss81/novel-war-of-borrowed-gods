# Chapter 18 — The Deeper Heat

They started at nine, because Inez had written nine on the board, and they were due to stop at three, because Inez had written three under it, and both times were announced in the hall and on Ash Street by a boy with a clipboard who took the announcement seriously enough to shout it.

The load plan came off the hall wall, where it had lived for two days, and went into Rafi's coat. The stop plan was a separate page, one survey, expiring that evening, and it carried the five names the board had carried, plus the hall, which could stop it by sending anybody out of the door.

“What is the target,” Tomas asked at the apron, one more time, for the record, with Joon's recorder running.

“The junction, the altered wall, and the space behind it,” Elias said. “Thermal, acoustic, gas, and a bore scope on a line. No door opened. The whole team comes out together.”

“Nobody holds anything.”

“Nobody holds anything.”

“Nine hundred hours to fifteen hundred hours,” Inez said from the top of the ramp, where the feeder switch was, and where she had positioned herself with a clipboard and a thermos. “If I cut it, you are on your own batteries in ninety seconds, and you will be slower, and you will still be here, and I will have done what I said I would do on the board in front of thirty-one people.”

They went down.

---

The lower junction had been quiet for three days in a way that made it louder.

Water still pushed through the gap near the floor in short pulses, carrying dust and the smell of burned cable, and the seam glass in Inez's frame in the hall had thrown the same duplicate shadow three nights running for anybody who stood still long enough. The altered wall looked exactly as it had looked when Nadi Bell's group came out through it: concrete, a horizontal gap, a cold draft that smelled of nothing at all.

Below the junction, the platform slab was where Tomas had spent the first forty minutes, with a laser measure on a tripod and a displacement gauge set to his own number.

“Baseline,” he said. “The instruments decide, not me. I want that in the file in those words.”

“Left hand's gone,” Elias said, before he had been asked, because his left hand had opened on the tripod leg and closed on air while he was taking the weight. “Give me the other end of that tape.”

Kellan gave him the other end. Nobody made an event of it, which was its own kind of event, and Elias noticed that he had started to be able to bear it for about nine seconds at a time.

Mara set up at the top of the ramp with the oxygen cylinder and a monitor and a hard rule: nobody goes further than the line, and nobody who is not on the line goes at all. Joon sat on an upturned crate with the recorder, the gas meter, and a paper sheet, and he wrote the time at every reading because he had learned that a number without a time is a rumour.

The readings did not agree, and they had never agreed, and by ten o'clock they had agreed to disagree in a documented way.

The first thermal camera, the one from the platform, put two human-sized signatures behind the wall at the lower junction, four and a half metres in, weak, separated by about three metres.

The second camera, borrowed from a depot in the Meridian and older than the first, put one signature at the same depth and a second bloom at nine metres that might have been a person and might have been a hot pipe.

“Two or one and a bloom,” Joon said, writing both.

“Do not average them,” Elias said.

“I was not going to average them. I have written *camera one: two. camera two: one and a possible bloom.*”

The gas meter gave them the first thing nobody had expected. The oxygen fell through the whole ninety minutes, and carbon dioxide rose, and both of them did it in a curve that was much too smooth for a fire and much too slow for a person.

“That is a room,” Tomas said. “A big one, and it has been closed long enough for the air to have opinions.”

“Or a shaft,” said a woman's voice.

---

It came out of the relay and the anchor lamp at the same moment, which Joon noted on paper, and which Elias did not like at all.

There was no notice of a gift. The lamp came up, and the text on the repeater assembled itself, and it said two things and nothing else.

```text
No loan offered
Warning: the branch is changing
```

Elias read it aloud for the record, because that was the practice now, and then he said into the relay, “I hear you. State what you want and what you will not allow.”

“I am not lending you anything,” the woman said. Her voice was unhurried and slightly hoarse, and it had the flatness of somebody who had been shouting in a stone space for a long time. “I want you to go home. I am telling you where the concrete is because you have written down the right to stop, and in my branch nobody ever wrote that down.”

“Who are you?”

“Halla Dren. I run outfall shafts and service tunnels nineteen years from where you are standing, on the north bank, and I have spent eleven of those years keeping a shaft open that your index calls a void.” A pause. “I am answering because of your paper. Do not make more of it than that.”

“Where is the concrete?”

“There is a second pour in the apron slab. Nineteen thirty-one repair work, different aggregate, laid against the original because the original was lifted. It runs eleven metres, north-north-east, and it stops six metres short of where you are pointing your cameras. Your heat is not behind the original slab. It is behind the repair.”

Elias looked down. The slab under his boots had a seam in it that he had walked over twice that morning and had not looked at, because it was a repair, and repairs are the least interesting things in a tunnel.

“Your instruments are correct and your conclusion is wrong,” Halla Dren said. “That is not a trick. You have two cameras made by two companies in two decades and they disagree because one of them is reading a warm pipe that a crew in 2031 ran for a pump house. There is a person in my shaft. In yours I cannot tell you, and I am not going to tell you, because I do not know, and I will not sell you a certainty to make this conversation worth more.”

Elias's hands had gone cold. He asked the question he had been not-asking since the first minute.

“How do we get to them?”

“You do not.”

“No. Tell me what I am allowed to ask you for.”

“You are allowed to ask me nothing,” she said, “and I will answer what I can. If you ask me for a hand to hold that wall, or for a way through, or for my branch to lean on yours, the answer is no, and it will keep being no, and if you ask twice you will find the relay has gone quiet and I will not be able to warn you about anything at all, including this conversation.”

“Understood. I will not ask twice.”

“Good. Then here is the whole of it, and it is not enough. Do not open the shaft. In my branch the shaft mouth is the only air four hundred people have, and it is open because it has never been sealed, and the moment it is named in a consolidated list as a location to be closed, it stops being air and starts being an item.” Her voice hardened by exactly one degree. “Do not write the shaft down. Not on your wall, not in your ledger, not in any list that goes to a building with a stamp on it. That is my want. I know what I am asking you to give up. I am asking anyway.”

Elias found that his hand had gone to his notebook.

“There is a person alive in a room and you are asking me not to write down where he is,” he said.

“I am asking you not to write down where *my* room is,” Halla Dren said. “You have two cameras and a gas curve. You can write those. You can write the second pour and the date of the repair and everything your instruments can carry. You cannot carry a location, because a location is the only thing I have that you do not.”

Joon's pencil had stopped. Elias looked at him and understood, with a clarity that felt like a blow, that the man holding the recorder was being asked to do the one thing his entire professional life was built not to do.

“That is a future person asking us to leave a hole in the record,” Elias said, “on the strength of her word.”

“Yes,” Halla Dren said. “And I know what your week has been like. You have been handed a clean map and told that the clean map is the lie. So here is the same offer from the other side. I am telling you that a clean map of this shaft is the safest thing that can happen to the people in it. I could be lying to you. I have a want and I have told you what it is, and you will do what you do with a want that is stated.”

The relay hummed. Then:

“If you open the shaft, or if you move anybody out of the Quarter without telling the people there first, I stop answering. That is not a threat, it is a boundary, and it is the only one I have left. And when the branch is changing, warnings stop being free.”

The channel closed.

Nobody asked her a second question. Elias found, when he went back over the next hour in his own head, that this was the hardest thing he had done in the tunnel, and he did not have a moment to think about why.

---

They put her wants on the page, exactly as stated, with her name and the time and the words *No loan offered* and nothing paraphrased, and then Tomas did something Elias had not expected: he read the page back to himself out loud before they moved, so that whatever happened next could not later be described as a team that had not been told.

“Eleven o'clock,” Joon said. “Camera one: two. Camera two: one and a possible bloom. Second pour found at eleven-forty, thirty-one repair, aggregate differs. Second pour terminates six metres short of the camera target. Warning from a possible person, nineteen years ahead, name given as Halla Dren, who declines to lend and states a boundary: the shaft is not to be named in any consolidated list. We have recorded the request. We have not accepted it. The entry stands as a request and not as an instruction.”

“And?” Elias said.

“And the request is refused,” Joon said, in the flattest voice he had, “because a record with a hole in it is a hole somebody can fill later. I am not leaving a space in a document because a voice on a relay asked me to. She is entitled to ask. She is not entitled to have it. If I did that, the next person to come through here would have to trust a gap, and gaps are what this whole week has been about.”

Elias felt the argument in him like a hot wire.

“She has four hundred people on that shaft.”

“She has told us that. She has told us nothing else, and she has told us that the shaft is air for them, and I believe her and I am not going to build her an address book out of a sentence on a relay.” Joon's jaw was set. “Elias. If I leave a gap, then in two years somebody can put a coordinate in that gap, and they will do it without asking, and she will never know how. I would rather we have a location we are ashamed of than a gap we are comfortable with.”

For a moment, the old Elias would have taken the notebook. He had a hand on the cover of it and his fingers had already moved.

Instead he said, “Write the request, keep the refusal, and keep the refusal in the same hand as the request. Both names on one page.”

“It is already one page.”

“Then it is the best answer we have.”

They ran the scope.

---

The bore scope went down the existing inspection bore on a line with a wax flag every two metres, because there was no other way to look without opening anything, and finding the second pour had changed the target by six metres in a direction nobody had argued for.

The concrete came up pale grey and then, at four metres, pale and coarse, and there was a lip at the interface, and Elias saw it on the monitor and put his hand on Rafi's arm before he said anything, because Rafi was feeding line.

“That is the repair. Hold.”

The line went on. At six and a half metres the scope passed a gap that was not a gap, an absence with a shape, and the picture went grainy and warm, and at seven metres the scope found a wall about a metre and a half on, made of brick.

“Brick,” Joon said. “Not concrete. That is a shaft lining, or a pump house, or a chamber somebody built inside a repair.”

“Do not guess yet.” Tomas ran the line back to the lip at four metres, took a forty-millimetre coupon off the bar exposed there, wrapped it in a paper twist, and put it in his breast pocket like a man pocketing a receipt.

They went back. The second camera was put on the bore line and aimed down the same hole, and for eleven minutes it read one human-sized signature, steady, at the wall.

“One,” Joon said. “Camera one still says two.”

“Write camera one still says two.”

“Camera one still says two.”

At noon Tomas tapped three times on the casing where the line passed the wall, because it was the code and because it was the only thing any of them had.

They waited.

Three came back. Then two. Then one.

The recorder was running and the microphone was against the concrete and the whole relief crew heard it, and the relay that Joon had left open carried it four hundred metres to the union hall, where Nadi Bell heard it and stood up off a cot. Joon's pencil stopped and stayed stopped.

“Route unsafe,” Elias said, into the recorder, so that it would be on the file. “Somebody is using the code. That is a person. That is one person and I am not going to say how many more, because one camera says one and one camera says two and I have been told all week not to make a number up.”

“Do you want me to knock again?” Rafi asked.

Elias looked at the wall, and at the gap, and at the dark, and he thought about his sister saying *do not let the green lamp choose*, and about a voice on a relay asking for a location to stay unnamed, and about a signature nine metres deep that might be a person and might be a pump.

“Yes,” he said. “Knock again.”

They knocked again. Nothing came back for ninety seconds, and then three, very faint, and then nothing.

---

The slab moved at twelve-forty.

Tomas's gauge did not do anything dramatic. It moved from a mark to a mark, four millimetres, over about a minute and a half, and it was the number in his own handwriting on a piece of paper that the team had signed in a hall two days earlier.

He did not say it. He turned the gauge so that Elias could see it, and then he turned it so Mara could see it, and then he looked at Elias, because the page said that anybody could call the stop and that the stop was not a discussion.

Elias's left hand was on the scope line, because his left hand was on everything, because his left hand did not know enough to stay away from things. It went open. The line jumped two metres through the bore, snagged, and Rafi shouted and hauled it back and the flag came up covered in fresh grey dust.

The wall in the screen flexed, and the picture grain went, and the range read nothing.

Nobody had said anything yet. The air smelled of dust and old water. Water was coming faster through the floor gap, and it was coming warm, and that was new, and Mara's hand was already on the cylinder.

Elias looked at the gauge, and at the wall, and at the knock that had come back three times, and at his own left hand lying open on a rope that had nearly taken the scope into a place nobody had measured.

He thought: if I say one more word, this is a rescue, and I do not have the room, the air, the concrete, or the permission, and there is a person on the other side of that wall who is using a code that a school safety week taught him.

He thought: my father was on the other side of a door that a rule said was closed, and the rule was mine, written by a man sitting in a boiler room an hour ago, and I have just watched that man give me the only piece of paper that could stop me.

“Nobody move,” Elias said. “That is the stop. I am calling it. Take the line up, everybody up the ramp, in the order we came down, and nobody touches the wall.”

The hall could stop it, he thought, and the hall had not stopped it, and he had, and he was the person who most wanted not to, and that was the entire difference and he would not be able to explain it to anybody except himself.

Nobody argued.

Rafi came up first with the line, then Kellan, then Joon backwards with the recorder held high, then Elias. Mara brought the cylinder and did not look back. Tomas took the tripod and the gauge and read the number one more time on the way up and wrote it down twice.

They were all on the ramp and accounted for in eleven minutes.

---

They brought back a sounding map, a gas curve, a camera disagreement, forty millimetres of a thirty-one rebar coupon, and a recording of somebody knocking in code.

They did not bring back a person.

“Write it exactly like that,” Elias said to Joon, at the top, with his hand shaking and his left hand still open. “We did not reach them. We did not open anything. We have one confirmed living person at a wall and an unconfirmed second reading nine metres in, and we stopped on a number that was written down before we went, by a man who wanted us to go, which is the only reason that number is worth anything.”

“The boundary,” Joon said. “The unopened shaft. Halla Dren’s request, refused, recorded as refused.”

“And her warning. *When the branch is changing, warnings stop being free.* Write that on the wall tonight, in the room, where the residents can read it, because in about a week somebody is going to say we were told and did not listen, and I want it in their handwriting as well as ours.”

Inez was at the top of the ramp with the clipboard and the thermos, and she had not cut the power, and she did not say so, and Elias understood that she was not going to.

“Three o’clock,” she said. “You have two hours of load left and you have used three and a half, and I would like the truth about what the rest of it is for.”

Elias looked at the ramp, and at the wall he could not see from here, and at the sealed envelope in his coat, and at the twenty-three people and thirty-one residents behind him.

“Listening,” he said. “Not entry. Whatever we decide, it is not entry.”

“Noted,” said Inez, and made him say it again so that she could write it, and it was written, and that was the first promise of his that had somebody else's handwriting on it.
