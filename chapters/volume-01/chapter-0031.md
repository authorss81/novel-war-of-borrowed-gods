# Chapter 31 — What He Wants

The board said Monday and the board got Monday, and the argument about Monday took twenty minutes and was lost by Elias in the first four.

It was not an argument about whether to go. It was an argument about who had signed.

“There are five names on the page,” Inez said. “Four of five, in the same week. This week started yesterday, and yesterday nobody asked anybody.”

“It is Monday.”

“It is Monday and it is ten past seven in the morning and I read the board out at seven last night, which is why we are having this in the hall and not on the ramp.” She had her hand flat on the page and she was not letting go of it. “If you go at four this afternoon with three signatures you have broken the only promise that puts you on a ramp at all, and you will have broken it in the way that is easiest to argue about, which is on a Monday.”

At the bottom of the page, in pencil, in a nine-year-old's capitals, Petar Lisk had written: *IF THIS PAGE RUNS OUT YOU HAVE TO ASK AGAIN AND THAT IS THE POINT.*

“One o'clock,” Inez said. “Not four. I am not having anybody in this building spend a Tuesday arguing about whether we were allowed to speak to a man on a Monday.”

---

Mara signed at twenty past eight, in her own name, under her own conditions, which were still two.

“No medic on the line. I stay at the top and I stay awake. And nothing gets written today that says a number, a category, or an outcome.” She capped her pen. “And if he offers you something — a route, a name, a way out — you do not say yes in the next ten minutes. You come up the ramp and tell me, and I will tell you no, and you will be permitted to be angry about it.”

Tomas signed at half past eight and did not put the pen down.

“My number goes on the page before anybody goes, and it is zero point four millimeters at the lip, which is the same as Thursday, and if it goes to one I say *enough* and everybody comes up the ramp, including the man on the end of the wire.” He turned the pen over. “And Doss comes as far as the fence and does not go down. There is one rope ticket in this building and one ticket does not make a pair, and today is not an entry, so we do not need a pair. I want that minuted. It is the first survey in eight days that is possible, and it is possible because we are not going in.”

Joon signed at nine and then asked for a different thing to go in the record, out loud, with witnesses.

“I am the man who said on Sunday that the quarter of a second in the sealed node means nothing, and I am still saying it. Nobody goes down that ramp today to look for it in a wire, and if we hear a voice today that is not that voice, I want it written that I said no before we left and not after we came back.”

Inez signed. Four. Petar Lisk did not offer, and said so when Inez held the pen out, because he was not one of the five and he read the board.

---

They walked out at one, because Inez did not negotiate that either: twenty-two meters of Meridian road in ordinary wind, past the fuel line, past the fence with the gap in it that Ru Sabar had stopped complaining about on Saturday for reasons nobody had asked her about.

Doss came as far as the fence with a hand lamp and a thermos, and said out loud that he was not going down, which was the first time in three days that anybody in that building had said a thing like that and been thanked for it.

Emil Baros was standing at the eleven-meter mark with his clipboard under his arm, because he was the keeper and a keeper goes and looks. The bar of soap from the inspection niche was in his coat pocket. It was the only object in the world that had been on the shelf at twelve meters, and it was on Ash Street now, and nobody knew what that was supposed to mean.

“Eleven again,” he said. “Four times this week. It is not moving and it is not getting longer and it is exactly where it was.”

“Good. Write *not moving*. It is a different sentence from *we did not look*.” Emil Baros wrote it down as an observer and not a signature, which he had accepted, along with the job of writing down everything anybody said, which is not the same as being heard.

---

They ran a handset on a cable into the run. The far end of the cable went out of sight at eleven meters, and Elias knelt down and read the card Petar had written for him.

“This is not an extraction and nobody is coming in. On Sunday you said the run is in use by people who are not on any list, and that is still true, and we are not asking you about them. We are here because the run is eleven meters long and it was thirty-one on Saturday, and you use it.”

The line hissed a long time. Then a man's voice came out of the dark end of eleven meters of concrete — a person in a room, heard through a wall, on a bad line.

“You called me a road.”

“I said a run.”

“You said *in use*. That is a different word and you used it on purpose.”

“Yes,” Elias said. “I did.”

---

They told him all of it. Not the version that made it sound like weather, and not the version that made it sound like a debt. Elias found the middle way by accident, by saying the numbers in the order they had been measured: thirty-one point zero on Saturday morning, laser, two passes, a boy on the far end of the tape; thirty-one again that afternoon with nine pallets of block; thirty-one on Monday, walked twice, counted twice, by a man with a clipboard and a witness; eleven point zero on Tuesday from the same datum with the same laser. Continuous concrete, no joint, a paint band cut square, a length of emergency-strip conduit ending in midair with a face on it that had never been weather. No rubble, no fall, no event.

And then the thing underneath it. A hand had been lent to a physician seventeen years ahead to keep one man alive on the first Saturday, and the hand ended when a wheel on a maintenance trolley stopped, and the only stretch of that run that has ever carried anybody in a record is inside the window the hand was open. Nobody can say why. The archivist has written that the file cannot be revised while the field with the name in it is empty. The page upstairs has no cause in it, because a cause we cannot carry is worse than a gap.

The line hissed.

“You want me to say something,” the man said.

“No.”

“You do. You have talked for four minutes and not asked me one thing, and a man does not talk for four minutes for nothing. Ask.”

Elias knelt with his left hand open on the concrete, gone somewhere, and had not said so and there was nobody there to say it for him.

He thought about eleven questions and discarded nine in about four seconds. A count was out — Mara had written it as a prohibition and the man had refused one on Sunday, and a prohibition a physician writes and a promise a man makes are the same shape. Whether anybody was hurt was out too: that was the second thermal reading dressed up as kindness.

*Tell me about the door.*

That one stayed. It was the smallest available and the one he actually wanted, and while he was choosing it he understood that wanting a fact he cannot use yet is not a reason to ask for it.

“When did it change for you,” he said.

“Define change.”

“When it stopped being long enough.”

A man moved a handset from one ear to the other.

“Saturday I could put a loaded cart in at the top and turn it in the dip and come out straight. Not in one push. But I could do it.” A pause. “On Tuesday I could not turn a cart at all. I tried at nine in the morning with a dolly and a half load of rice and I could not get it round the bend at nine meters, so I dragged it back up and carried it in two trips. So I do not know what day it changed. I know what day I found out.”

“Which was Tuesday.”

“Which was Tuesday. I would have found out Monday if I had tried, because on Monday nothing was wrong and I did not try.” A sound that was almost a laugh and did not arrive. “That is the part I want written down in my words and not yours. I did not measure it. Nobody measured me. I just found out.”

“It goes in your words. Joon will type it back and you will hear it before it goes anywhere.”

---

“You are all certain of something,” the man said, “so say it, because you have been careful twice and careful three times is a person with a plan.”

“We are not certain of anything. That is the honest answer and you are entitled to it. We do not know whether it stopped because of the hand. We cannot prove it did and we cannot rule it out. There is a woman nineteen years ahead of us who told us twelve days ago that a named place becomes a place to be closed, and who asked us not to name it, and we refused her once and recorded the refusal, and we are not permitted to ask her anything again.”

“Then she is the one who would know and you cannot ask her.”

“She is, and I cannot ask her,” Elias said, “and that is the real reason four words went up on a wall on Saturday night and nobody acted on them, and I have had a day and a half to get used to that and have not managed it.”

“Fine. That is a person telling the truth at cost, which is more than most. Now tell me what you want, because I have four minutes of line left and I do not want to spend them being polite.”

---

That was what the card was for, and Elias did not use it, and the reason he did not was that every line on it began with the word *could* and had his name on it.

“I don't want anything,” he said.

“No. You do not. And do you know why I am still on this wire? Because you are the first group in sixteen days that said a thing to me and then stopped talking, and the stopping is the whole of the answer. But *I don't want anything* is what people say so the other person feels better about the two minutes they just gave away. So do not do it. Ask.”

“All right.” Elias put his right hand flat on the concrete. “One more thing, and I am not buying it with anything. On the Saturday you came through here — did you come through this run, or the other way?”

The line was quiet.

“Yes,” the man said.

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one I am giving, and you did not earn the other. I want you to notice that I have just handed you a fact worth a great deal to some people, for free, so that you know I can.”

“Understood,” Elias said, which was the only correct thing available, and he meant it, and it settled nothing.

---

“Now tell me what you want,” the man said, “and I am going to say no to most of it, and you will have to do something with the no, and if you do not do something with the no I stop answering, because a man who takes a no and keeps coming back is a man who is shopping.”

Elias put the card away, and the putting away was the whole of the promise, and Petar Lisk, who was nine, watched him do it and wrote the time on the sheet.

“Three things. They are not a request. They are a schedule, and you can say no to all three and I will not come back with a different version.”

“Go on.”

“First. Eleven meters goes on the paper as eleven meters. So does thirty-one. So does the line between them, with the dates, and in the words you just used. Not *the corridor is unstable*. Not *prior extent uncertain*. Thirty-one on Saturday, eleven on Tuesday, and *it did not break, it did not stay* — yours, with the part about the cart.”

“All right.”

“Second. We come down Mondays and Thursdays, four to six, at the loading lip, and the days go on the board with the dates on them, so anybody on your side can know when a line is live without asking us. Not just you. Whoever you are speaking for.”

“Who is four to six.”

“Mondays and Thursdays.”

“Elias. The line is live when I put a handset on it. It is not live when your calendar says so. If I am not here at four on Thursday, your people will come down and stand in a dead corridor for an hour, and the ones who are not on your board will think it is shut. So write the days down if you like. Just do not tell me they are appointments. Tell me they are the days I have said I will be here, and if I am not, nobody has been lied to, because I did not promise.”

Elias got up off his knees, and his left hand stayed where it was.

“Then that is what we will write. The days you have said you will be here.”

“Third.”

“Third is that we do not run up this ramp to tell you the road has changed.”

The handset was quiet long enough that Tomas marked the sheet.

“Nobody has ever offered me that,” the man said. “Sixteen days. Not once, and not from anybody. Say the rest of it.”

“If the length changes it goes on the paper the same day, and the paper is at a hall on Ash Street with the date on it. If you want to come and read it you can. If you do not, the paper is still there with the date on it, and nobody runs up here to knock on a wall and tell you your world got smaller while you were asleep.”

“That is not mercy,” the man said. “I want you to hear me say that, because I have had mercy from people who were storing it. That is *procedure*. And procedure is the only thing anybody has ever handed me that did not have a hook in it. So do not thank me and do not make it a story about you. Put it in the schedule and go away and come back Thursday.”

“Thursday,” the man said, “and if it is not eleven on Thursday you tell me before you measure it, because I will know by the cart.”

---

They walked it back to the fence and Petar read the whole thing out to Joon's sheet in the order Elias had said it, and Elias corrected him twice and let the second correction stand even though Petar's version had been shorter and better.

Doss poured three cups out of the thermos and handed one to Emil Baros, who had written *not moving* on the sheet and signed as an observer, and who would now go home with a bar of soap in his coat pocket, from a shelf at twelve meters in a corridor that no longer has a twelve.

At the fence Ru Sabar was standing with her arms folded in the wind, for reasons she gave as *checking the fence*.

“Two afternoons a week,” she said. “Out of a hall that is the only place in this neighborhood where anything gets decided, for a man who cannot sign anything, does not live here, and will never hold a card.” She started walking. “And when Inez makes you put it in the power column, because she will, you are going to try to give him one.”

Elias did not answer that for about four hundred meters.

---

The board at seven had four new lines and Petar Lisk read all of them, and then the older ones, because that was the job.

**MONDAYS AND THURSDAYS, 1600 TO 1800, THE LOADING LIP. THESE ARE THE DAYS A MAN AT THE OTHER END OF THIS RUN HAS SAID HE WILL BE THERE. THEY ARE NOT APPOINTMENTS. IF HE IS NOT THERE, NOBODY HAS BEEN TOLD ANYTHING.**

**THE LENGTH GOES ON THE PAPER THE SAME DAY IT CHANGES, IN HIS OWN WORDS, INCLUDING THAT HE DID NOT MEASURE IT AND FOUND OUT WITH A CART.**

**NOBODY RUNS UP THE RAMP TO TELL HIM THE WORLD GOT SMALLER.**

And the fourth was not Elias's and not anybody's, and Petar had written it in the margin at half past five because Emil Baros had said it at the fence.

**HE HAS A STOP ON ALL OF IT, LIKE THE REST OF US, AND HE IS NOT ON THE BOARD.**
