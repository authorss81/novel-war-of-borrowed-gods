# Chapter 42 — The Survey

First light on Wednesday was gray and dry and four degrees colder than the day before. Ohno read the chain out loud at the loading lip at ten past five, all four conditions, twice, because Inez had asked for it twice, and the residents' copy was in a plastic wallet Ru Sabar had carried down the ramp herself. Nobody thanked her and she had not expected to be thanked and said so to Anja Ro, twice.

Petar Lisk read the limits before anybody put a hand on the ladder. He was not the observer. He was the person who read the limits, because on Monday he had asked for the job in front of nine people and Elias had said yes before anybody could make it a favor.

**NO MOVEMENT AT THE LIP BEYOND 0.4 MM. THE SURVEY STOPS AT THE STATED DEPTH AND STOPS AT THE STATED TIME, WHICHEVER COMES FIRST, AND NEITHER CAN BE EXTENDED BY ANYBODY ON THIS SITE, INCLUDING THE OFFICE.**

Then he read the other one, in his own hand, the one that had no depth in it.

**WE ARE GOING TO LOOK AT A NUMBER. THE NUMBER HAS BEEN ON A SHEET SINCE THE FOURTH DAY. WE ARE NOT GOING TO DECIDE ANYTHING ABOUT IT BEFORE WE SEE IT.**

Emil Baros was on the lip at ten past five with his own tape and his bar of soap in his coat pocket, and he wrote the reading before anybody asked for it, in the order he had written it for thirteen days.

**ELEVEN METERS. NOT MOVING. TUESDAY ELEVEN, MONDAY ELEVEN, SUNDAY ELEVEN, FRIDAY ELEVEN.**

He gave the copy to Joon and kept the original, which is the arrangement, and which is not in any document anywhere.

The resident observer on the chain was Teodor, and nobody had expected that, and Teodor had not wanted it, and he had it anyway.

“In February I asked for a stop and you gave it to me without asking me anything, and the condition was that I have to be in the building to use it. That made sense on Saturday. It makes more sense today, because the only thing a stop is worth is having somebody in the room, and the room on Wednesday is a hole with two technicians from a regional pool in it, and neither of them has ever met a person in my building.” He read the chain over twice before he signed it. “I am not qualified for this and I want that in the record. I am qualified for one thing, and it is that I will say the word *stop* out loud in a hole in front of a Director's chain and nobody has told me I cannot.”

Ruzicka looked at him for four seconds and said, “Good,” which from a woman who had said nothing else for two days turned out to be the whole review.

---

They went down at twenty past five, and at four and a half meters the gas told them what it was.

That was the thing every technician in the building had noticed by the fourth day and nobody had written down. The records room had a graph of it going back twenty-five days, and it was the flattest line in Havenport. Carbon dioxide at four and a half meters had risen to a level that would put a person asleep in an afternoon and kill them overnight, and it had stopped rising on the eighth day, and it had stayed stopped for seventeen days.

“A room with a person in it does not do that,” Doss said from the lip, into a handset, with the read-back procedure nobody had managed to stop since the second week. “It goes up until it kills him and it keeps going up, because the body is still making it. Seventeen days of flat is not a room. It is a room with something in it taking the old air away and putting new air in, and I have been standing over that graph for three weeks calling it a bad sensor.”

“It is a good sensor,” Elias said. “And I have wanted it to be a bad sensor for three weeks, because a good sensor means somebody is alive down there and a bad sensor means the fourth day was a hot pipe.”

They went to the dip at nine meters on a line, one at a time, and the floor there had a quarter of an inch of standing water that nobody had reported because nobody had gone that far since the second week. Emil Baros's eleven meters was on the far side of it. Nobody measured it. Nobody put a tape to it. The stated depth was nine and a half, and all three of them stopped.

---

Ruzicka put the camera down over the dip at twenty to six and worked it for eleven minutes without saying anything, which is how a good technician works, and which in a corridor with a person on the end of a wire is a thing that makes people want to scream.

Then she said, “I have heat.”

“How much heat,” Elias said.

“There is no such thing as how much heat.” She did not look up. “I have bodies. I am going to tell you what I can tell you and then what I cannot, and I am saying the second part in front of witnesses, because the second part is the one that kills people.”

“Tell me the first part.”

“There is a brick lining across the run at nine-two. Not on any drawing I have. Old lime mortar, not the 2038 pour, somebody built it, floor to ceiling, a meter and a bit thick, and it is warm on the far side.” She turned the camera. “Behind it is a space nine meters long, four wide, two and a half high, ceiling curved. It is not a corridor. It is a chamber, and it is brick-lined, and it is four degrees warmer than this side.”

Doss said into the handset, in a completely flat voice, “Eleven meters is on the other side of the wall we are looking at, and the wall we are looking at is at nine-two, and I would like the recording to say both of those sentences are true at the same time.”

“It is recorded,” Joon said, from the top.

---

They counted for forty minutes, and the counting is the part that goes in the record exactly as it happened, because Ruzicka insisted and because the Office chain said every frame came to the residents' observer before it left the vehicle and because the observer was a man who had bought a crossing with his own name on it.

She swept the chamber nine times. On the third pass she turned the gain down so that the frame showed shape and not brightness.

“Heat sources,” she said. “I am going to give you the number and then the sentence, and the sentence is the part that goes in your log.”

“I did not say eight.” She looked up, and she was not being dramatic, she was being exact, which was worse. “Eight discrete sources. Four are large and roughly the shape of a person lying down. Two are smaller at the far end, and I will not put a name to the smaller ones on my own film, and a smaller one is a child or it is a dog, and I cannot tell you and I am not going to guess in a document.”

“Two children,” Elias said. “Eight sources and I have got six people off them. Where are the other two.”

“One is on the left-hand wall a meter and a half up. A rectangle, about the size of a small stove, and it has been warm for a long time, because you cannot get that temperature out of a brick wall in a sealed chamber in twenty-five days without something in the chamber making it or without air coming in and carrying heat from somewhere else.” She put the camera on a ledge. “And one is a man sitting up against the far end, who is not the same temperature as the other four and has not been for three passes. The man sitting up is the only source in that chamber my camera can see moving.”

Nobody said anything for about a minute and a half.

“Number of persons,” Joon said from the top, in the flat voice he used for a count. “Four confirmed, one probable, one unknown, and a chamber with moving air in it. Is that the whole of it.”

“That is the whole of it and I would like it written in that order,” Ruzicka said, “because *eight* is what the machine says and *six* is what I will sign, and the difference between them is a stove and a man who will not lie still. I have been in this job long enough to know that the difference between the number and the count is where a person goes, and that a stove in a record is a thing that later becomes a person.”

---

Tomas's number held, and holding it cost somebody something, and the cost was Ruzicka's goodwill.

The 2038 pour was on the survey sheet as a feature at thirty-eight centimeters, and the second leg of the survey would have gone down the apron to a meter, and Ohno had the auger out and had a bit in his hand, and the technician who had found the pour eight days ago was the one standing over the hole, and Teodor said the word.

Nobody heard him. Everybody heard the auger go back in the case.

“I have a stop,” Teodor said. “That is all. I do not have a reason and I do not need one, and I am not going to pretend I have one, and the chain says I do not need one.”

Ruzicka put the auger case down herself.

“Then it is stopped,” she said, “and I will say the thing a technician says, which is that the rule is right not because the pour is going to fall. It is right because if I put a bit in there I will find out something, and the moment somebody writes it down the answer changes from *nobody knows* to *somebody said so in a chain with my name at the top*.” She buckled the case. “That rule is worth more than a meter of core, and I have been on the wrong side of it twice.”

---

Sable Arden's second condition came at eight o'clock, the way she had promised it would, which is that it was not a suggestion and it was not hers to lift. Ohno read the clock on the radio at two minutes to eight and nobody argued with him, and the crew came out, and the water on the floor at the dip was a quarter of an inch deeper than it had been when they went in.

“Stated time,” Joon said, into the recording, at eight o'clock in the morning on the twenty-fifth day, “and nobody extended it, including the office, and I want it written that I checked, because in three weeks somebody is going to say the office pushed.”

“They did not push,” Elias said.

“They did not push,” Joon agreed, “and I am going to write *they did not push* in the same hand as the number, next to it, so that the two cannot be separated by a person who wants one of them and not the other.”

---

Mara made the triage decision at ten past ten in the morning, in public, with the graph on the table, because she has never once made one in private and the whole of this month is the argument about that.

“This ledger has a row for twenty-two people and I know what every one of them had on the day they came out of that tunnel. It has no row for a person who has never been out of anything, and I am not going to invent a row at ten past ten in the morning for four people I have not seen.” She put both hands on the table. “And I am not going to write a number of hours. If somebody upstairs writes *four hours* on a piece of paper, then a man dying at five is a man who was late, and I have watched that sentence be said about people I have treated, and I am not going to be the physician whose ledger taught somebody else to say it.”

“So what is the decision,” Elias said.

“Three, and they are mine, and they are about me and not about them, and I am saying them in the order I made them.” She held up one finger. “I will not triage them before I have looked at them. That is a real cost and I am not hiding it, because looking at them means putting a person in a chamber through a wall that has no opening in it, and that is not a medical act, it is a piece of ground, and it belongs to Tomas and to the rope pair and to a woman with a chain.”

“Second. If somebody in there is dying now, then the second we spend deciding about the other three is a second I am spending on a person I have not seen, and I want that written in my name and not in the name of a principle. I am the physician. It is me. Not the plan, not the classification, not the hall. Me.”

“And the third, which cost me the room. Whatever is decided is decided tonight, in this building, by five people who each own a piece of it and by nobody else. If the Office sends a form in the morning I will read it out and I will not sign it, and I will tell you tonight exactly which of those five things I have decided and which of them I have refused, and you can write it down.”

Lena Mir wrote it down, in her own handwriting, on the ward page, at the top, under the day.

The number nobody wanted was four confirmed, one probable, one unknown, and a chamber with air moving in it, and it had been under a street for twenty-five days, and the graph said the air was being replaced, and the seventh person in that arithmetic was the one whose body heat had changed three times in forty minutes and who was sitting up against the far end, nine meters past a brick lining that no drawing in four buildings holds.
