# Chapter 45 — Saturday

Saturday was antibiotics and laundry and one argument, and the argument had two halves, and a nineteen-year-old on a powered frame won the first one and a woman with a movement record won the second, and the second is the reason this building is what it is on Monday.

Lena Mir did the round in forty-nine minutes and wrote it on one page in plain sentences, and the page is the only clinical document in that building anybody can read without a key. Oren Pike walked to the end of the hall and back and was not winded and told two people about it. Anja Ro did the same carrying a full water can. Ruta Amsel had the brace off for the ninth day. Tarin Voss asked, once, whether anybody had put his name back, and was told no, and said good, and went back to reading.

Comfort Osei was at the clinic, on a bed, and not on a frame, and the reason was the two hours a night. Inez told Osei Kwarteng herself on Saturday morning at nine, standing up, the way she has learned to deliver things in this hall.

“The east ward is dark from two until four, and I am not moving them while there is a hole in a wall that somebody in this building may be carried through. Your wife is on a bed at the clinic for six days because I would not put her on a frame that goes off for two hours every night. That is the whole of my reasoning and it is not a good one, and I would like you to be angry at me rather than at her.”

“I am not going to be angry at you,” Osei said. “I gave it away too.”

“You did,” Inez said, “and I have been carrying that sentence around for eight days like a stone, and you are the first person who has let me put it down.”

---

The argument started at eleven and it was not about power and it was not about the plan and it was not about the wall.

It started with a stranger.

On the operating plan on the chalkboard, drawn in Emil Baros's hand and traced so the residents could add to it, there was a route. It came out of the mouth of the hole at the loading lip, went down the ramp, came along the front of the hall, and went into the ward, and the ward is where nineteen of the twenty-two sleep.

“There is a corridor,” Niko Senn said, from the table by the window, where he had been sitting for four days since he came back from the east bank. “There is a dry end and there is a corridor and there is a laundry and there are eleven meters of empty floor between this door and the front of that room, and the plan goes through the room.”

“It goes through the room because the room has a hard floor, a door that opens outward, and a nurse in it,” Elias said.

“It goes through the room because the room is the room people are in,” Niko said. “I am not arguing about the floor.”

Ansel Duarte turned his head on the frame. He had been awake since five and he had been listening since about nine, and everybody knew that and nobody said it, and when he spoke the room stopped the way it always stopped.

“No,” he said.

It was a small word and it did not have a reason attached, and everybody in the room waited for the reason, and Ansel had told them on the day they gave him the power that he did not owe anybody one.

Then he gave one anyway, because he wanted to, and he said it slowly.

“On the second day I was in a room on a mat and a man with a clipboard told me there was a bed at the end of a list,” he said. “And the thing that made that room bearable was that I could see the door. Not the door out. The door in. I could see the shape of anybody who came in and decide whether it was a person or a thing. That is the entire difference between a room and a corridor.” He looked at the board. “You are going to carry somebody past nineteen people at two in the morning, and every one of them is going to wake up, and they are not going to know whether it is a man being carried out or a man being brought in, and that is a promise about a place. And I have been on the end of that promise.”

“It is eleven meters and four minutes a person,” Elias said.

“Then it is eleven meters and four minutes a person,” Ansel said, “and it is the cheapest thing anybody has asked me for this month. Take the corridor.”

Nobody argued with him, and then Inez argued with him, and she was right about something else.

“The corridor is the laundry,” she said, “and the laundry has a floor drain in it, and I am not running a carrying line over a drain. And the eleven meters means a door one and a quarter wide and four minutes a person, and the four minutes is me or Ru or Anja on a wet floor holding a light.”

“Then hold the light on a wet floor,” Ansel said.

“Yes,” Inez said. “All right. Yes.”

---

The part that came after was the part that Petar Lisk read out on Saturday night, and it is the reason this building is what it is on Monday.

Nadi Bell had been sitting at the back with the movement record open in front of her for an hour, and she had a pencil and had not written anything, and at half past three she stood up and asked the twenty-two a question, and it was not a good question and it was the right one, and she asked it herself because Joon is the only person authorised to ask her anything and she is not using him.

“How long do you think you are going to be here,” she said.

Nobody answered for about a minute and a half.

“Some of you are getting better and some of you are not, and both of those are true and neither is the question. The question is what this building is. A hall where people are getting better and a ward where people are brought in are two different buildings, and they cannot both be this floor. This Saturday is the day that gets decided, because on Sunday morning somebody is going to come through that door who has never seen it.”

She put her hand on the record.

“If this is a place people are brought to, then it is a ward, and a ward has a job, and a job is done by the people in it, and that means the nineteen of you are not residents of a shelter any more. You are the people who run it. And the day I write that word down, Ruta Amsel, who has had the brace off for nine days and has nothing to do with her hands all day every day, becomes a person with a job, and Ansel, who cannot turn over, is a person with a job, and Niko Senn, who went to his sister on a bus and came back, is a person with a job.” She looked around the room. “And that is better and I am telling you it is better, and I am telling you what it costs, because you have all watched me do this for three weeks and I do not do it for free.”

Somebody at the back said, “Say what it costs.”

“It costs the twenty-two and it costs the number,” Nadi said. “On Monday the Office gets a return that says *this is a ward, maintained by the residents, twenty-two of them, with a name against each function*, and that is a return the Office has never received from anywhere, and it will not reconcile with a count, and there is a woman on the Meridian who is going to look at it and write a paragraph, and the paragraph is going to be reasonable.”

“Write it yourself first,” Elias said, “in our file, in the shape she will write it. The only thing worse than her writing it is her writing it first.”

Nadi Bell looked at him for a long moment.

“That is the first useful thing you have said to me since the corridor,” she said. “All right. If she ever asks for it she will find that somebody in this hall did her work and did it worse on purpose.”

---

Emil Baros came down the ramp at ten past five, the way he had every evening for thirteen days, and stood at the end of the front room where the light was and did not write the line.

The others noticed before anybody said it. Anja Ro stopped folding. Petar Lisk put his pencil down.

“You have written *keeper present, nothing changed since Monday* on that sheet thirteen times,” Inez said, “and you have never once been on the movement record, because you are not one of the twenty-two, and you are not a visitor, because a visitor came and can leave. I do not have a word for what you are and I have had a month to find one.”

“You have,” Emil Baros said. “You have got *keeper*. You put it on the wall in the second week because I said it at a fence and nobody had asked me anything, and you wrote it up because it was true.” He turned the sheet over and put it on the table, blank side up. “Now this building is going to be a ward, and a ward has people who run it, and every one of them is going to be on a piece of paper with a name against a function, and I am going to be on a piece of paper with a word against me that I chose about myself on a Tuesday while standing in a corridor that is now shorter than it was.”

He did not raise his voice and did not need to.

“Thirteen days I have come down that ramp,” he said. “I have a room on Ferry Street and a wife and a bar of soap and I have never once been asked to stay and I have never once asked to, and I am not asking to now, because asking to would be a different thing.” He put his hand flat on his own blank sheet. “I want to know what I am going to be, in writing, on this paper, next to whatever the twenty-two are, and I want somebody else to have to say yes to it out loud, in this room, in front of nineteen people who are going to be a staff.”

The room was quiet in the way it had been quiet about nine times in twenty-five days.

Inez Sato said, “Yes,” and then made the room say it, one at a time, because she had learned in three weeks that a thing granted in a room where only two people are listening is a favor, and her whole life is a fight against favors.

Ru Sabar said yes. Anja Ro said yes. Niko Senn said yes. Tarin Voss said yes without looking up from his book and Petar Lisk wrote that down as it was said and did not add anything to it.

Lena Mir said yes, and then said, “One more thing, and I am putting it in the record before anybody stops me. The keeper's line is eleven meters from this building and nobody in this room can walk it any more. The next line in that column has to be somebody who can walk it, and thirteen days at the end of a corridor is not the same as being at the end of it, and if anybody in this room thinks it is, they can say so, and I will still say no.”
