# Chapter 41 — Tuesday, the Day After

The hall did not know it was the last week of anything. It knew the day because Lena Mir wrote it at the top of the ward page, and it knew the dark hours were coming at two because Petar Lisk put the lamp by the door at one forty-five and sat down beside it to wait, which he had done every night for a fortnight and which nobody had ever asked him to stop doing.

Elias came in at ten past eight, a day after a refusal. The refusal was four lines and a date, and it had survived a night, and a document which has survived one night has crossed the worst part of its life. The question was what happened on Wednesday morning, and it had gotten louder overnight, which is how a real question behaves.

Nobody started it. Lena Mir did, with a clipboard, at nine.

“Four people on antibiotics, two of them due a second dose today, and the round is fifty-one minutes if nobody sits down. I am saying it in this room and not in the corridor because on Wednesday three people are going to be somewhere under a street, and the physician who decides what happens to these four will be holding a pen at the top of a rope. I would like a sentence in the plan about who does what with me while you are gone.”

“The medical decisions do not stop,” Mara said. “They get done by the person who is not writing your plan down.”

“Then that is the sentence,” Lena said, “in my handwriting, not yours, because if it goes wrong the person the residents ask is not going to be you.”

---

There were three fronts, and Petar had all three of them on the board by nine, because he had started writing down the state of the world and nobody had asked him to and Inez had let him. The first was the ward: four carded draws a day, one feeder the residents can cut, and two hours in the middle of every night that the building is dark, because a circuit that is on at two is a circuit somebody on this street is paying for in a way they did not agree to. The clinic's generator margin came back Monday, paid for, and Comfort Osei's frame had been on since Sunday afternoon, and nobody said so in front of Osei, who was in the room.

The second was a clock. A provisional classification may not stand ninety days without either a seal or a finding, and Sable Arden wrote that on a Tuesday at nine at night, and Petar had copied the date off her letterhead, and now he wrote it on the board every morning without being asked.

**EIGHTY-THREE DAYS. THE NUMBER IS FROM THE SEVENTEENTH.**

The third was Calder Street, and Inez put the routing log on the table at nine twenty without sitting down.

“Five events since the license. One subject Tuesday night, three on Wednesday, two on Saturday, one on Sunday, two on Monday. Consent on file, witness on file, return on file, every one, in my own words, and no observer has attended any of them, because the clause says *may*, and this is the eighth day.”

“Nobody came,” Doss said.

“Nobody came. And the room is still running and it is fuller than it was on Thursday, and I know that from a woman who sweeps the shop next to it, and it is on this sheet as hearsay and I am not going to pretend it is not hearsay.” She looked up. “One sentence. We are about to spend Wednesday on the reading at nine meters. The thing that costs this street the most is not the Office and is not the crack in the slab. It is that a man is selling a warm room to frightened people for ninety dollars a night, and it is cheaper and safer than this building, and both of those are still true.”

Nobody argued, because it was already in her chalk from Thursday.

---

Doss did the arithmetic at half past nine, standing up, because Doss always did it standing up.

“I need two names on a piece of paper for Wednesday,” Doss said. “Not a promise. Names.”

Elias wrote to Sable at ten past ten on the hall's own paper, dated it, and read it back to Joon before it went.

*Two certified confined-space technicians from the regional pool, named, for Wednesday morning, for a thermal survey of the lower service run to the stated depth and no further. Office instruments. The hall's resident observer. The hall's written limit and the Office chain.*

The answer came at ten past four, in nine lines.

*Ruzicka and Ohno are assigned. Wednesday, first light, the same vehicle, the same chain number, three copies, the residents' copy travelling with the observer. Four conditions. One: no movement at the loading lip beyond 0.4 mm for the duration. Two: the survey stops at the stated depth and stops at the stated time, whichever comes first, and neither can be extended by anybody on this site including this office. Three: every frame the instruments produce is delivered to the residents' observer before it leaves the vehicle. Four: the Office pays for the instruments' power.*

Nobody argued about the first three.

“The second one is the important one,” Inez said, “and I am going to say so, because I have read four hundred chains and I have never seen a time limit that the party writing the chain could not lift. She has lifted hers in advance, in writing.” She turned to Petar. “Write that in his words and do not let anybody shorten it.”

Then she turned the chain over.

“Now my column. One term. It is not a favor, and it is going to cost somebody a thing they rely on.”

---

“Instrument power is a residents' carded draw,” Inez said. “Not the clinic generator. Not the hall feeder. A card, on the board, with a household's name on it, and that household can cut it tonight with no notice to us, and the instruments go dark and the survey stops, and that goes in the chain. If anybody here objects to the instruments stopping, the answer is that they are ours for four hours on Wednesday morning and they are not ours on Wednesday night.”

“Whose card,” Ru Sabar said, from the back.

There was a pause of about two seconds, and Elias understood in it that Inez had known the answer before she asked, and had asked anyway, in a room, on the record, which is the only way this hall does anything.

“Mine is not available,” Inez said. “And that is not modesty. I withdrew it on the day I had to give your card back to you, and a withdrawal that can be resumed was not a withdrawal, and you said a sentence about that in front of fourteen people, and you were right.”

“Then whose,” Ru Sabar said.

“Two households on Bell Row hold a card for a lamp and a pump for a medical cooler, for a man whose wife does dialysis at home because the clinic has no room. So it is one of those two, or the machine shop.” She did not look at anybody. “The machine shop card is in the envelope. The machine shop is yours. The card is still your card.”

The room went very quiet.

“I am not asking you to take it back,” Ru Sabar said. “I said I would not ask again, and I meant it, and I would like everybody in here to notice that nobody asked me.”

“Nobody asked you,” Inez said. “I am going to ask you on Thursday at seven, after the survey, and give you the reason then and not now. And if you say no, the instruments run on a Bell Row cooler, and I will explain that to those two households myself, tonight, at their doors.” She put both hands flat on the chain. “That is the cost and it is mine, and I am naming it out loud in advance so nobody can tell me afterwards that I did not know, and because this hall has spent a month finding out that the things that cost something are the ones somebody wrote down first.”

---

Mara brought the medical standard to the table unread.

“The shape of it and not the words. What the crew may carry, when somebody is stopped, who holds the stop, at what hour the crew comes out. It does not say what happens if the survey finds a person, or what category they would be, or how long they have. It does not have a contingency section, and I have written *no contingency* across it in my own hand so a clerk in a hurry does not help me.”

“Elias,” Lena said. “She has done the thing where nobody said a number out loud and it is going to be in a form for the Office.”

“I know what she has done. I want it in a different place, that is all. That page tells a man in a corridor that the answer to the question he is about to ask was considered in advance and found not to be a medical matter. That is a sentence about a man he has never met, written by somebody who has never met him.”

“Then who writes it,” Mara said.

“You do. That is not a criticism. You are the one person here allowed to say the sentence to his face in a corridor in nine seconds, and I want that to be your sentence and not a page.”

“All right,” Mara said, and then immediately, in the voice she used for a thing already decided: “And no. The standard does not contain a predicted outcome, and neither does any conversation I have had about this survey. If a person under that apron is a person or is not a person, that is a thing you learn by looking at them. What I have written gives you the hour afterwards, not the hour before.”

---

Tomas came in at half past six with the survey sheet, and the sheet was wrong in a way that took eleven minutes to explain.

“They have put the 2038 pour on it,” he said, “as a *feature*. A technician is going to stand over a hole with a camera and look at a hairline crack that has been in a photograph for eight days. I am not accusing Ruzicka of anything. She put down what the last survey found. That is what a survey does, and that is the whole trouble with surveys.”

“What do you want.”

“The number in my hand above hers, in a place a camera cannot point at, and somebody who is not me to read it out before anybody leaves the building in the morning.” He put the pencil down. “And I want it said in a room that the reason is not that I think the pour will fall. It might never fall. A stamp in the ground under a street is a question, and a question under your feet with nobody in a position to say *we do not know* is how a thing becomes an answer in somebody else's office.”

Petar Lisk got up off the bench and read the limit out, because Elias had asked him on Monday to write down what a survey is for before anybody wrote down what it might find.

**WE ARE GOING TO LOOK AT A NUMBER. THE NUMBER HAS BEEN ON A SHEET SINCE THE FOURTH DAY. WE ARE NOT GOING TO DECIDE ANYTHING ABOUT IT BEFORE WE SEE IT.**

“That is the whole limit,” Petar said. “I know it has no depth in it. Tomas has the depth. I have that one.”

“It is a good limit,” Tomas said, and meant it. And Elias Rook, standing in a hall that had been running for a month with a left hand that had been numb for three weeks, understood that a nine-year-old's sentence covered him and did not cover Tomas, and said so out loud, which took him longer than it should have.

---

The board at seven had six new lines, and the seventh was the one that started the week, and Joon wrote it because it was his finding and Petar wrote what Petar found and nobody was going to blend them.

At half past ten that night, with the terminal on the records table and nothing on it, Joon Park said the thing nobody had said in twenty-five days.

“I have been back over every form this site has produced since the second day,” he said, “and there is not one of them with a number in it about a person who has never been in this building. We have twenty-two, and it is wrong, and it moves because people get better. We have nine credentials and no names. We have five with a stamp on the other side of them and a shop that does not exist. We have a form with a box for a number and no box for a street.” He put his hand flat on the terminal. “On Wednesday morning this survey is going to put a number about people we have never met into a document. And whoever has the number owns the sentence that comes after it, and that sentence is the only one anybody in four buildings is ever going to read.”

“So say the question,” Inez said.

Joon did not soften it.

“What is the survey for,” he said. “Is it for them, or is it for the file. Those are not the same, they want opposite things, and the second one is a document. I have sat here four days waiting for somebody to say which one it is, and nobody has, and I have checked, and I am not going to check again. So I am going to say the last part out loud, because that part is a fact.”

He read it off his own hand.

“**The first person in this building to say out loud that a document is for the file, and not for the people in it, was Petar Lisk, on the seventh night, when he wrote *we are twenty-two, we are not a number*, and he wrote it about people he lives with.**”

Petar was nine years old and was at home, and his line stayed on the board.

The thermal survey was at first light on Wednesday, the twenty-fifth day, at a stated depth, with a limit written before anybody went.
