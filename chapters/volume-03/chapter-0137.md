# Chapter 137 — A Lamp On A Table Gets Voted On

Cliff Marner was ninety days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and twenty days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and twenty days in, and Elias Rook was in the front room on Ash Street at ten past ten on Sunday night, the fourth of January, because the yard was shut, the counter was shut, the board was shut and the hearing room was shut, and the only one of those four that had a piece of paper on it was the yard, and the yard's piece of paper said the twenty-fifth to the twenty-eighth of December and was the only notice in the district that was out of date, and nobody in that building had any way of finding out that it was.

Petar Lisk read the board at seven in the morning, out loud twice, and the second half of the oldest question was still a bracket with nothing under it, and the nineteenth of November was still the only date on that wall, and there was no room on it, and there was not going to be a second wall.

There was a lamp on the table in the front room.

---

It had been on the table since Saturday afternoon. It was a lamp, a big one, a rechargeable one with a handle, the sort of thing a works issues and then loses, and it had been sitting on the shelf in the hall where the shelf of things people bring back lives since the week of the collapse, and the last person who had picked it up was a boy of nine in September who wanted to know whether it worked.

Inez Sato had put it on the table because the light over the stairs had been out since Friday evening, and because a torch is a thing that runs out at four in the morning in front of people who are trying to get to a bathroom, and because the building has a float and the float had about twenty pounds in it in the world on Saturday afternoon.

"So it is on the table," she said, "and therefore it is a vote, and I am not going to pretend that a thing on a table in this room is not a vote, because that is exactly how a hall stops being a hall and starts being a corridor with a kettle in it."

"You said in December that a rule with two people in it is worth two people," Elias Rook said, "and you have two refusals that have been current for thirteen weeks."

"I have," Inez Sato said, "and neither of them has anything to do with a lamp, and I am going to say them so that nobody in this room gets clever. One: this building does not get itself together about a form. Two: there is no meeting, no form, no line on a pad and no vote about the thing we found in the autumn and I am not having one, and if it turns up on a pad in January I will pull the pad off the nail myself."

"And a lamp."

"A lamp is not the thing we found in the autumn and a lamp is not a document about anybody's address," Inez Sato said, "and if it were, I would not put it on that table either. A lamp is a lamp. I would like it minuted that I said the word document and then said no anyway, because that is how you catch yourself."

The vote was at half past ten on the Sunday night, in the front room, out loud, and it was nine for and four against and one abstention and it passed, and it cost eleven pounds and fifty of the float, and the two against it said it should come out of the caretaker's money and the one who abstained said she had not been in the building since Thursday and did not know what she was voting on, and nobody put a name to the caretaker and nobody went to a shop until Monday.

Petar Lisk put the resolution in the book on the page after the twenty-second of November, and the four minutes were on the page above it, and neither of them went on the board, and the board had no room.

---

Petar asked him about the lamp in his coat at about eleven, in the corridor, in the way a nine-year-old asks a question in a corridor and then waits.

"You have got one," Petar Lisk said. "In your coat. It is the second one and the other one is on the table and it cost eleven pounds and fifty, and I voted for it and I would like to know why the one in your coat is not on the table."

"Because it is mine," Elias Rook said. "It cost a pound and something in a shop on Christmas Eve and the batteries in it cost a pound and ten, and it runs on two batteries that a man in a shop sold me and not off anything this building voted on."

"That is not a reason," Petar Lisk said. "I asked you a reason and you gave me a shop."

"I gave you the reason," Elias Rook said. "A lamp on that table is the building's by Wednesday. Then it is on a list, and then somebody wants to know why there are two, and then one of them needs batteries out of a float, and then in a month somebody is going to say the building has a lamp because a man from another district brought one, and this building has been very careful for thirteen weeks not to be a thing that has a man's district in it."

Petar Lisk thought about that for longer than a nine-year-old is supposed to.

"You are frightened of the vote," he said.

"I am frightened of winning it," Elias Rook said.

Petar Lisk went back to the front room and told somebody, and about ten minutes later Elias Rook heard his own arrangement come back at him in Petar's voice, slightly wrong, with an extra sentence in it about the ninth of November, and he did not correct it, because a nine-year-old had taken it apart in a corridor and had it nearly right and had been right about the only part of it that mattered, and that was worth more than the arrangement, and he was not going to be the one to say so.

---

Joon Park came in at about half past eleven and put the custody schedule in his coat and did not take it out, because it was twenty-two items and it was going to be twenty-two items on Wednesday.

"I am not going to ask you what you did on Thursday morning before that street shut," he said.

"I did not know you knew there was anything about Thursday morning."

"I did not," Joon Park said, "and I am not going to ask, and you are not going to tell me, and I am going to carry on behaving as though that is a normal thing for two men in a building to do on a Sunday night. I have thought about that for two days and I have decided that if I ask you, then you will tell me, because you have never once been able to leave a thing alone, and then I will know something about a morning that a man in a regional office has not been told and a foreman has not been told, and I will be the fourth person in the world holding it, and the fourth person is the one who eventually says it out loud in a corridor to somebody who is about to do something." He took his coat off. "So I am not asking, and the price of that is that you get to keep it, and I want you to notice that I have charged you nothing for it and that is not generosity, it is me not wanting to be the fourth person."

Petar Lisk, from the table, said, "What is the sixteenth of January."

"It is the far end," Joon Park said. "You have asked me in a room, so I will tell you once, and it is the last time I am going to say it to anybody in this building unless somebody asks me again, and if somebody asks me again I am going to say the second of January is the near end and stop, and that is also the last time."

---

The box of working logs was on the top shelf of the records room with the 1998 one at the bottom of it, and it was Monday before Christmas that it had come down and come back up, and it had been up since the Sunday morning after Boxing Day, and it was not going to come down again in January for the reason it had nearly come down on Boxing Day, which was that a man had come home and looked interested.

Petar Lisk had two lists. He had them in the back of a squared pad and he had never shown them to anybody and he had told Inez Sato about them once in November and she had written on the front of the one that mattered, *not in the custody schedule*, and she had been right about that.

He came down and opened the pad on the landing.

"There is a name on the second list," he said. "It has been on there since 2029 and it is in my mother's hand and it is not anybody in this building, and I have never met him, and I am not going to tell you who he is because you would write it down."

"Why not."

"Because the list is the list of people who have a reason to open it and the reason is in the pad and the name is on the list, and if you know the name you will want to know the reason, and if you know the reason you will want to open it," Petar Lisk said. "That is how a box gets opened. Not by a person who decided. By a person who found out."

Elias Rook did not go up, which was the whole of what he had come to say, and he said it in about nine words, and Petar Lisk wrote the date on the front of the second list and closed the pad and went to bed.

---

At ten past midnight the four dark hours came. He went up with the notebook in his coat and the lamp in the other pocket and the envelope to Nell in the front of the notebook, unsent, the reason four words long, and the wall had three questions on it and nowhere to put a fourth, and a book on a shelf had four minutes in it and a vote in it and the name of a man he had never met on a list that was not in the custody schedule, and in about five hours he was going to get the 11 and go to a district where nine kitchens lived behind five door numbers on a street that a foreman had put in a notice without asking anybody, and he had four men and twenty-one lamps and a sheet with four ruled lines on it and not one of them signed yet, and he had been on a stair in the dark once in his life and a lamp in his coat that was a lamp and not a man, and he was going to be on a road at seven o'clock in the evening with a woman's stair and a foreman's forty minutes in his head and nothing else in his hands, and there was no part of that which anybody could put on a table.
