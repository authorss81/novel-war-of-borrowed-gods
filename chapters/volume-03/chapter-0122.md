# Chapter 122 — The Rule Is Four Minutes And It Is Not A Courtesy

Cliff Marner was seventy-five days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and five days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and five days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Saturday morning, the twentieth of December, and the cell's public counter above the shop at number six was shut the way it is on every Saturday, and the third floor at Civic buildings was shut, and the yard was open, because the yard runs six days.

He rode in on a bus with a woman and three shopping bags and no other passengers who had anything to do in that district. The shutter on the newsagent at number two was up and there was a queue of nine, and the woman in the vest was folding a table into a van, and the van said the program on the side of it in one color of ink the way everything in this program was in one color of ink.

At the corner of Cutbank Row and the yard approach there were about a dozen people standing round a bollard in coats, and Winifred Alagoa was reading her sheet out loud to them, the way she had done every Saturday since 2031, street by street, in her own hand, at her own pace, and nobody thought it was remarkable.

He did not stop. He had a thing to do at nine o'clock that night and it was the only thing in his head and he walked past a woman reading a thousand doors to her own street because he had something to do, and that was the whole of the kind of man he was at that moment and he did not see it and would not have admitted it.

At the far corner she got to Sallow Court, which she does last and has done since 2031 and has never once said why, and about eight of the dozen people stopped listening and two of them did not.

"Three to twenty-seven," she said. "Thirty-nine doors."

That was her number and not the board's. The board's printed list for the same block said forty-one households, and the difference of two had been on her kitchen table since June and she had done nothing about it because there was nobody to tell and she was not going to start now and be a woman with a grievance at sixty-three. A man had asked her about it once, in a kitchen, in December, and she had told him the answer to the question of who was right and not the answer to the question of what the two of them were.

Elias Rook went on to the bus stop at the top of the hill with the folder under his right arm and the tube against his leg, and what he took away from the corner of Cutbank Row was that a woman of sixty-three had said a number out loud in the street and a dozen people had heard it and not one of them had thought there was anything remarkable about it, and that in three weeks he had not once written a number out loud in a street.

---

He was on the eleven at half past four and on the far bank at ten to five and on Ash Street at twenty to six, and he did the shop's milk and the hall's bread because it was his turn, and he carried both bags in with his right arm against his chest and let the left hang.

At nine o'clock he telephoned Mara Okafor in the corridor with the door that does not close properly. She was in the city and not in the district, because it was a Saturday and her week in Tidegate was Monday to Thursday, and that had been the arrangement for six weeks and neither of them had ever once moved it.

"I am going to ask for something on Tuesday," he said. "I am not asking you for it. I am telling you that I am going to ask, and I am going to tell you who, because you will want to know, and then I am going to stop talking and wait four minutes and if you have not said yes in four minutes I will not ask at all."

"Say the whole of it back to me," Mara said.

"You do not get the ask. You get the asking. And you get the name."

"The name."

"Mara Okafor," Elias Rook said. "Twenty-one years ahead. The hands. Not your hands, and not you, and not me — I want to be exact, because I have been imprecise about this for two days and Joon Park has been listening to me be imprecise about it. It is a woman who has never met me and I am going to put a question to her about eleven people I have never seen."

The line did what lines do.

"Four minutes," Mara said. "Say the other half."

"If you say nothing for four minutes, that is a no, and I do not ask. If you say no in four minutes, that is a no, and I do not ask. The only thing that lets me ask is you saying yes out loud inside four minutes, and a yes that takes five minutes is a no with a bad memory."

"That is a rule and not a courtesy," Mara said. "It is right. You have it wrong twice and I am going to tell you where both times were. The first time you rehearsed it on me. You asked me the question in the middle of the four minutes, so that whatever I said was an answer to a question you had already moved to. The second time you made the four minutes a thing I had to fill, and a thing somebody has to fill is not a no."

Elias Rook stood in a corridor on Ash Street with a telephone against his right ear and the left hand hanging at his side doing nothing, and could not think of a single thing to say, and did not say one for about nine seconds.

"That is step four," he said at last. "I have been doing it for three months and I have not understood it once."

"You have understood it," Mara said. "You have been doing it to rooms instead of to people."

"I know."

"Then do this one to me and do it on the telephone and not in a room, because I am not going to be a person you say it to in front of twenty-eight people so that they can see you did it. You tell me on Tuesday. I will not say yes and I will not say no on Saturday night from the far side of the city, because that is not a decision, that is a forecast. And on Tuesday you tell me, and you stop, and you count."

"And if you say nothing," he said.

"Then you come home on the eleven and you do not ask," Mara said. "And you do not decide that my silence was permission, because you have been doing that since the fourth of December and you do it so kindly that nobody catches it."

There was a heater on the landing that gave up about every nine minutes, and four people in that building had learned not to speak across it, and Elias Rook counted the nine seconds in his head and said nothing at all, on purpose, because there was a woman on the other end of that wire who had just told him what silence was for.

---

Petar Lisk was in the front room because Petar Lisk was in the front room on Saturday nights with a book and a pencil, and there were nine people in it, and Cliff Marner was in the chair by the window with a blanket out of the store room, and Inez Sato was at the table with the lock-box and the power budget and a consent card she did not need tonight.

Elias Rook told them what he had told Mara Okafor, in the words it had been said in, before he acted on it.

Petar put it in the book, on the page after the twenty-second of November, with the date at the top, and he did not go through the room asking anybody whether it was the right thing, because a man who asks a room that question has asked it in order to be told.

"You have got it wrong twice and right once," he said, from the table. "I heard you on Saturday night through that door and I am the wall's man and I am telling you that the third one is the one that counts and that a rule which has been said out loud three times in a corridor is a rule that has been tested and not one that has been written down."

"Does that go on the board?" Petar Lisk said, with the pencil not capped.

"No," Elias Rook said.

"Good," Petar said. "There is no room and I am not putting a Saturday on a wall that is finished. Whoever comes in here after me is going to think somebody was tidy, and then somebody is going to take a piece of chalk and tidy the next thing, and that is how a wall starts lying."

Nobody said anything about that, and Inez Sato said something else instead, which was the price of it and not a speech.

"You have now made a rule with two people in it and the hall is not in it," she said. "Two people is a small enough number of people for a rule this sharp. Do not take it up to the yard. Do not take it up to the clinic. Do not take it into a room with a form on the table, because in a room with a form on the table a rule with two people in it becomes a rule with everybody in it, and everybody in it is how it stops being a rule and starts being a policy."

"That is your standing refusal," Elias Rook said.

"It is both of them and you have not asked me which," Inez said. "Go to bed. You have got a Tuesday and it is a large one and I am not going to be part of it."

---

He went up at eleven and he did not sleep much, and at about two in the morning he got the notebook out with his right hand and turned to the inside back cover, where two dates were in his own handwriting, the second of January and the sixteenth of January, and under them he wrote the four-minute rule out once so that he would have it in front of him on Tuesday and not have to remember it.

Then he tried to write what he was going to say to the woman who had not met him, and he got as far as the second line of it and stopped, and put the notebook back in his coat, and lay in the dark listening to somebody three floors down walk to a bathroom and back.
