# Chapter 105 — Nineteen Cutbank Row

The house at number nineteen on Cutbank Row is two rooms and a landing and a boiler that has to be lit before anything works, and the woman who lives in it came to the door at about eleven on Wednesday morning, the third of December, the eighty-eighth day, and said, "Shut that behind you, the landing's cold, there's a draught comes down it that the landlord's been not fixing since before he died."

The landlord died in 2038. She told him that on the step, before he had said anything, and he understood that she was not making conversation.

---

Cliff Marner was fifty-eight days out of the ground. Della Marner was eighty-eight days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was eighty-eight days in. Elias Rook had got the 11 at a quarter to nine and had read the same page of the same folder on the bus, and the folder was a program reference and four columns and a footnote in italics, and the four columns were address, structure, occupancy class, and status.

**Her address was on it. It said `PARTIAL` in the third column and `TRANSITIONAL` in the fourth.**

That was correct, and it was the thing he could not get past for the whole of that day: the two columns were right, and she was in a two-room flat, and the classification of a two-room flat is `PARTIAL`, and nobody had to decide anything for a seventy-one-year-old woman to be absent from a document about her own address.

Her name was Ezinne Abioye. She was seventy-one. She had been at number nineteen since 1998, and she let him in with the chain still on and took the chain off when he had got his one hand out of his coat, which he thought was decent of her.

"Sit down. Not that chair, that chair is for the table."

The kitchen was half of the flat. There was a table with a cloth, a gas ring with a kettle, a window over a yard, and a chair with a blanket over the back of it. On the wall there were two photographs, a calendar from a chemist that was still on the month it was given out, and a card of a saint with a nail through the top of it.

"You will want to know why I am not angry," she said, filling the kettle.
"Yes."

"And I am not going to tell you, because if I tell you that then you will write it down and it will be the reason for a thing, and I am not a reason for a thing." She set the kettle on the ring. "I am going to tell you what I have worked out, and then you can go away and do what you are going to do."

---

She had worked it out herself, on her own, with no document, and she had worked it out before any of it.

"They came with the rates," she said. "A card with a program on it and a number on the card and a line that said my address was not in the retained district, and I read it about four times and I did not understand it, and then I understood it at about the fourth read because I have been a person at an address for fifty-five years and I know exactly what that sentence is.

"They are not putting me anywhere. They are putting my address somewhere, and I am at the address."

She said it once and it was the whole of it and she did not say it again for four hours.

"Do you understand what I am telling you," she said. "Because I do not think you do, and I think you have come here to be upset on my behalf and I have had that from three people since September and I have been very polite about it and I would rather not do it again.

"They have not put me anywhere. That is the thing people get wrong. Nobody has come and said *you are going*. There is nowhere to be *going* to, there is a wall going up at the end of the road in about eleven months, and the water that comes up this street in a bad year is the river backing up and not the sea, and I have seen the river back up in this street in 2031 and it is not the end of the world. So they are taking a piece of ground to build a thing against water, and my two rooms are on the piece of ground, and my two rooms are `PARTIAL`, and `PARTIAL` is a word for a building, and it is correct, and it is the only word in that card that anybody wrote down by accident."

"Then what do you do about it," Elias Rook said.

"Nothing," she said. "**I am not a household and I have not been a household since 1998 and there is nothing on any paper in this city that says I am one, and I would like that noticed, because I worked it out before September and I have been living on it for forty-four years.**"

---

He had a folder in his hand and he did not open it.

There is a moment in a thing like this where a man with a folder opens the folder. He came eleven minutes on a bus with a folder, and the woman in front of him had told him a thing he could not argue with, and the folder was the only thing in the room that was on his side, and the folder was the program's own document, and opening it would have been the whole of his purpose in the room.

He put it on the chair behind him instead.

He had been four seconds from opening it. He noticed himself four seconds too late, and then he sat there for the rest of the afternoon and was no use at all.

"What do you do, then," she said, at about half past eleven. "You have not said. You have come five days running and you have a folder and one hand and you have not told me what your job is and I have been waiting since you sat down."

"I am a rescue service field technician. I am on suspension from one kind of work and I am not on suspension from the rest of it. I am in a building on Ash Street that was a refuge after a tunnel came in on the sixth of September, and I have been there twelve weeks, and I have three endorsements that were taken off me on the twenty-seventh of November and they are not coming back and nobody has to give them back."

"And are you any good."

"I was."

"Are you now."

"Not for this," Elias Rook said. "That is what I have spent four days finding out."

Ezinne Abioye poured the tea and said, "That is better than saying you are going to fix it."

---

Here is what she talked about, in four hours, and Elias Rook wrote none of it down and could not have reproduced more than a third of it if he had tried, and every part of it is more load-bearing than anything on the schedule in his folder.

The boiler. It had to be lit and it was lit at seven in the morning and it had cost two hundred and forty pounds in 2039 and a man had come to look at it in March and had not come back. It was going to go at some point, and when it went she was going to be a woman of seventy-one in two rooms with a card that said `TRANSITIONAL`, and she had got as far as the thought and no further.

The stairs. One flight to her own door, which was not a problem, and three flights to the street in bad weather, which was. And the woman on the floor above, whose name was Adaeze and who was thirty-four, had been coming down and doing them since the spring without being asked and without saying anything about it, and Ezinne Abioye had tried three times to give her something for it and had been refused three times, and the fourth time she had left a bag of oranges outside the door on the landing with no note on it, and Adaeze had taken the oranges and left a jar of something on the way past on the Tuesday, and that was the arrangement, and neither of them had discussed it, and it had been going on for eight months.

Her sister, who had died in 2019, and who had lived at number nineteen for nine years and who had also been `PARTIAL`.

And a whole hour of it about the yard.

"You have not asked me one thing about the sea wall," she said at about three o'clock. "Everybody asks me about the sea wall. There has been a man from the board in this kitchen and a woman from a newspaper in September, and all three of them asked me about the sea wall, and it is the biggest thing that has ever happened in this street and it is going to happen in about eleven months, and it is not what is going to happen to me."

"What is going to happen to you."

"The yard gets sun until about two," she said. "In December you get it about half eleven and that is the whole of it. And the boiler. And Adaeze on the stairs, and whether she keeps doing it when there is no boiler and no reason for it."

---

At about half past three he asked the question, because there was one thing he had to ask and he had been working round to it for two hours.

"Is there anybody in this street whose name is on anything."

She looked at him.

"That is a strange question to ask a woman over her tea on a Wednesday," she said. "Most people ask it as a way of asking whether anybody in this street is all right. You are not doing that. You have a folder and you have asked it in a flat tone and you have written nothing down for two hours, and you have now written something down for the first time, and I watched you do it, and I have not asked to see it and I am not going to."

He had not noticed himself write it. That was not true — he had known he was writing it — and she was telling him something about the room and about herself, and he did not correct her, because she was entitled to have got it wrong in a way that was good for her.

"No," she said. "**Nobody in this street has their name on anything. I would know. There is a woman at number thirty-one has been on this row forty-six years and every single person on this street calls her by her husband's name, and the postal office calls her by her husband's name, and I have never once seen her be Mrs anything on any paper she has been handed in her life, and she is seventy-nine and she does not know there is anything to be. And I will tell you the true thing, which is that I have been aware of that since about 1998 and I have never done a thing about it, because it is her business and because I could not think of a single sentence that would not make it worse.**"

He wrote that down because she had said it and not because it was useful, and it was not useful, and the folder in the room was four hundred and twelve retained addresses and this street was not one of them, and the sentence he had just written down would do nothing at all for anybody.

"You are writing it down," she said.

"Yes."

"Because I said it."

"Because you said it."

"All right," said Ezinne Abioye, and poured the kettle again, and that was the end of that and she never mentioned it again.

---

He stayed until it got dark because there was a bus every twenty minutes and no reason to be in a two-room flat on Cutbank Row on a Wednesday evening that he could give, and she did not offer him anything to eat and did not make an excuse, and he understood both of those.

She put the chain back on and then took it off again, which is a thing that means two opposite things.

"If there is anything you need —" he said, and stopped.

Because he had been four seconds from it on Wednesday morning and had caught it then, and he had said it anyway at six in the evening like a man who has been in a warm room, and it was the sentence, and she had told him three hours earlier that she had had it from three people since September and had been very polite about it.

"No," Ezinne Abioye said. "But you will come again, won't you, and you do not have to, and I am not asking you to, and I am telling you that you will."

"Yes."

"That is not a promise," she said. "I do not want a promise off a man with one hand and a folder. Come on Thursday if it rains and if it does not rain, and shut the door behind you on the landing."

He got the 11 at ten past seven and the driver looked at him when he paid in coins, and he put two pound notes on the seat and said nothing, which was correct on both sides.

The back hurt. He had been sitting on a wooden chair in a two-room flat on Cutbank Row for six hours, having been of no use to anybody at all, and he had paid eleven pounds in fares, and he had learned one thing that could not be put in a paragraph.
