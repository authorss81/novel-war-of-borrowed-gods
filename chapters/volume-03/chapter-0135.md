# Chapter 135 — The Second Of January Is A Friday And A List About Crews

Cliff Marner was eighty-eight days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and eighteen days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and eighteen days in, and Elias Rook was standing at the counter at number six at five to nine on Friday morning, the second of January, which was the near end of a records request somebody had put in at a counter on the other side of this city on the fifth of December.

The counter was open until half past four on a Friday. It would be shut on Saturday and Sunday and open again on Monday, and that was the whole of the week in that building, and nothing in it had been shut for the day.

Wendy Tallow had the counter log open in front of her, which is a book at the counter with a hand on it and is not the regional verification log and is not the clearance record and is not the works' shift book, and it is the only one of those four things that anybody in that room could actually open.

"Nothing," she said, before he had got the words out.

"I did not ask."

"You were going to," Tallow said, "and I am going to save you the two minutes. A near end is a service target. It is not a date the service is going to meet by being pleased with you. It is the date on the estimate when it was taken, and the estimate was taken on the fifth of December and said four to six weeks, and a near end in four weeks and a far end in six is a service telling you the truth about itself, which is more than most documents do." She closed the log and squared it. "What has come back is nothing. What has not come back is an explanation, and I am not entitled to give you one and I would not know it if I had it. I have a hand on a book at a counter and that book is not it, and the log is held regionally, and there is a person in an office a long way off who knows what is in it and today is not a day anybody is going to tell either of us."

"And the far end."

"The sixteenth," Tallow said, "and I have said it because you have read it out in a room twice in December, which I know about because it is the kind of thing that comes back to a counter, and a person who has heard it twice from you can hear it a third time. I am not going to say it to anybody else first, and if you are in a room on the sixteenth and somebody asks you, that is between you and them."

Elias Rook said thank you and meant it, and did not ask whether she would chase it, because she was thirty-four and had a job and had once been owed an ordinary professional answer in a corridor in December and had been given a lie, and there was no version of this morning in which he was entitled to ask her to do one more thing.

---

Tobias Ferrand came in at twenty past nine with a coat over his arm and no folder, which was the first thing Elias Rook noticed about him and the only thing that told him anything.

He was fifty-two. He had been a duty flood officer in 2027 and in 2031 and in 2036, and he had been in this district nine days in December and had stood in a church hall off Marden Row in front of two hundred people and said a number out loud twice, and he was not a man who came into a room with papers in his hand when he did not have to.

"Nerys," Ferrand said. "Selwyn. Mr. Rook. Thank you for a Friday morning in a shop, and I have got about twenty minutes and then I have got a train, and I am going to give you one sheet of paper and say four things and go."

He put it on the trestle and squared it.

"Four things," Ferrand said. "One. The pilot authorization carries a date in August and it stands, and the method it authorizes is not withdrawn and I have not come here to take anything back. Two. The Retention Schedule of the second of December is held. It is not withdrawn, it is not superseded, and no further schedule is to be issued against this reference, and that is not a punishment for anybody, it is a decision I made in a letter on Boxing Day and I have not changed my mind this morning. Three. This is what a crew works from as of today, and it is a document about what a crew does next, and it is one page, and it is what a man is handed at seven o'clock in the evening, and it is going to stay like that, because a crew sheet that carries a name is a document somebody is eventually going to be asked to produce in a room and I have been in that room."

"And four," Elias Rook said.

"Four. Two hundred and eighty-five," Ferrand said. "That is the number I said in the hall in December and it has not changed and I am not going to pretend it has, and one thousand one hundred and forty addresses inside the alignment and about two thousand nine hundred people on a model that has been adjusted twice, and I have been doing this for nine years and I have never once had a document come back from a district asking me for fewer figures." He put his hands in his pockets. "I am not rebuilding the schedule. I said in that hall that I would not rehome four hundred people in an afternoon and get it wrong, and I have thought about it every day since, and on Tuesday night I am going to empty a block on Marlow Street with four men, and I know what I am signing and so does Nerys, and that is the job, and the reason I will not do it differently is that the next time it comes I will be the man who did it wrong to somebody."

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

"There is a page in the file dated the first of January," Ferrand said. "I have read it. I am not going to discuss it in a room with a program officer in it and I am not going to deny it, and if somebody from a newspaper is ever in this building I would like it noted that I did not deny it. It is one page, it is dated, and it is not withdrawn, and when the board asks me in February I will answer with the document and not with a sentence I have thought up on a train."

"Are you going to say anything about it to the forty-four," Elias Rook said.

"No," Ferrand said. "Because I have not got anything to say to the forty-four that would be true, and I have not got anybody to say it to, and a `TRANSITIONAL` line is not somebody to talk to. That is the honest position and I would rather say it in a room than have it said for me in a corridor and quoted back at me in March."

He picked his coat up off the back of a chair.

"What are you doing on Tuesday night," he said.

"I am going to be on that road."

"You are not on any sheet of mine and I cannot put you on any sheet of mine," Ferrand said, "and that is not me telling you to get out of it. It means that in February, when this is in front of a board, there will be no line in any document of mine with your name on it, and I have thought about that on a train for an hour and I have decided that I can live with it, and I would rather it were that than a form with a box on it that says a member of the public was present."

He said that last part almost to himself, and it was the only sentence in the twenty minutes that did not sound rehearsed, and it sounded like a man who had lost an argument with himself in December and had not told anybody which way he had come out.

He went out at four minutes to ten and the door shut behind him and nobody followed him out.

---

Elias Rook stood in the corridor outside number six for about a minute and a half and did not go down.

In his coat there was a tube with a roll of printout in it, and in the tube, folded in four, was a page dated the first of January with forty-four addresses on it and a signature block at the foot, and in his chest the thread had been on and off all morning in the way a light in a passage is on and off, and what it gave him was an answer to a question he had never thought to ask it: it was a way of knowing that a man was in a district, near ground somebody was being asked about, in a district with streets in it.

He stood in a cold corridor and put the two facts side by side on purpose, because he had promised himself in December that he would look at them once and not look away, and a man who looks away has already done it.

So: a band and a list of six hundred and ninety-seven rows on a piece of paper in a program, out of a man who cannot be consulted about it, and a man from the Cinder Quarter who can tell you when that reading is live, which is to say when the man is near, and no form anywhere in the city that would ask him to prove it and no second copy and no clerk who would ever see it. He could have said it to Anum in that room an hour ago. He could have said it in one sentence, and Anum was forty-one and had not slept since the twenty-ninth of November, and Anum would have believed him, and the thing he would have been handed would have had no author on it and no footnote and no second occasion, and there is nobody in that office who could ever have asked whether it was right on the day it was said, and nobody would have known, and he would have known.

He did not do it. He had said in December that he would not, and he had thought about it in a front room with thirteen people in it, and he had thought about it again on a bus with a new page in his coat, and he had not told anybody he was right to stop, and nobody in that building was going to, and the thing he had instead of a reason was a pair of times on a page in a notebook and the memory of a woman at a desk saying *bring me a time*.

He went out into a street where a foreman's notice with four rows on it was drying behind glass, and Nerys Hobbs was in the room with the one sheet of paper, and she had it flat on the trestle and her finger was on the third line.

"Five," Nerys Hobbs said.

"Five."

"I have got nine kitchens on that block," Hobbs said. "I have got them because a foreman of mine walked it in daylight in December and counted them himself, because a door is not a household, and he has been saying that to me for nineteen years and I have never once had it in writing. Five is not wrong. Five is what that street is. I am not going to sign your list, Mr. Ferrand, and I am not going to sign anything of anybody's, and I am going to sign my own sheet on Tuesday night and put my name at the bottom of it, and when the board asks me in February I am going to tell them that the crew went to nine households and the list said five and the difference was in my head and not on a piece of paper, and you can take that however you like."

"That is the correct thing to do," Elias Rook said.

"It is the only thing I can do," Hobbs said, "and it will not be worth anything to anybody, and I would like you to notice that I have noticed that."
