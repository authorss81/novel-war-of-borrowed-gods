# Chapter 106 — Margin, List, And Then Ash Street

Cliff Marner was fifty-nine days out of the ground. Della Marner was eighty-nine days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was eighty-nine days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Thursday morning, the fourth of December, with a folder under his right arm.

A woman of seventy-one had read a program card out loud to him on her landing at half past eight with the door shut behind them, and had then turned it over and written a date on the back of it in pencil in front of him, and had put it in a drawer.

"That is not the sheet," she had said. "It is a card they gave me in September and I have kept it because I keep everything. **It is not a record. It is so that I know how old it is.**"

The card said what it had always said. 19 CUTBANK ROW. STRUCTURE: TWO ROOMS. OCCUPANCY CLASS: PARTIAL. STATUS: TRANSITIONAL. And in the bottom corner of the back of it, in soft pencil, in a hand that had been keeping things for eleven years, there was a date and nothing else.

18 SEP 42.

He had spent twelve weeks behind a wall with nineteen hands on it, and four days in this district on the fact that a column exists, and is right, and is about a building. A woman of seventy-one had put a date on the back of a card in pencil so that she would know how old it was, and three months had gone by in which nobody in this city had asked her why.

---

Mara Okafor was doing a session at ten with six people in it and made him hold a box of gloves and a bowl, and she did the work with both her hands and did not talk for twenty minutes.

"Ninety-one," she said, washing her hands. "People who have come to this health center with a letter. Ninety-one in eleven weeks and every one of them says the same four things in the same order. That is a count of people. I am telling you the number and not what it means, because you will do what you want with a number and it is not my job to help you or to stop you."

"Go and be asked questions by somebody who likes you," she said, and went back in.

---

The survey office at the works yard has a trestle in it and a kettle and a window that looks at a wall, and Elias Rook asked for the second reading at eleven and got it in about nine seconds, which was the first thing that went wrong with his week.

Selwyn Anum unrolled it on the trestle without asking what it was for.

"Twenty-ninth of November," he said. "That is the new one. The eleventh of September is the old one and it is in the tube and I will get it. You are going to want both, and flat, so do not take them off the table."

It was the same soft line in the same three colors of ink, across the same west bank. The September roll came out of the tube ten minutes later and went over the top, and the two lines were four inches apart along about two streets and on top of each other everywhere else.

"What I have got," Elias Rook said, "is two pieces of paper about the same district and neither of them knows the other one exists."

"Count it yourself. Take the overlay and the pen, and do not put the schedule on the same table."

He counted for fifty minutes on a trestle with his right forearm flat on the overlay to hold the sheet down, and it was forty-four addresses along two streets, and not a blob.

"Good," Anum said. "That is ours, and it went in on the twenty-ninth and it came out on the first and nobody rebuilt anything, and I would rather you had it in the right order than I gave it to you."

Elias Rook took the folder out and opened it on his knee, because the man had told him not to put them together, and checked the forty-four one at a time with a finger.

Every single one said `TRANSITIONAL`. Not one said `RETAINED`. He had six hundred and ninety-seven rows in front of him and forty-four streets' worth of new measurement, and the two of them did not disagree about anything, because a schedule cannot disagree with a reading.

"One question," Anum said, at the kettle. "You are going to ask me why we did not rebuild it. I am going to answer it once and you are not to ask me it a second time.

"**If you built the schedule off the band every time the band moved, you would be rehoming four hundred people in an afternoon. And you would do it badly, because there is no version of an afternoon that is not bad. And people die in the moving.** I have got a man in this yard whose entire job is not to have that happen twice, and this district has already had it happen, and I have not slept properly since the twenty-ninth of November and I am not going to be told by a man on a bus that I got it wrong on purpose."

Then he sat down and opened a laptop and said nothing else for forty minutes, and he was not being cold. Elias Rook did not ask him a second time, and nobody in that room saw that either.

---

The corridor outside the survey office is eleven meters long with a door at each end and a window that looks at a wall. Nerys Hobbs came along it at about half past one at a speed that had nothing to do with him in it, and she stopped, which was the part he did not expect. Hard hat under one arm, drawings under the other.

"Miss Hobbs. If I asked you about the works—"

"I am going to stop you there, in a corridor, and say one thing about the corridor." She shifted the drawings. "**I do not discuss the works in a corridor. Ask me in a room and I will answer it in a room, and if the answer in the room is no, it will have been said where somebody who was not me can write it down.** That is not a complaint about you. Ask me in a room."

She went.

Elias Rook went and stood somewhere else with his back against a different wall, on purpose, because of what she had said about rooms. It was the first time in thirteen weeks he had somebody who might have helped and had let her walk, and he was not going to write that down.

---

He said the error out loud once, at about half past four, to a man who was not on his side and had not asked to be, standing in the yard by the survey office door because the door was locked.

"I have been in this district four days looking for a hand in a document, and there is not one, and I want to say that out loud to somebody who does not care whether I say it."

Anum shut the laptop.

"There is not one. There is one man who signs the schedule and he signs it in a room in the Meridian, and if you want to be unpleasant to him about it you will have to do it when the next reading comes, and I am not writing to tell you when."

"And the reading on the twenty-ninth is a fact. I understand that."

"It is a fact, and the schedule is a fact, and one of them is three months older than the other one, and I have never been able to explain to a room that both are true and that nobody has lied. It is the worst property of the work and I would not be doing it if the alternative was a hole in the ground with a district over it."

He picked the laptop back up. "Go home. It is a Thursday."

---

The footnote was on page two of nineteen pages, in italics, four lines long, and Elias Rook had read it on a bus on Monday and not understood it, and on Thursday afternoon in a hostel he understood all of it in four minutes.

*The band is indicative. This schedule is the operative document.*
*Where a reading and a schedule differ, the schedule governs.*
*Readings are taken at intervals and are not continuous.*
*Current reading: 11 September 2042.*

The last line was a field on a document dated the second of December 2042. It was not a lie and it was not a mistake, and there was no box on that page the twenty-ninth of November could have gone in.

He got out the notebook, turned to a page he had decided some weeks ago not to tear out, and wrote three things and crossed out two of them. Both of the two were attempts to say what the page was. He left this one:

> **Two pieces of paper about the same street, made three months apart, and neither of them can find out what the other one said. I have the right question and I do not have the sentence. Somebody in this city writes sentences about forms for a living and it is not going to be me.**

He read it, did not underline it, and closed the notebook.

---

He got the 11 at ten past six and was at Ash Street for twenty to seven, and the corridor light was lit and somebody had left a chair in it.

Petar Lisk was at the board with a pencil behind his ear and nine people were in the front room, and Joon Park was at the table with the custody schedule in front of him — twenty-two lines in his own hand — writing a line about item fourteen with the date in the margin, which is a thing he does and nobody has asked him to stop.

Elias Rook put the two rolls on the table end to end, and the folder beside them, and said nothing about any of it.

Joon Park looked at the four of them for a while.

"I have got a question and it is not a difficult one. Both of these are about Cutbank Row. This one is three months old, this one is two days old, one is called a schedule and one is called a reading. I have worked in a building for twelve weeks where the answer is on the right piece of paper and not the other one, so somebody is going to tell me in one sentence what the difference is, and it is going in a notebook either way, so I would like it to be the right sentence."

Tomas Vale was in the chair by the wall with a cup of tea he had brought with him, and he had said nothing for two hours, and nobody in that room was going to ask him about Tidegate, and Elias Rook had not asked him anything at all.

"Not about the district — about the method. It is mine to give, and that is the condition I put on it, and a method is worth nothing if only one man can use it."

"I have taught it to a man in a records room with nobody in it." He put the cup down on the floor beside the chair, carefully, with two hands. "Somebody asked a question just now and I am going to answer it in the order I would for a new method officer, and then I am going to stop, and if either of you improve on it afterwards I will know.

"Force goes somewhere. That is the whole of the ground. A reading tells you where it will want to go under a structure, and that is a measurement, and you can go back tomorrow and get the same answer or a different one, and either way you know which of the two you are holding."

"And the schedule," Joon Park said.

"A schedule tells you who is a household." He said it without any weight at all, which was the thing that made it land. "It is not smaller than the reading and it is not wrong, and it is not the same quantity. Somebody has to take the answer to the first question and put it into the second one, and **that is where people disappear** — not in the reading, because the reading does not know anybody, and not in the row, because the row is correct. In the conversion. A measurement and a list are not the same quantity, and the man who does the conversion is not doing either of the two jobs."

Nobody said anything for about four seconds. A woman at the back said, "That's just," in the voice of somebody who has said it in meetings, and it was not a hostile voice, and nobody answered her, and that was correct.

It is not just anything. It is also obvious. And no sentence a room can produce will hold both of those at once, and a room of nine people at nine o'clock on a Thursday did not have one, and that was the shape of the whole thing happening two miles and eleven minutes away, and nobody in that room said it out loud either.

Joon Park wrote it down as it was said, on the same sheet as the other twenty-two lines, in his own hand, in the margin where he puts dates. It did not become a twenty-third item, because he does not hold the folder and he does not hold the rolls.

Elias Rook put them back in the satchel at half past nine and went to bed with a back that was going to hurt on the Tuesday.
