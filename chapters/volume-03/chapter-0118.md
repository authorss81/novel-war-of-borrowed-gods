# Chapter 118 — The Shutters On Cutbank Row

Cliff Marner was seventy-one days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and one days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and one days in, and Elias Rook was at the depot on the far bank at ten to six on Tuesday morning, the sixteenth of December, because Tuesdays at half past six were his and had been since the end of October.

R. Dunleavy was fifty-nine and did not do the talking part of it. The hose was on the wall with a pressure gauge on it and a board with two columns. The needle went up and stayed, and Dunleavy wrote two figures in a book in his own hand and turned the book round so the page faced the man who had brought the sheet, and said nothing at all. Twenty minutes. Elias pinned the sheet with his right forearm because the left hand was gone and would not hold it, and carried the copy back with the right arm against his side.

The Tuesday cost him an hour of sleep a week for the rest of his life and he had done the arithmetic in October and got the right answer and had not looked at it since. He got the 11 at ten past eight with the folder under his right arm and the tube against his leg, and the shutters on Cutbank Row were half up because it was a Tuesday and the shops opened late in winter.

At the newsagent at number two he bought nothing and stood in the queue the way he had stood on the Sunday, because Postridge and Co. had a counter and a queue and a nail, and the nail had a sheet on it four times longer than nothing yet. The woman in the vest was not there on a Tuesday. The pile of leaflets was inside by the till. He did not take one, because he had carried one around for seven days the week before and had not shown it to anybody, and the program's public face was competent before anybody hostile had said a word about it.

---

The cell's public counter at number six opened at nine. The woman at the counter had a name card and a role card and the role was enquiries, and beside her at the counter stood a woman Elias had not seen before, thirty-four, with a notebook and a hand on the counter log.

"Mr. Rook," the counterwoman said. "Standing request of the first of December. This is your observer."

Wendy Tallow was thirty-four and a regional pilot observer, appointed under the named observer clause that came in with the pilot authorization in August and was in every cell running one of these programs. She had a hand on the cell's counter log, which was a book at the counter and was not the regional verification log and was not the clearance record and was not the works' shift book. She was correct the way everybody in that building was correct, and she asked fair questions.

"Mr. Rook," she said. "I have a hand on the log and I will keep it there while you are in this building. If you are shown a document I will note that you were shown it. If you are told a thing I will note that you were told it. I am not here to stop you."

"Understood," Elias Rook said. "Before I say anything else I have not told this counter what I am about to do, and I am telling you now and not afterwards. I am going upstairs to look at the rolls."

"Then I will note that," Wendy Tallow said, and kept her hand where it was.

Upstairs Selwyn Anum had a typed query on the trestle, one side, from the board's surveyor at Civic buildings, third floor, dated the sixteenth of December 2042, asking the cell for the date of the reading the Retention Schedule was built on. It could not be answered at the counter, so it had gone up to the regional office, and a copy of the Retention Schedule had gone up with it in the same envelope. It was the first time that document had left the district.

"The board has no procedure for a third category and does not intend to build one during an active alignment," Anum said, reading the referral line the way a man reads a thing he has read before. "They want a date and we have two of them. The schedule says the second of December and the footnote says the eleventh of September. There is no box on that page the twenty-ninth of November could have gone in. I am sending the copy and the query together, and what the regional office does with it is not mine."

Elias laid his right hand on the trestle edge while Anum sealed the envelope, because the left would not have held it. The thread was live in the room above number six the way it had been live on Friday, faint and exact. Off on the far side of the cut. Off in the Cinder Quarter. Live here.

"I am going to put my hand on the stair rail on the way down," he said, "and look at what is under this shop. It is damaged at the landing where the stair shakes. That is what the gift is for and I am telling you before I do it."

"Then do it and tell me in the words it happened in," Anum said.

The stair on the outside of the building shook under him the way it always did. He put his right palm on the iron rail where the landing met the wall, bare hand to cold metal, and the gift came the way it came when hand was on structure. Direction and stress through the shopfront, through the two rooms, down into Cutbank Row. Force going where the November roll said it went. It revealed direction and stress and nothing else. It was not a repair — the stair still shook and would go on shaking. It could not tell him how many people were on the street. It could not be carried out of the building. He lifted his hand after two minutes and went back up.

"It is carrying where the roll says it is carrying," he said. "I cannot write it and I cannot hand it to you."

"Then it is your word," Anum said, "and my schedule still governs."

---

Wendy Tallow met him in the corridor on the way down, between the stair and the counter, with the counter log still upstairs and nobody else in it.

"Mr. Rook," she said. "Can I ask you a fair question in a corridor, and you can tell me it is not a room."

"Ask," Elias Rook said.

"Has the resident engineer asked you for anything in a room. Miss Hobbs. Anything I should have in the log as a request."

The corridor was eleven meters long with a door at each end and no window that looked at anything but a wall. Hobbs had told him in a corridor that she did not discuss the works in one, and that if the answer in a room was no it would have been said where somebody who was not her could write it down. Ask her in a room. She had asked him in a room with the door shut for a sentence said out loud about whether the line moved, and he had given it, and there was no form for it and no reference anybody had to answer.

He owed the woman in front of him an ordinary professional reply. She was correct and she had a hand on the log and she had asked a fair question.

"No," Elias Rook said. "She has asked me for nothing."

It was a lie told to a program officer in a corridor, and the corridor made it worth nothing, and he knew it while it was in his mouth. He said it to keep a room with a shut door from becoming a line in a book at a counter, because Hobbs had asked for a fact and not a document and there was no form for that and the absence of the form was not anybody's fault. He did not write it down. He let her walk, the way he had let Hobbs walk in a corridor on the Thursday before, on purpose.

Wendy Tallow nodded and went back to the counter, and he got the 11 at twenty past eleven with the folder under his right arm and the tube against his leg and the left hanging empty. He rode past the street where a man of sixty-four lived without getting off the bus, because the man was not available as a structural authority on a designated site and nobody in the city was going to ask him about one, in writing or out of it. The query and the copy went up to the regional office in the same envelope, and a copy of the schedule left Tidegate for the first time since the second of December, and nobody in that building treated it as a decision.

That evening at Ash Street Joon Park asked what the observer had asked and Elias told him in the words it was said in, before he acted on it, because any condition went to the people who paid for it.

"She asked whether Hobbs asked me for anything," he said. "I said no."

Joon Park wrote nothing down, because a lie in a corridor was not a record and there was no form for it either.

"You lied to a program officer," Joon Park said.

"Yes," Elias Rook said, and did not explain it, and the chapter did not let him off.

Petar Lisk read the board at seven the way he did every evening, and the three questions were untouched and the nineteenth of November was still the only date. Elias stood in the doorway with the tube against his leg and listened, because a lie told in a corridor did not go on a wall and a reading that could not leave a building did not go on a pad. Inez Sato counted the resident cards without looking up — twenty-two cards, twenty-eight people — and the four dark hours came at midnight the way they did every night. The query was on its way to the regional office with a copy of a nineteen-page schedule in the same envelope, and what the Meridian did with it was not anybody's in that building.
