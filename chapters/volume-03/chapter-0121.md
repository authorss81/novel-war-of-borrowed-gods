# Chapter 121 — Two Names On One Card

Cliff Marner was seventy-four days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and four days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and four days in, and Elias Rook got up at five on Friday morning, the nineteenth of December, because the depot ran Tuesdays and Fridays at half past six and the Friday half was on the notice and empty in his week.

The notice was on the fridge at number six in a plastic sleeve gone milky with age. He looked at it while the kettle went. The Tuesday column had his name in it. The Friday column had a tick in it for every week of the year and no name in it at all, and the tick was a depot clerk's tick and not a cancellation, and it had been like that since a Tuesday in October for reasons that belonged to that Tuesday. Twenty days now, twenty fares, a hundred and ten pounds of buses, and the Tuesday cost him an hour of sleep a week for the rest of his life and he had done the arithmetic in October and got the right answer.

He did not go to the depot. He made tea and sat with the notebook closed, and then he got the 11 at ten past eight with the folder under his right arm and the tube against his leg.

---

The yard was doing what it did on a Friday. Two lorries in on the alignment and a third waiting at the gate for a slot, and behind glass on the gate a printed card saying the alignment went from open to closed in stages with dates on them, and that after the closure the public side could not be stood on.

Nev Peart was fifty-one and had been the foreman of that yard for nineteen years. He was standing at the bench by the kettle with his back to the door when Elias came in, and he did not turn round.

"There is tea," Peart said. "It is terrible and I have not made it better in nineteen years."

"I know," Elias Rook said. "I drank some of it in November."

The shift book was on the end of the bench under its rubber band and it was not the terminal. Nobody had asked about it. A man of forty-one stood at the trestle in the survey office with his back to the door and a laptop open, and he said that the sequence of the works was on the wall and the sequence was on the wall because the terminal in that office had gone down on Thursday and would be back on Monday.

"What is the sequence for?" Elias Rook asked.

"It's for the nights," Peart said.

---

There were four nights left in the year and the program had them in a wall planner with the weeks on it and the January four written in a different pen, because the January four had been added on Monday.

Eight nights. He counted them twice. The last four were in January and had been on the wall since the week before, and the last four were a booking of twenty working days that the yard would be inside until the fifth of February, and the twenty-fifth, the twenty-sixth, the twenty-seventh and the twenty-eighth of December were shut, and the last night in the year was the twenty-fourth, which was a Wednesday, which was also the last working day before the card behind the glass said the twenty-ninth.

Peart came over and looked at what he was looking at.

"That is the works," he said. "That is what is left. Whatever else is happening in that building upstairs, that is what I have to be on."

"How many people is that?"

"I have got a card for every door," Peart said. "Nine, eleven, eleven, and the rest. Three hundred and something across eight nights, near enough. I will not tell you the number off the top of my head and I will not write it in a book that somebody finds in a hundred years."

Elias Rook looked at the January rows. "Two blocks on the tenth."

"Two blocks on the tenth," Peart said. "And two more on the seventeenth. And the woman at thirty-one wants to be last on the seventeenth, and I have told her she can be, and she has not thanked me for it, and she is not going to."

---

The cards were in a wallet of plastic sleeves on a nail by the office door. One card for each door, printed by the works and filled in by the works, because the board's schedule was not the works' list and the foreman had never once been given the board's schedule. The address at the top. The night. The crew. His own initials at the bottom in ink.

Beside that nail was a second one, and on the second one were the worked cards, squared up, one for every door for every night since the beginning of November, and there was no reason on earth why a man should go near them.

At the yard gate, with the wind coming in under the corrugated, Elias Rook took the wallet off the nail and the sleeve door blew open against his forearm and the second nail went over and forty-odd worked cards came out of it into his hand and went across the wet concrete.

He squared them up because that is what you do with a pile that has fallen over. That was the whole of his reason and it was true and it was not the reason.

The card on the bottom was off the night of the sixteenth. Nine Cutbank Row, and a woman's name in the works' block capitals, and under it, in a different hand and a different ink, a man's name, and beside that one small figure for a floor.

He read the address and the first name and saw that there was a second line under it, and he stopped there, and he put the card back the way it had been, which was upside down, because he could not think of anything else to do with it, and then he squared the pile again and hung it on the nail with the front of it the wrong way round.

Nev Peart came out of the office with his hands in his pockets and looked at the nail, and then at the man, and squared the pile up the right way round without one word, and hung it back, and went inside.

He knew which card it was. Elias Rook understood that about four seconds later, on a pavement, with the folder under his right arm.

One name. Then a name somebody had added at the door, in the dark, with a torch, because the first name was a woman who was not going to be able to do that stair on her own at eleven o'clock at night, and it had been added the same way on the ninth of November, and nobody in the district had ever been told any of it and nobody in the district ever would be.

---

Above the shop at number six the stair shook under him the way it always did. He put his right palm on the iron rail where the landing met the wall and held it, and the reading came up, and the stair shook the whole time he was standing on it, and it was going to go on shaking after he had gone, and it had told him nothing whatever about the woman in the shop below or the crew in the yard or the two names on a card on a nail.

He was crying when he came out of the door and he stopped on the step and let it finish, because it was four minutes before the shop opened and there was nobody on Cutbank Row except a man putting out a barrier.

An old woman with a barrow stopped and looked at him.

"You're the one that's been in that district three weeks," she said. "You all right?"

"Yes," Elias Rook said. He got his right hand up to his face to show her there was nothing wrong with it, which was a lie and was not about her.

"A man went down a stair backwards with a woman in his arms on Marden Row in November," she said. "I watched him do it. It took forty minutes and he was still going at one in the morning."

"Who was he?"

"A man," she said, and then she stopped, and then she said, "A big one. Ordinary overalls. He had a torch in his teeth and he went down one tread at a time and he counted out loud, and I could hear him from the gate, and the crew stood at the bottom and let him take as long as he wanted and not one of them said anything about the time."

"That is the crew's business," Elias Rook said.

"That is the whole of it," she said. "Thirty-one years on this street, and the crew is the reason it is what it is, and it is not a program and it is not a man from an office. It is a set of blokes who will not be rushed on a stair. You cannot put that in a form and you cannot lose it in a schedule, and when that lot goes, that street is a different street and nobody can write down how." She took her barrow on.

---

The thread was live in the survey office at half past twelve and off in the corridor and off over the water and off in the Cinder Quarter, and it told him whether the ground he was standing on was the ground he was being asked about, and it did not tell him where anybody was, and it had been telling him that for eight days and he had got used to it in the way a man gets used to a light in a passage.

The man it belonged to was sixty-four and lived in a street he rode past and did not get off at, and he had set a boundary in writing in November in the past tense and forever, and he was not consulted about any designated site by anybody in writing or out of it, and Tidegate had been designated on the second of December. What that man had was a reading. It was the only true thing anybody had offered him in thirteen weeks, and it could tell him where force went and it could not keep a woman's heart going on a stair at eleven o'clock at night.

There was one other person in this city he could ask for that.

He stood in the survey office doorway with his hand on the frame and worked the whole of it out in about ninety seconds, and it was short, and none of it was a discovery. A question is not a choice of street. A question is a hand put out. The hands he wanted belonged to a woman who would be fifty-four in twenty-one years, and she had never met him, and he did not have a right to spend her, and the fact that a woman on Cutbank Row had let a man from the Cinder Quarter sit in her kitchen and read out nine hundred doors out of a sheet she had kept since 2031 did not give him anything whatever with this one. Being asked for is a thing that happens to other people.

That was the arrangement and the whole of it. He did not telephone her. He got the 11 at half past three and sat on the far side of the cut with the folder under his arm, and for about forty minutes he did nothing, which was the first nothing he had done in this district since the twenty-ninth of November.

Nineteen hundred people on the alignment, and two hundred and ten meters of migrating void under it, and a reading that was right, and a schedule with two hundred and eighty-five addresses on it and no column anywhere that a person goes in. A woman of seventy-one at number nineteen who is not a household and does not want anything. A woman of sixty-three at number twenty-two with a sheet of doors in a kitchen pencil. Four dead people in half an inch of margin that nobody had ever asked her for, and that he had asked her for. A man of fifty-one whose shift book is on a bench under a rubber band and shut, and whose initials are in ink at the bottom of every card on that nail.

And somewhere in that district, at that moment, somebody was keeping a list of the people who could not get down a stair in the dark, and he had not found her, and would not have found her in another fortnight, and that was the method, and the method had got him a folder and a tube and a thread and nothing else at all.

He would have to ask somebody. The only somebody in this city whose hands had ever been on offer to him was a woman who did not know he existed, and that was a thing you did not do at half past three on a Friday without a rule, and he did not have the rule, and the rule was not his to invent.
