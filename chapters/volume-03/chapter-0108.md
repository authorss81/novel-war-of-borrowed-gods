# Chapter 108 — Saturday

Cliff Marner was sixty-one days out of the ground. Della Marner was ninety-one days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was ninety-one days in, inside a tunnel that had been sealed for fifty-eight days and classified in nine words, and the handset line was dead, and nobody was going to a lip.

The Havenport Tidal Board and the Bellwether cell's public counter had shut at six o'clock on Friday night and were shut until Monday, and about a hundred and forty people in Tidegate had a Saturday, and there was nobody in the district whose job it was to tell them the truth about a schedule.

Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight with the tube under his arm and a card in his coat pocket, and he was not on his own time any more and had not been since Monday, and he had told nobody that and nobody had asked.

---

The corner of Cutbank Row and the yard approach on a Saturday morning at about half past ten is the best thing in that district and it takes an hour and ten minutes to happen.

A woman in a fluorescent vest had a folding table out of a van and a stack of leaflets on it and no ceremony whatsoever, and Elias Rook recognized her from Saturday the twenty-ninth of November and from four other days since, and she was doing the same job she did on a Tuesday, which was handing a piece of good paper to anybody who came past and saying one sentence about it. She said the sentence to him, and he said he had one already, and she said *most people do, love*, and put the leaflet back on the stack.

"Forty-one thousand of those went out with the rates," she said, to nobody in particular. "There's one on every door. I hand them out on a Saturday for the ones that go in the post and get put on a sideboard."

Nobody looked at the table.

And at about five past eleven a woman of about sixty-five came round the corner from the post office with a folded sheet of paper in her right hand and stopped at the bollard, and Elias Rook understood about nine seconds later what he was looking at, and then stood very still.

---

Winifred Alagoa did not make an announcement.

She unfolded the sheet, turned it so that it faced the street, and ran her finger down the left-hand column, and said the numbers out loud.

"Rennard Street. One to thirty-one. Nineteen, and the one at the top has been empty since August, and I have a date for it because I went and looked."

Thirty-one people had stopped. Nobody had been asked to stop. A man with a dog stood and listened to about four numbers and then went. Two women came out of the post office and stood behind her. A man in a high-visibility vest from the yard, who had been eating a sandwich, crossed the road to hear whether his own street was on it.

"Murrow Street. One to nineteen. Fourteen, and the two at the end have been empty since the spring, one since March and one since August, and I have a date for the second one because I went and looked."

"That's her," somebody said. "That's the one she does."

"I do Rennard, and I do Murrow, and I do Marlow, and Sallow Court I do last," she said, about four minutes later, "because I have to be careful with Sallow Court and I am not telling you why until I have got to the end of it."

It went on for about an hour.

Elias Rook stood on the far side of Cutbank Row for fifty-five minutes of it and did not say one word to anybody, and at some point around twelve o'clock he understood what he was looking at, and it was not a discovery, it was the floor coming out.

**He had spent twelve weeks learning a method.** Petar Lisk's three questions on a wall. Finding 3, used correctly, by the people it was written for, in a cooperative, in a building with its own lights. Step four of eight pages, said out loud before anything else so that a room knows it was not told. A form that could be made to tell the truth by the people who were in it. Twelve weeks of a man in his thirties learning that the instrument is not the movement and the movement is not a man, and that the whole of the job is to get people to do the ordinary thing in a room without him in it.

And it had been happening on a corner in another district every Saturday morning for eleven years, and it did not need him, and it did not need a wall, and it did not need anybody to be a rescuer or a field technician or a man with one hand. It needed a woman with a sheet of paper and a bollard and five hours.

**Thirty-one people, and not one of them thought it was remarkable**, which was the part that undid him, because the whole of his education had been conducted in rooms where the fact that a thing was happening was visible from the street.

He had a leaflet in his pocket that he had carried around for five days and had not shown to anybody, and it was the program of a regional pilot program with a Director's name at the bottom of it, and it was better paper than anything the Continuity Office had put on a table in eleven weeks, and it was in every kitchen in this district, and it had cost nothing and done more in a fortnight than his building had done in twelve weeks.

---

The yard runs six days and the Saturday is when the program's own people are most visible, and at about half past one Elias Rook was inside it and the canteen was doing lunch and the foreman wanted a hand with a trestle.

Nev Peart was fifty-one and had been the foreman of this yard for nineteen years and kept a shift book in his own hand, and it was on the tail of the bench by the kettle with a rubber band round it, and it was not the terminal.

The trestle was for the Saturday notices and it took two men because of the weight of the top and because the ground was soft where the lorry had been. It was about fifteen meters from the canteen door to the gate and the route was not straight because of the generator.

He carried it eleven times.

He is a man with one hand and eleven times is a fact about his grip and about the fact that he is thirty-five and has been carrying things out of buildings since he was twenty-one, and by the fifth one he had stopped being careful about it, and by the eighth one he had found the rhythm that works with a right forearm under a rail, and by the eleventh a woman he had not met came out of the canteen door with two mugs in her hands and stopped and looked at him.

"You're the one that got off the eleven on Tuesday," she said. "With the folder."

"Yes."

"Right." She went back in.

That was the entire relationship the district had with him at the end of the day, and it was better than the one he came for.

---

He got the folder out on the trestle at about half past three, with the tube leaning against his leg, and the notice board up behind him with the alignment area figures on it, and nine people in front of him, and about a hundred and forty people in the district with nowhere else to be.

"Before I say anything else I am going to tell you that I have not told this yard what I am about to say, and I am telling you now and not afterwards."

That was step four and it came out of him without a decision, which is what a step is.

"There is a document. It is nineteen pages. It is dated the second of December and it has six hundred and ninety-seven rows and two hundred and eighty-five of them say a word that means your address is not in the district that is being kept, and that word is on your card and you have all had it, and here is the number—"

And he stopped, in the middle of it, with the folder open, in front of nine people.

Because he had no version history and no forum and no standing in a district he had been in for six days, and every one of those nine people was going to believe him, and the only reason they were going to believe him was that he was a stranger with a folder and a good coat, and in about nine minutes he would have said a number out loud in a yard to nine people, and by Monday it would have been in every kitchen on that row, and there was not one person in Tidegate who had any obligation whatsoever to answer him, and if he was wrong then the number would be in the district and he would be the only man in the country who could take it back.

He had spent twelve weeks in a building learning that a document can be made to tell the truth. He was the one who had been wrong, in a corridor, on a Tuesday, and he had said it out loud to a wall.

He folded the folder shut.

"I am going to stop," he said. "I have got no forum and nobody in this district has to answer me, and if I say that number in this yard you will believe me because I am a stranger and I have got a folder, and I have been wrong once this week already and I found out from a man who did not have to tell me."

The nine people looked at him. Nobody said anything. Somebody at the back said "fair enough" in a voice that meant it, and then there was about ten seconds of nothing, and then the woman from the canteen said, "Well, you'll be back on Monday then," and went back to the kettle, and that was the end of it.

It was the first time in thirteen weeks that Elias Rook had stopped himself in a room, and there was no notice in any building in the city that told him he was allowed to.

---

The last hour of the Saturday was carrying and drinking tea and being handed a paper cup of it by a man who did not ask him anything.

At about five he went and put his hand on the shift book on the bench by the kettle, and Nev Peart looked at him and did not open it, and Elias Rook took his hand away, because on Wednesday a woman had watched him write something down and had been entitled to it and he had written it anyway, and because on Thursday a man had told him in a corridor that a thing said in a corridor is worth nothing, and because on Friday a woman behind a stone counter had told him that anything that came back sooner than four to six weeks is a person guessing.

Three people had told him something in three days that could not be used for anything, and all three of them had been right, and they had never met, and the only thing he had done about it was leave a rubber band where it was.

He got the 11 at ten past six and rode it into the city with his back very bad and had a chemist's plaster on the second right knuckle, and it had come off and gone again inside his coat pocket, and the tube under his arm was the only thing that had cost him money all day apart from the fare, and forty minutes of a Saturday had not been anybody's time but his own and nobody had paid for it and nobody was going to.
