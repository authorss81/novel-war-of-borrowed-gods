# Chapter 113 — Nineteen Years Ahead

Cliff Marner was sixty-six days out of the ground. Della Marner was ninety-six days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was ninety-six days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Thursday morning, the eleventh of December, with the rule he had said out loud in a kitchen in Cutbank Row running out that day and nobody in the district knowing it but him and one young man.

Sunday to Thursday was four days. He had said it to a man who did not need to be told anything, and Joon Park had said write it down and had not written it down. The four names were still in a margin in a kitchen pencil and on the back of Joon Park's hand. He had not knocked on four doors. He had not put them on any schedule and given them no number. The day ran out at midnight and he had spent the three before it getting a date and a time and a foreman's name on a band, and a room with a door that shut, and nothing else.

He carried the folder under his right arm because the left hand was gone and had been gone since before any of this district had a name to him. He could hold the tube with the right forearm against his side and pin a slip with the same forearm when the wind took it. He could not hold a pen in the left and could not catch a door with it when the stair shook. On the bus he kept the notebook in the inside pocket of his coat and did not take it out, because there was nothing in it that needed to be on a bus.

---

The room off the canteen was free at half past nine and he put his name in the line on the board by the door. It was the same table and the same four chairs and the same window looking at a wall. The woman from the canteen brought one tea and left the other side empty without being asked.

Nerys Hobbs came at half past nine with the hard hat under one arm and nothing under the other one, and shut the door behind her.

"You have got one day," she said. "You told me yesterday you had something in a kitchen and you were not going to use it for four days from Sunday. Today is the fourth."

"Today is the last of it," Elias Rook said. "I am going to tell you what I am about to do before I do it, and I am telling you now and not afterwards."

"Say it, then."

"There are four names in a margin in Cutbank Row that are not on any list of anybody's. I am not going to say them in this room and I am not going to write them down in this room. The rule runs out tonight. When it runs out they will still be in a margin and on the back of a young man's hand, and that is the whole of what I am going to do with them."

She looked at him for a second and then nodded once.

"That is a real answer," she said. "Most men would have spent the fourth day finding a way to make the rule mean something else."

"I have spent three days finding out I have no forum," he said. "I am not going to spend the fourth finding one in a kitchen."

She put the hard hat on the chair. "Then ask me what you came to ask."

"I want to stand on the alignment where the two lines are four inches apart and look at what is under it. Not the schedule. The ground. I want to know whether the line moved after it was given to us, the way you asked yesterday. I do not have a document with a reference on it and I do not have an observer and I do not have a forum. I have a request you made in a room with the door shut, and I am going to try to answer it the way you asked it."

"You do not have a way to answer it," Hobbs said. "You have got a folder and two rolls and two yellow slips with a foreman's name on them. None of those is the ground."

"I know."

"Then what are you going to stand on it with."

"My hands," Elias Rook said. "That is the whole of it and it is not a method and I am telling you before I do it because you are the person who would pay for it if I got it wrong."

---

Sallow Court at eleven in the morning was a block with a shut gate and a works notice on it and nobody behind it. The notice had been there since the ninth of November and the ink had gone pale at the corners. Three printed lines. Clearance, 9 November. Nights of 8 and 9. Residents relocated by arrangement. Clearance complete, no outstanding.

He did not go through the gate. He stood on the public side of the alignment, which was public ground for three weeks more and then was not, and the alignment went from open to closed in three stages with dates on them whether or not anybody wrote them down for him. A season was a window and the window was still open by about three weeks. He put the folder down on the pavement against the wall where it would not get kicked, and put his right hand on the brick of the low wall by the gate, because the left would not have told him anything and would have slipped off.

The wall was cold through his glove. He took the glove off with his teeth and put his bare palm on the brick, and the cold went straight into the knuckles.

Nothing happened for about twenty seconds. A bus went past on the low road. A man came out of number twenty-seven with a bag and did not look at him.

Then the seam came the way it always came, without any sound he could have described to anybody, and the cold in the brick changed under his hand.

He had been a First Witness for thirteen weeks. He knew what an offer felt like the way a man knows a room has gone quiet. It was not a voice and it was not a light. It was a sentence waiting to be read, in plain words, in the shape the emergency network used when it could carry one safely.

Give it the season. I am not asking for the district.

Elias Rook kept his hand on the wall and did not move it, because moving it would have been a decision and he had not made one yet.

Nineteen years ahead. Sixty-four now, which made the lender eighty-three, or the age a man of sixty-four would be in nineteen years if he lived. A man with the same name and the same method and a different life, standing in another room in this city or in no room he could picture, offering the one thing nobody in this district could give him. The present man was sixty-four and standing in another room in this city and was not asked. He had set a boundary in writing in November, in the past tense, forever, and Elias had spent the whole autumn learning that a boundary was not an obstacle to be worked around. He did not try to find the street the man lived on. He did not rehearse a question he would not ask. The wanting was visible in the way he kept his hand where it was. The asking was impossible and he did not attempt it.

Hobbs had asked yesterday, in a room with the door shut, for a sentence said out loud in a room by somebody allowed to say it about whether the line moved after it was given. The panel asked for the same sentence, in the same shape, out loud, in a room. Two people with the same request, nineteen years apart, neither of them knowing about the other. He noticed the shape and did not say anything about it, because noticing was not using.

He said it out loud to the empty street, because a return had to be answered in words and not in thought, and because there was nobody to hear it and that was correct.

"Yes," he said. "I will take it on those terms."

The brick warmed under his hand by about half a degree. It was not a repair and it did not fix anything. It showed him where force wanted to go under Sallow Court, the way a man who had done it for forty years would see it without being told. Direction and stress. A line running off the block toward Marlow Street, four inches on a roll, forty-four addresses wide, carrying where the September roll said it carried and not carrying where the November roll said it did not. It did not tell him how many people were on the street. It did not tell him who was a household. It did not leave the wall he was touching. He stood there for about four minutes with his right hand on cold brick and learned nothing he could write down, and that was the gift working exactly as it was offered.

When it eased he put his glove back on with his teeth and picked up the folder with the right arm, and the left hung at his side and did nothing, which was its job now. His right knuckles ached the way they did after carrying a trestle eleven times. He wrote nothing in the notebook. A reading did not leave the building it was taken in, and this one had been taken on a pavement with nobody watching, and there was no version of it that could be carried to a counter.

---

He went back to the yard at half past twelve and Hobbs was in the corridor with drawings under her arm, going somewhere quickly.

"In a room," Elias Rook said. "Not here."

She stopped, looked at the corridor the way she had looked at one before, and nodded toward the room off the canteen. The board by the door still had his name in the line. They went in and she shut the door.

"I told you this morning what I was going to do, and I did it, and now I am telling you what happened in the words it happened in before I act on it," he said. "A future engineer nineteen years ahead offered a load reading on the terms you asked for yesterday. The boundary is that the reading is his to give and the list of who is on it is not. The return is to tell him out loud in a room whether the line moved after he gave it. I said yes. That is the whole of the offer."

Hobbs sat down slowly.

"Did you ask him," she said. It was not a question about the future.

"I did not ask anybody," Elias Rook said. "The present man is not available and I did not go near him. This came off a wall on Sallow Court with my hand on it and nobody else in the street."

"And the line."

"The line moved," he said. "Off this block and onto Marlow Street. That is a fact about force and not about anybody. I cannot write it down and I cannot hand it to you and I cannot carry it out of the street I stood on. If I say it in this room it is my word and not a document, and you cannot build to it and you cannot sign it."

"No," Hobbs said. "I cannot."

They sat there for about a minute with the door shut and nobody writing anything down.

"That is the first honest sentence about the eighth of November anybody has said in a room since November," she said at last. "It is not a sentence I can use and that is why it is honest. Thank you for not bringing me a piece of paper."

He walked to the bus with the folder under his right arm and the tube against his side, and the four-day rule ran out behind him without any ceremony. The four were still in a margin and on the back of a young man's hand. He had not used them to get the reading and had not traded them for a forum. On Marden Row he changed buses with eleven minutes each way and about two pounds seventy-five a fare, and the money and the sleep and the travel were the cost of going to Tidegate and they came in that order and nobody paid them but him.
