# Chapter 136 — What A Notice Does Not Carry

Cliff Marner was eighty-nine days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and nineteen days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and nineteen days in, and Elias Rook was in Tidegate on Saturday morning, the third of January, because the yard was open and the counter at number six was shut behind a locked door and the board was shut and all three of those things had been decided in a week in December and printed on pieces of paper, and none of them had been announced to anybody.

The door slip was still in its plastic holder on the inside of the door, screwed to the wood, and the counter log was not in the room because the room was not open, and a Saturday is a Saturday.

He got the 11 at ten past nine and paid the fare, and it was a fare and not a day of a run, because the run had stopped on the Wednesday before Christmas and had stood at twenty-four days and a hundred and thirty-two pounds, and he was not going to start one again because a month had gone by and nothing in that district had got any easier.

---

Nev Peart was on the bench by the kettle with his back to the door and there were two lorries in and nothing else at all, because the booking did not start until Monday.

"Nineteen years," Peart said, without turning round, "and I have never in any of them had a Saturday in January with nothing on it, and I am not going to enjoy this and I am not going to be told about it either."

"Is there any of it worth knowing."

"There is one thing and it is not mine and it is yours," Peart said. "Mrs Postridge has had six sheets on that counter since Friday morning and she has sold four, and one of the four went to a man on Rennard who has been telling people on Rennard since Friday afternoon that the seventeenth is Rennard, and it is not, and Rennard is on the tenth and again on the nineteenth, and I cannot correct a man on a street about a notice I put up myself, because if I correct him I am saying the notice is wrong, and the notice is not wrong, it just does not carry everything, and a thing that does not carry everything is still a true thing, and I have known that since 1994 and it has never once stopped being inconvenient."

"Which notice does not carry everything."

"None of them do," Peart said. "That is not an accusation. That is a Friday morning."

Nerys Hobbs was in the survey office with the terminal off and her coat on, in the last hour before the yard shut on a Saturday, and she had a sheet of her own on the trestle with four ruled lines on it and not one of them signed.

"That is Tuesday," she said, before he got to the door. "Four lines. Four men, one ladder, one sign at the bottom, and the fourth line says the street was walked at the end and the barriers came down. I write it at about half past six in the morning and I do not write anything else on it, and I have been asked by two men in an office in a fortnight to add a line, and the line they want is a line for anything left behind, and I have said no to both of them, and the reason I have said no is that a line for anything left behind turns a sheet about a street into a sheet about a suspicion, and I have signed enough of those in my life."

"Somebody is going to leave something behind."

"Somebody leaves something behind every single time," Hobbs said. "It is usually a chair and a curtain rail and a child's shoe, and it is a chair and a rail and a shoe, and the day I write *item left* on a works sheet is the day a man in a good coat can stand on a pavement on Rennard and ask an old woman whether she is sure she took everything. I would rather the sheet says the street was walked."

---

She was at the bollard at about half past ten with about fifteen people and it was a cold morning and the queue was not a queue, it was four people who had come early and then the people who had seen them.

Winifred Alagoa read it the way she had read it every Saturday for eleven years, standing with the sheet in both hands and the wind coming off the Cut, and she did Rennard, and she did Murrow in a flatter voice because he had walked those two in December, and she did Marlow Street and she stopped on Marlow Street for longer than she had in December, and then she did Sallow Court last and said thirty-nine doors and did not say the other number, because the other number was not hers and had not been hers since 1971.

"She has read that notice four times," Elias Rook said to nobody, from about thirty feet away, which is as close as he had got in three weeks and was as close as he was going to get.

"Four times is not many," Alagoa said, without turning round. "It is a Saturday and it is cold."

"You have read it four times."

"I have read it four times and I have not written anything down and neither will you," she said. "I want to say one thing about that notice and then I am going to do Sallow Court."

"Say it."

"It is a true thing," Alagoa said. "Four nights, four streets, four crews, and every one of them is a night a crew is coming. It is true and it is on a nail and it is the first true thing about January that anybody in this district has been given. And it does not have December on it."

Somebody at the back said, and it was a man of about fifty, "It does not need December on it."

"It does not need December on it and December is not on it," Alagoa said. "And there is a woman on this row at number nine Rennard who went out of her house on the twenty-second of December with a bag and her cat in a basket and has been asked three times this week by her neighbors when her street is next, and she has had to say it went on the twenty-second, and every time she has said it she has had to say the street is not on the notice because the notice is about January. That woman did everything anybody asked her to do in December and she is on no nail and she is in no list and I have not got a piece of paper with her name on it either, so I am not saying one word to her and I am saying it here because here is the only place a sentence of mine has ever gone anywhere."

Nobody said anything for about five seconds, which on a Saturday morning at a bollard in Tidegate is a long time.

The woman of forty who has lived at number eighteen since 1988 was at the edge of it with her hands in her sleeves, and she had wanted a number in December, and she wanted it to be the right one.

"Thirty-nine," she said. "Is thirty-nine the right one."

"No," Alagoa said. "It is the one I have. I have never said thirty-nine was right and I am not going to start in January in the cold, and if I knew which one was right I would not tell you, because then you would go and tell somebody and it would be off this corner by Tuesday."

"That is the first straight answer anybody has given me since September," the woman said, and went home.

"That is the whole of what I have got," Alagoa said, and went back to Sallow Court.

---

He stayed for about half an hour and then walked away from it, which was new, and on the Friday before Christmas he had walked away from it with a card in his coat and a rule in his head, and this time he walked away from it because there was a sentence in the air and it was not his and it did not need him to repeat it to somebody in an office.

At the end of Cutbank Row, outside two shops and under the awning of the post office, a woman of about seventy came up to him and asked him whether number thirty-one was on it, and he said he did not know.

"You have been in that district since before the shut," she said.

"I have."

"Then you are the man people ask," she said, "and you do not know, and I have watched you stand at that corner three Saturdays in a row and I have watched you not tell anybody anything, and I have decided that you are not a man who knows and is keeping it, and I would rather you were that, because a man who knows and keeps it is a thing I have had a great deal of in my life."

"I am not," Elias Rook said, "and you are right to have decided that, and I would rather be checked at a bollard than trusted at one."

"Well," the woman said. "Do not stand there like a man who knows."

She went, and he stood there, and it took him about a minute and a half to work out that he had been going to that corner for three Saturdays to give things away, and had stopped giving them away in December, and had assumed since December that stopping was the same as listening, and that a woman of seventy had just told him the difference in about forty words on a Saturday morning without knowing his name.

---

He got the 11 at ten past twelve and sat on the far side of the cut for about forty minutes and did nothing, which was the second time since the twenty-ninth of November that he had done nothing in this district, and the first time he had done it on purpose.

In his coat there was still the card with four dates on it and no addresses, and it was still the four dates that had been and gone, and there was a nail at the yard gate with the four that were coming on it and no addresses either, and there was a woman on a Saturday morning who had said out loud in the street that the women who went in December were not on it, and had said it once, in the cold, to about fifteen people, and had not written it down, and would say it again next Saturday if anybody asked her and would not say it in a room to anybody, and there was now a piece of paper in a district office two miles away that a clerk had typed on Friday which did not have her in it and was not going to.
