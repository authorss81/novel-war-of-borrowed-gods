# Chapter 145 — What Is Your Name And What Is Your Address

Cliff Marner was ninety-eight days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and twenty-eight days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and twenty-eight days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Monday morning, the twelfth of January, and the counter at number six opened at nine because it was a Monday and Mondays and Fridays were the two days of the week on which anybody in this district could put a name into a system in a building with a door on it.

Wendy Tallow had the counter log open and a second book beside it that had not been on the counter in the four months Elias Rook had been coming to this room, and it was the district's own arrival book, and it had been on a shelf in the board's third floor for twenty-six years and had come down to Cutbank Row for the first time in its life on Friday, because a district being emptied on a schedule that has got no column on it for anything a person goes in has acquired a public counter, and that was the whole of the reason and nobody wrote it down anywhere.

The three of them came in at ten past ten, and Piotr Drzewicki came in separately at a quarter past ten because he had not walked with them, and Declan Hough had not come at all and sent a woman of about fifty with a folder and said he would be there in the afternoon if there was anything to be there for.

"What is your name," Elias Rook said to the man of forty-one, "and what is your address."

"Emrys Vaughan. A flat in a building on a street that is not in this district, and I have a key, and I do not think it is the flat the key is for."

---

It took from ten past ten until about half past one, and it took longer than anybody expected, and nobody in that room was a villain.

The clerk behind the counter was a man of about fifty called Garnet Okpara and he had been doing this for nineteen years and he was the fourth man he could remember being told by somebody from a program that he had the wrong document. He was correct. He stayed correct all the way through, and the reason he was correct is that every document on the table that day was valid.

"There is nothing in front of me that is not true," Okpara said, at about eleven o'clock, to the room, with a finger on the top of a stack. "I want that said first, in front of the visitors, because the three of them have been told by at least three people that there is something wrong with their papers and there is not. This is a certificate of marriage. It is a register of births. It is a tenancy. Every page in this stack was signed by a person who was alive and entitled to sign it, and every one of them is a true document, and the difficulty is that two of them are true about a world that is not this one and I am not able to say which."

"Can you say which," Elias Rook said.

"No," Okpara said. "And that is not me being careful, that is the position. If I could say which I would say which and it would be a morning's work and I would have done it. I have got a man here whose marriage says Ceri Vaughan and I have never seen Ceri Vaughan, and I have got another man here whose father says he is not his son, and I have got a woman here whose son is her nephew, and every document I have looked at says what it says."

"Whose marriage," Elias Rook said, and got as far as looking at the man, and stopped. "That was a third question and I did not ask it. I have asked two all day and I am not going to ask three, and that is the arrangement and I made it on a pavement on Sunday, and you are all three of you inside it."

"Then here is the position in a room," Okpara said, "and I have given this position four times this month. Every one of these three people is a person in this district. That is not in doubt and it is not in dispute and I have entered all three. What I cannot do is make a record that says a thing about one of them that they have told me with their own mouth, because when I write it, it stops being what a man said on a pavement and becomes what a district holds, and in eleven months somebody is going to use it in a room and I will not be in it and neither will they."

---

Marek Drzewicki, nineteen, gave his name and his address and then said that he had a receipt from the fourth of March, which was a Wednesday, and Okpara took it and looked at it and put it down and said the words *this is not a document, this is a receipt*, and did not put it in the book, and did not throw it away, and put it in a folder of his own and wrote the date on the folder, which is not a form and is not in any book and which he said out loud he had done for a man in 'thirty-nine as well.

Marek Drzewicki watched him do it and then said, to nobody, "He put it in a folder."

"Your father says you are not on a birth certificate," Okpara said, "and I have looked at the index and he is correct, there is no page, and I have looked at the register of births for that year and there are two hundred and forty-one entries and none of them is you. I cannot enter a birth that the register does not have. What I can do is enter you, which I have done, at half past eleven this morning, and the row behind your name says *evidence held, page not found*, and that row is the only honest sentence I have been able to write on a piece of paper in this room in nineteen years and I would like somebody to look at it."

"You can put a sentence in it," Elias Rook said.

"I can put a sentence in a column that has been blank since 'thirty-nine and has been used eleven times," Okpara said, "and I have never had to explain it to anybody because nobody has ever needed it, and I am not going to explain it to the district."

---

At about half past one Sabina Hough sat down on the wrong side of the counter and said the sentence she had come to say, which was that a woman at the health center on Marden Row in November had told her that her son was her nephew, and that the woman had been extremely careful and had looked at two documents before she said it, and that the woman had also said that there is no form.

"There is no form," Okpara said. "I have looked. There is a relationship field on a tenancy and it is a field for a spouse and it is a field for a person who is not on the tenancy. There is nothing for what you are describing and I have not got a second opinion, because there is nobody in this building to give me one, and I am not going to write *nephew* in a column headed *relationship* in a district that has already had enough of my handwriting this winter."

"Then I do not exist," Sabina Hough said, "in the way you mean it."

"You exist," Okpara said, "at half past twelve this afternoon, in a book on this counter, with your name and your address and the day, and that is the whole of what a book will hold, and I am sorry it is not more, and I have been sorry about that since 2004 and it has not got better and I have got very good at it."

Declan Hough came in at twenty past two and stood in the room for about nine minutes and did not ask a single question, and at about half past two he asked one.

"If I put my name against hers, does that make it true."

"No," Okpara said. "It makes it a thing two people said. That is not nothing and I will not tell you it is nothing, and it is also not the same as a relationship being recorded, and I have not got a column for what you want and I have got a pen."

---

At about ten to three Elias Rook went out into the corridor outside number six and Joon Park was on a chair in it with the custody schedule in his coat and the fourth name of a ninth of November written on the back of his right hand in ballpoint, forty days old and going pale at the edges.

He had not asked them anything. He had not taken the schedule out. He had been in that room for three hours in the chair by the door with a book of his own and he had not written in it once, and when Elias Rook came out he held the pen up with the cap still on.

"Twenty-two items," Joon Park said, "and it is twenty-two, and I want to say one thing about this afternoon and then I am going to go and do my round."

"Go on."

"Three people came in off a street on Sunday with no notice of any kind and three people went out of a room today with a name in a book, an address on a page and a row behind their name that says *evidence held, page not found*," Joon Park said, "and that is the correct outcome and it is the whole of what a counter can do. Every question that was asked them this morning was about a page or a column or a date, and not one of the three of them was asked what they wanted, and I sat in that room for three hours and I know the difference between the two kinds of question, and I want the second kind written down somewhere by somebody, and I am not going to be the one who writes it, because I have been the one who writes things down all winter and I would be putting my own thumb on the scale."

"Who were they going to ask."

"Everybody," Joon Park said. "You, me, the woman at the health center, the man in the good coat, and eventually one of them, in a bad afternoon, in about four months, a man with a clipboard. And every one of those four would have had a form."

He got the 11 at ten past three and there was a length of paper four feet long somewhere in this district with about ninety names on it in about ninety hands, and he had seen it once, on a trestle by a door, on a Thursday night, from about eight feet away, and nobody had told him a thing about it, and the accident form was still on the gate at the yard with eleven names on it because nobody had told the foreman to take it down, and the night schedule was still on the gate with four rows and no addresses, and Monday was the first of the twenty-two working days the booking had left, and somewhere in a file in the Meridian a folder had a copy of a document in it that said the fourth of March in a year and could not be checked by anybody, and none of the three people who had come in off a street on Sunday had been asked what they wanted, and that was going to have to be good enough, and it was going to have to be somebody else's turn to be uncomfortable about.
