# Chapter 124 — A Form In A Drawer Since August

Cliff Marner was seventy-seven days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and seven days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and seven days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Monday morning, the twenty-second of December, on the twenty-second day of a run that had started on the twenty-ninth of November and had two Sundays out of it.

Twenty-two round trips at five pounds fifty, and a hundred and twenty-one pounds, and a Tuesday that cost him an hour of sleep a week for the rest of his life, and the order the costs came in had been travel and then sleep and then money every day since the beginning of December and would be until the end of this.

---

The clinic shut at two for the holidays and Rhoda Mistry was still in it at ten past two, and there were four people waiting on the bench outside who came in first, and she made him wait for them, which was correct.

She had a hard copy of the second outfall's discharge list folded in four on her desk and eleven names on the back of it in a ballpoint, and she did not read them out.

"They are people I am worried about who live alone," she said. "Sixty-one, seventy-four, eighty-four, two of them in their eighties. Three of them cannot do a stair at all. Two of them have a stair with a turn in it and a light that has not worked since the summer. That is the whole of what the list is and it is three years old in its first line and three months old in the rest."

"I am not going to write it down."

"I know you are not," Mistry said. "I am going to say the conditions and you are going to hear them and then you are going to make a face, and I am telling you now that I do not mind the face."

"Go on."

"It goes to the works," Rhoda Mistry said. "To the foreman, and to whoever signs the sheets, and to nobody else. Not to the cell. Not to a program. Not to a regional office. If it goes anywhere else I will know, because the cell is very good at asking and has never once asked me for the thing I have, and a man from a different office asked me for it in November on the grounds that he wanted to be complete, and I said no to him, and he was not in the least bit interested in the eleven people and I was correct to say no."

Elias Rook made the face.

"Go on," she said. "You have twenty minutes and then I have a person at half past two who is going to be here whether we are talking or not."

---

The yard was busy enough to be loud and Peart took the paper in the office with the door shut, which was not a thing anybody did in that office, and read the back of it standing up.

"That is a list of stairs," he said.

"It is a list of people and it is a list of stairs," Elias Rook said. "There is no third thing in it and no addresses on the front, and if anybody in this office writes an address on the front of that piece of paper I will take it back off them."

Peart looked at him for a second. Then he took a sheet of the works' own card stock out of the drawer, which is where every clearance card in that yard had ever been made, and he copied the eleven onto eleven cards, and he wrote a floor and a stair on each one in the block capitals the crew used, and he signed the bottom of every one of them in ink, which is what a foreman's initials are for.

"Ladder out," he said. "Not a carry. A ladder out, one at a time, and a bloke at the bottom of it, and if anybody argues with me about it I will put them on the ladder myself."

"Peart."

"I have been doing this nineteen years," Nev Peart said, "and I have had four people die on that ground in a clearance and I did not lose anybody on a stair, and I am not going to lose anybody on one in December, and I do not need a man from the Cinder Quarter to come and tell me what a stair is."

He put the eleven cards in a second wallet on the nail, next to the first one, and did not say anything else about it, and Elias Rook left him alone with them for about four minutes before he came out.

---

Nerys Hobbs was in the room off the canteen at half past three and she had the door shut before he had it open, and two teas were on the table because the woman from the canteen had brought two and had left the second side empty without being asked, which she did.

"You have got something," she said. "You have had it since you came through that door. You have got it in your coat and you have been holding your right arm against your side for two days and I would like it on this table and I would like it in about four sentences, because I have drawings at four o'clock."

Elias Rook put the eleven cards down, and he put them down one at a time, and he did not read any of the names out loud in a room.

"Eleven people on this alignment who cannot get down a stair in the dark," he said. "Peart has them as eleven cards. He is not carrying anybody. He is putting a ladder out."

Hobbs read two of the cards and turned one of them face down, which he noticed.

"That is the wrong way up," he said.

"Yes," Hobbs said, and put it back the right way up. "Now say the rest of it, and do not stand there arranging furniture."

"I am going to ask somebody for hands tomorrow morning," Elias Rook said. "I told you the terms of the other thing on the eleventh of December in this room, before I acted on it, and I am telling you the terms of this one now, in this room, before I act on it. This is a physician's hands, twenty-one years ahead. It is not mine. I am not asking Mara for anything, because the present and the future are two people and one of them is not a woman in this room."

"What do you want the hands for," Hobbs said.

"To keep somebody alive on a stair while somebody else carries them," Elias Rook said. "That is the whole of it. It is not a plan and it is not a method, and there is no list of anybody it works for, because I am not going to be the man who writes a list of who the hands go to, and that is the promise I am making to you now, in a room, and you may write it down if anybody ever gives you a form for it."

Hobbs put both hands flat on the table, which she had never once done in any room he had been in.

"Then let me tell you what is going to happen," she said, "and I am telling you in a room with a shut door because I said in a corridor in November that I would, and because you have been decent about that for a month and it costs me nothing to pay it back. You are going to be asked to prove that. Not by me. By whoever writes it down. And there is no form in the world in which a man's word about a woman he has never met fills a box, and so within a week there is going to be a piece of paper in that building with a heading on it and a line for the answer, and you are going to be asked to put a person's name in a box on a piece of paper, and you are going to be tempted, because a woman on this street has kept a list in her own hand for eleven years and been asked for it once, and it turns out that being asked is most of what there is."

"Somebody wrote that as a method," Elias Rook said.

"Mr. Anum wrote that as a method," Nerys Hobbs said, "and it is a good method, and it is why I have been able to work on this ground for twenty-two years, and it is also the exact size of the hole this program has been standing on for four months." She gathered the eleven cards into a stack and squared the edges. "I am not going to stop you. If I were you I would be at that door at nine o'clock tomorrow, because I have seen what a night clearance with a lamp on it and a crew that knows the stairs does, and I have seen what it does when they do not, and the difference is not in any of the documents in your coat."

---

He left the eleven cards on the table in front of Nerys Hobbs, who squared them, put them in her own drawer and shut it, and then he went up the outside stair at number six at twenty to four, which shook, and put his right hand on the rail and took it off again, because a man who puts his hand on a wall in a room he is about to have a conversation in is a man who has decided something in advance.

Selwyn Anum was forty-one and had the laptop open and a form on the trestle that had not been on the trestle at any time in three weeks.

"Where has that been," Elias Rook said.

"In a drawer," Anum said. "Since August. I have never had anything to put in it and I have never thrown it out, and if you ask me why I kept it I will tell you because the pilot authorization has a section headed *Acquisition and Return* and the section has four sub-headings and three of them are addressed to an operator and the fourth one is addressed to a program, and a program that has never written one of these has not decided that it will not need one, and a man in my position does not get to decide it will not need one."

It was one side on the program's own letterhead. Two lines of headings, a ruled space, and at the foot a place for a signature and a date and nothing else at all.

`SECURED SUBJECT`
`SUBJECT HELD: [ ................................ ]`

"There is a heading for who is held," Anum said, tapping the second one, "and there is no heading on that piece of paper for whether they agreed. That is not a complaint, that is a design. My design. I wrote it in August in an afternoon and I could not think what to call the other thing, so I did not call it, and it turns out that a form is exactly as honest as the man who fills it in."

"And if the answer is no?"

"Then I write it on the back," Selwyn Anum said. "There is nothing on the front. That is the whole of the conversation and I have now had it in a corridor and I would like you to leave."
