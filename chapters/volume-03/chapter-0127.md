# Chapter 127 — The Whole Thing Was Still There

Cliff Marner was eighty days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and ten days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and ten days in, and Elias Rook did not get on a bus on Thursday morning, the twenty-fifth of December, because it was Christmas Day and the yard was shut and the counter was shut and the board was shut and the yard's own card behind the glass said so in a typed line along the bottom of it.

Twenty-four days. That was the run, and it stopped there on Wednesday night, and there was no ceremony about it and nobody in the building marked it.

---

The four dark hours came at midnight and ended at four, and there were twenty-two resident cards on the rail and twenty-eight people in the building, and Inez Sato counted them without looking up because she had been doing that since September and it was the same number every day of the year including the day the roof came off a shop on the next street.

Petar Lisk read the board at seven in the morning, out loud twice.

Who edited the Kestrel response record. What the Office believed a single approved outcome would prevent, with the second half of that still a bracket with nothing under it. Who benefits when a branch is officially deleted and why does the form call it an error, in capitals, in a nine-year-old's hand. Under the last two, in a hand nobody else in the building had put there, 19 November.

He read it and then he put the pencil behind his ear the other way round and said, to nobody, that it was Christmas Day and that the whole thing was still there, and that he had got a tin of biscuits in the front room and that the tin was not a rule and could not be used as one.

Elias Rook stood in the doorway and listened without adding anything, the way he had added nothing on the twenty-first and would add nothing for the rest of his life, because the wall was full and somebody was going to have to build another one and it was not going to be anybody in that building.

The four minutes of Saturday night were in a book on a shelf and not on that wall, and would be there in a hundred years, and the woman who had said them had not been in this room and had not sent anything and would not hear about any of it.

---

There was a turkey in that building on Christmas Day because a woman called Marigold Truscott from a housing scheme on the far side of the station had brought one in at eleven in the morning wrapped in a blue blanket she intended to have back in January, and eight of the twenty-eight had worked on it in two kitchens and it was too big and somebody had cut it wrong and somebody else had cut it right.

Ru Sabar said it was the same as a thing she had eaten in 2039 and refused to say where. Cliff Marner was in the chair by the window with the blanket out of the store room and had a plate on his knee and did not eat most of it and was fed about a third of it by people who did not ask him whether he wanted any more. Petar Lisk carved it and got two slices out of the breast that were not a shape anybody recognized. The heater in the front room gave up about every nine minutes and four people had learned not to speak across it and on Christmas Day three of them spoke across it anyway.

Elias Rook had the notebook in his coat and the tube against the wall and no lamp and no list, and he had a plate, and he ate most of it, and the reason he could eat most of it was that a woman of sixty-three had read a thousand doors out loud at a bollard a week ago and a man of fifty-one had copied eleven names onto eleven pieces of card and signed the bottom of every one of them on Monday, and neither of those things had anything to do with him and both of them had made this room possible.

He did not think that sentence very often. He thought about it in the dark that night.

Petar Lisk brought the tin of biscuits out at about four in the afternoon and put it on the table on its side, the way a man does when he has carried a tin in a coat for six hours and does not want anybody to make a thing of it, and said that it was a tin and not a rule and could not be used as one, and then ate four of them in about a minute, and Cliff Marner ate two of the ones with the hard edges, and Ru Sabar told him the whole history of the tin, which is that it had belonged to a woman who had lived in the second flat on that landing until 2038 and had gone into a bin and had been in a skip until Petar had taken it out of the skip.

---

The rule was that Mara Okafor was not in that building on Christmas Day, and the reason was Saturday night, and the rule was four minutes long and had been made by the two of them in a corridor and had cost one of them the whole of an afternoon on Monday, and on Tuesday the rule had been kept and had not saved anybody anything.

She had a household of her own and a flat four streets from a hospital and somebody else's Christmas to be at, and he was not going to ring her at ten in the morning on the twenty-fifth of December, and he did not ring her at two, and he rang her at ten past four when the four dark hours had ended and the turkey had been eaten and the building had gone quiet in the way buildings go quiet on the day after the loudest thing they do all year.

It was two minutes and forty seconds.

"Nobody was hurt," he said. "Not one person. Eleven doors and eleven cards and a ladder out and a man at the bottom of it, and a foreman who got there at ten past seven to tell the clerk first, before he had said good morning."

"That is your list working," Mara said. "That is a woman who kept a list for three years and a man who put it in his own hand. It is not yours and you are not allowed to be proud of it in the voice you are using."

"I am not. I am saying it because I asked a woman in a room on Tuesday for hands I did not need, and the two of those facts are going to be in the same sentence in this conversation whether I put them there or not."

"That is the first true thing you have said about Monday," Mara said. "Do not go looking for a way to be forgiven in it. There is nothing to be forgiven and that is the part that will be hard in January, and you will want somebody to be angry at so that it stops being yours."

She said that she was going to be at a table with six people and one of them was a boy of nine who had had a bad autumn, and that the boy wanted to be told a story about somebody who did not have any powers at all, and that she had told him about a man from a tunnel with a left hand that did not work and a woman who said no to a number in a box, and that the boy had asked her whether the man was brave, and she had said she did not know and would find out.

"He is not," Elias Rook said. "Tell him whatever you like. You are the one with the table."

"Four minutes was the right rule," Mara said, and put the handset down, and did not say anything else that either of them would have to remember.

---

Joon Park came into the front room about eight with the custody schedule in his coat and the two dates on his fingers in pencil, because he has never once written a date on a pen and has never once said why.

"Two January and the sixteenth of January," he said. "There. I said them and it is Christmas Day and nobody in this room has asked me and I have said them anyway, because in three years somebody is going to want to know what our position was on Christmas morning and I am not going to be a man who says I said nothing because nobody asked."

"Who would want to know?"

"I do not know," Joon Park said. "That is the honest answer and it is also the only one we have got. A request for a log that is held in a regional office four to six weeks, and the near end is the second of January and the far end is the sixteenth, and neither of them is a day anybody in this building gets to hurry. The second of January is a Friday and so is the sixteenth, and I have been annoyed about that for a fortnight, and I have decided that it is not a reason to say anything. I have not said it sooner than I am saying it now and I will not say it before anybody asks."

He looked at the book on the shelf with the four minutes in it, and then he said the other thing, which he had clearly been carrying up the stairs.

"There is a file upstairs," he said, "of working logs, and there is a 1998 one on the top shelf in a box, and it has been up there since before I came, and there is a machine in that room that has not had a look at it in ten days, and if the room is shut then the machine does not know, and if anything is ever going to know it will be a person with a torch, and I have been thinking about that since the fourteenth and I have not said it to anybody because saying it out loud makes it a plan and a plan is a thing you can be asked about."

"Nobody is asking you," Elias Rook said.

"That is why I said it to you and not to Inez," Joon Park said, "and you will now go to bed and I will go to bed and neither of us is going to do anything about a box on a shelf."

---

They went to bed at about eleven on Christmas Day, and the board was on the wall behind him and the dark hours started at midnight and the run had been twenty-four days, and on Friday the yard was shut for Boxing Day and on Saturday the yard was shut and on Sunday the yard was shut and the card behind the glass said the twenty-ninth, and in that yard there were four nights left in a booking that ran to the fifth of February, and one of them was a Monday, and there was not one person in the building on Ash Street who had a torch and a ladder and a list of eleven, and the one person who could have given anybody four minutes on a stair had been asked about a different thing entirely, and had said no, in words, before anything was done with it, which was the correct way to say it, and it had cost him the thing he had gone to Tidegate twenty-four days running to find.
