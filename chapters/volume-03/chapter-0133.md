# Chapter 133 — A Notice With No Addresses On It

Cliff Marner was eighty-six days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and sixteen days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and sixteen days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Wednesday morning, the thirty-first of December, and the counter at number six was open until half past four and the yard was open and the board was open, and it was the last working day of the year.

The closure card was still behind the glass on the gate. Nobody had taken it down, and Peart had put something up beside it, which was a second sheet of the same stock with a drawing pin through the top corner and the drawing pin bent sideways.

Elias Rook read it standing on the pavement, in the rain, at ten to nine in the morning.

```
WORKS · NIGHT SCHEDULE · FOURTH STAGE TIDAL DEFENSE · ALIGNMENT
Issued 31 December 2042

 6 JAN  14–22 Marlow St                     crew 4
10 JAN  22–32 Rennard · 16–18 Murrow        crew 5
17 JAN  14–22 Cutbank Row · 31 Cutbank Row  crew 4
19 JAN  15–21 Rennard · 2–12 Cutbank Row    crew 4

Printed by the yard. Not a program document.
```

He read it three times, and on the third time he stopped reading the dates and started looking at what was not on it.

There were no door numbers on it. There were four nights, four blocks, four crew numbers and a line saying it was not a program document, and there was not one address on the whole sheet, and it had been pinned up by a foreman at eight o'clock in the morning in the rain for anybody in that district who wanted to know which night their street was.

---

"You have read that three times and you have still not come in," Nev Peart said.

"I have read it three times."

"It went up at eight and the woman at the newsagent has had a copy on her counter since about half eight because I gave her six of them," Peart said, "and by now it is in two shops and a cafe and it will be in the church hall on Saturday, and Mrs Postridge took one without asking me a single question, which tells you what that sheet is for."

"You printed it."

"I printed it," Peart said. "I typed it and the girl in the office printed it and I pinned it up. That is a yard doing its own night schedule and putting it where the street can see it, and it has taken me three weeks and two arguments and a woman from the board's communications telling me that anything on a gate had to go through her department first."

"What did you say to her."

"I said it was a date and a street and a number of men and that she was welcome to have it," Peart said, "and she sent it to a lawyer, and the lawyer sent it back in four days with a note that said the district communications plan covers published works information, and I wrote back one line, and the line said that a district communications plan is a thing the district has and this is a thing the works has, and one of those two has a stake in it and the other one is a man with a ladder."

He put the kettle on.

"And there are no door numbers on it," he said, "and before you ask me, that is not because I have been told not to put them on. There are no door numbers on it because I have got four men and twenty-one lamps in the store and nine doors on the first of them, and if I print nine door numbers on a sheet that a woman in the newsagent's shop will sell or hand out, then a person standing at that gate on Tuesday night can write down which house in that row a crew is going into, and there is a woman of eighty-four on Rennard who opens her door to anybody who knocks and I am not printing her door."

"That is the right decision."

"It is my decision and I made it in about nine seconds and I have thought about it since and I would make it again," Peart said. "It is also the reason that sheet on that gate is worth about a fifth of what it is worth. A block is a street. Fourteen to twenty-two Marlow Street tells a person in that street that Tuesday is Tuesday. It does not tell them anything about a house, and I have never printed a house in nineteen years and I am not starting in January because a man from the Cinder Quarter has started asking me questions."

---

Nerys Hobbs had the sheet for the sixth of January on the trestle in the survey office with the terminal running, and she wrote the date and the crew number on the first line and stopped there, and then she squared the pen and the cap and put both in her pocket.

"That line does not get signed until about half past six on the morning of the seventh," she said. "A sheet about a street gets signed by whoever was standing on it when it was walked. I am not signing a Tuesday on a Wednesday, and if anybody in an office wants my name on a Tuesday night in advance, they can come and watch me write it at ten past seven with the barriers down and the hour on it."

"You have read the notice," she said.

"I have read it on the gate."

"Then you know what is the most useful piece of paper in this district," Hobbs said, "and it is a sheet of A4 with four rows on it that a foreman typed himself, and the reason it is useful is that it is the only document about this ground in existence that was written by the people doing the work and pinned up for anybody in the street to argue with. Everything else in this district was written by somebody who was not going to be on the road on the night, and the reason that matters is that a document you can argue with is a document with a date on it, and a document with a date on it is a document that can be wrong, and every other piece of paper about this alignment has been right about itself on the day it was drawn and there is not one of them anybody in that street could have picked up."

She put the clipboard down.

"And I want you to hear the second half of that from me and not work it out," she said, "because I know what you have been doing with your face since the twenty-ninth of November. The works' notice does not say who lives in those four blocks. It says which street and which night. The day somebody prints the door numbers on it is the day this stops being a works' notice, and on that day it is a program document, and the program will want to see it, and the program will want it in a form, and I have watched a form take a district apart twice in twenty-two years and I am not doing it in January because it would be tidy."

"You put that up at eight o'clock in the rain," Elias Rook said. "The woman at number thirty-one does not know her door is on the seventeenth."

"She wanted to be last on the seventeenth and I told her she could be," Peart said, "and she has not thanked me for it and she is not going to, and I did not tell her what the notice would say, because on the twentieth of November I did not know what the January booking was, and a foreman who promises a woman a number before the number exists is a man selling something." He looked at the sheet through the gate. "She will read it on Saturday like everybody on that row, and I do not know whether she minds, and I have not asked her, and if she minds that is her business and not a thing I can fix by taking a nail out of a gate."

"You would not take it out."

"I would put a second sheet up saying the seventeenth is subject to change, and that is the end of what a gate is for," Peart said. "After that it is a leaflet, and a leaflet is a thing somebody can be told afterwards that they were misinformed by."

---

He stood at the gate in the rain at half past four with the tube under his arm and worked the arithmetic out and it took about four minutes, which was the amount of time it takes to walk from the yard gate to the end of the alignment and back.

On the twenty-seventh of December, at a bollard on Cutbank Row, a woman of sixty-three had read a sheet of her own doors out loud to about fourteen people, and he had been standing on that pavement with the four December nights on a card in his coat and had not given them to her, and had gone home and been right about it, and Inez Sato had told him it was a good arrangement and that he had arrived at it three days late.

He had been right about one thing, and it had turned out to be the smallest thing available.

The four January dates were on a nail at the yard gate and would be in the newsagent's shop and in the church hall on Saturday morning. She would have all four of them by ten past nine on Saturday without him, and she would not have had to ask, and the thing she would not have, and could not have, and that nobody in the world was going to print on a sheet pinned to a gate, was which house.

And the only copy of that in the district was on a kitchen table on Cutbank Row in a hand that had been doing it since the fourth of March 2031, and it had never once been asked of her, and he was not going to ask her for it, and when he worked out why he was not going to ask her for it the reason was no longer a rule he had made in a room in December and was told off by a woman of thirty-one for arriving at three days late.

It was that he had asked a woman in a room on the twenty-third of December for a thing, out loud, in the words he would have used on a stranger, and it had taken eleven seconds, and eleven people were going to be hurt in the second week of January because of it, and he was not going to walk up to a table in a kitchen and put a second version of that sentence on it.

He got the 11 at ten past five and it was the last bus that got him in before the dark, and the thread was off in the corridor and off over the water and had been on all day under a yard he had been standing in, and he had told a woman on Tuesday that he was going to ask at ten past six on Thursday, and she had said yes at three minutes and then had said she was not going to be in it, and there was a notice with four rows on it drying in the rain behind him and a sheet of a woman's own doors on a table he had no business at and a foreman's four men for Tuesday.
