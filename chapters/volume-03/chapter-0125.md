# Chapter 125 — Two Hundred And Eighty-Five Addresses

Cliff Marner was seventy-eight days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and eight days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and eight days in, and Elias Rook was on the far bank at ten to six on Tuesday morning, the twenty-third of December, because the depot ran Tuesdays and Fridays at half past six and the Tuesday half was his and had been since the end of October.

R. Dunleavy did not do the talking part of it. The needle went up and stayed up and he wrote two figures in a book in his own hand and turned the book round so the page faced the man who had brought the sheet. Twenty minutes. Elias Rook pinned the sheet with his right forearm because the left hand was gone and would not have held it, and the Tuesday cost him an hour of sleep a week for the rest of his life, and he got the 11 at ten past eight with the folder under his right arm.

---

The room above number six was booked in his name from half past nine until one. There was a trestle, two rolls in their cardboard tube, a laptop shut, and the Retention Schedule of the second of December open at page two with the footnote in italics facing the ceiling, because Selwyn Anum had been checking a figure against it and had not shut it.

Joon Park came at twenty past nine. Anum came at half past and did not sit down. Wendy Tallow came at ten to ten because Elias Rook had asked for her, and he had asked for her because on Saturday night he had told a room that the people who pay for a thing go in the room, and the person who paid for this was a program officer of thirty-four with a hand on a book at the counter, and so he had invited the program into his own question and knew it while he was doing it.

Mara Okafor came in last, at ten past ten, in a coat and with her bag, and shut the door behind her.

"Say it," she said.

"I am going to ask," Elias Rook said. "That is the whole of what I am telling you. Not the ask. The asking. I am going to put a question to a woman twenty-one years ahead this morning and I am going to ask it in this room, and I am going to stop talking and count four minutes, and if you have not said yes inside the four minutes I will not ask at all."

He stopped talking. He counted. The heater on the landing was not in that room and he heard it anyway. The trestle had an overlay on it with forty-four addresses along two streets and a foreman's initials in ink at the foot of a slip, and he looked at the woman instead of at the table, because he had been told to count and not to arrange furniture.

At two minutes and forty seconds Mara Okafor said, "I am not going to be a person you ask for permission to use. Go and ask her. I want to be in this room when you do it, because I am the one who has to watch you do it, and because I am the only person in this room who has any idea what it costs."

"That is a yes."

"That is a yes to being told," Mara said. "It is three and a half minutes and it is inside your four. Now go and ask her, and do it in the words you would use on a stranger, and do not put this page in it."

---

He did not put the page in it.

What he did was worse than that, and he did it in front of five people, and it took eleven seconds.

He put his right hand down hard on the wood of the trestle, fingers spread, where the overlay lay, and he said out loud: "There are eleven people on this ground who cannot get down a stair in the dark, and I am going to be in a building tonight when eleven doors on this alignment are opened, and I am asking you — is there a woman twenty-one years ahead of me who would put her hands on those eleven people while somebody else carries them."

Joon Park did not move. Wendy Tallow's hand went to the notebook on the table and stopped there and did not pick it up. Anum said, "Mr. Rook," in a voice with nothing in it, and Elias Rook did not turn round.

Then the seam came the way it always came, without any sound he could have described to anybody, and the wood under his hand went cold and then went about half a degree warmer, and the offer arrived in the plain shape the emergency network used when it could carry one safely.

It sat in the middle of five people with the laptop shut and the page open at page two.

"A hand," Anum said. "That is a hand. That is her name and her hand and it is sitting in my room and none of the five of us has a box for it."

"Mr. Rook," Wendy Tallow said. "I am going to have to write this down. That is my job and I am not asking your permission. It goes in the counter log tonight, in ink, in the present tense."

"Write it," Elias Rook said.

He lifted his hand off the trestle. The thread under it was still there, faint and exact, on ground he was being asked about, and it did not stop, and nobody had asked it to and he had not asked it to.

---

"Anum," Elias Rook said. "I am going to read the terms out loud, and then I am going to stop, and I am telling you now that I am going to read them and not afterwards, and that is step four and it is Mr. Vale's and I am using it late."

"You are using it in the wrong room," Anum said, "and Mr. Vale said a method is only worth anything if somebody else uses it, and you are about to find out what happens when the somebody else is the one being asked. Go on."

Elias Rook read the terms out loud to the room, in the words they were written in, to the five people who would have to live with what was done with them. When he came to the boundary he read it twice, because the second half of it was *do not use my hand to make a schedule come out right*, and the schedule was open at page two on the same table with its footnote in italics, and nobody in that room pointed that out and it did not need pointing out.

Then he said: "I have one question I am entitled to ask and I am going to ask it once."

He asked it: "Is there a version of this that you would do?"

And the answer came in the way the offer had come, in plain words and assembled out of her, and it was in that room before anything had been done with it, which is the only order in which it was ever any use to anybody.

"You have written down what happens to them before you have asked me," the future Mara Okafor said, "and I am not going to put my hands into a plan that already knows. I have spent twenty-one years refusing to write a rule and only ever writing a decision, and a decision is about one person at a time, and there are two hundred and eighty-five addresses on that page and I have not met one of them."

---

Nobody in the room spoke for about nine seconds, and then Wendy Tallow said, "That is the log entry. That is exactly what goes in the log and I am not putting it in my own words, and I am not going to summarise it either, and if anybody in this city wants to know what a woman said in a room above a shop this morning, it is that, and it is in ink tonight, and it is in the present tense."

Selwyn Anum opened the drawer and took out the form he had kept since August. He filled in the date in the box at the foot, and the hour, and ruled a line under the two headings, and then he stopped with the pen down and looked at the front of his own form for a while.

"There is no field for no," he said, to nobody. "There has never been a field for no."

He turned it over and wrote it on the back, in the same ink, with the date and the hour, and signed it, and put the form face down on the trestle and squared it with two fingers.

"It is the same rule you refused to break in November," Anum said to Elias Rook, "and I wrote the form, and I want to say one thing about that and then I never want to speak to you about it again. On the back of that piece of paper, in my hand, tonight, is the first honest sentence anybody in this pilot has produced, and it is there because I did not build a box for it. I would like somebody in an office a long way from here to think about what that means and to say it out loud somewhere I cannot hear."

---

Mara Okafor did not say anything for a while, and then she said it to Elias Rook, and she said it in front of the other four, and it was not quiet and it was not loud.

"You asked her about them," she said. "Not about you, not about her. You put a question to a woman about eleven people she has never seen, and the question was an instruction, and you did it eleven seconds after I told you not to put the page in it. I am not going to be the person who tells you that was wrong, because you are the one who has to be able to say it, and you knew it in the eleventh second and let the eleventh second go by because you wanted her to say yes."

"I wanted her to say yes."

"You wanted her to say yes to eleven people on a page," Mara said. "And I want to be very clear with you about the other thing, because I have thought about it for the four minutes. I am not entitled to be angry at a woman twenty-one years ahead of me who has answered for her own capacity. She does not know this room and she does not know the form and she is not mine and she is not yours, and I am not going to stand in this room and defend her, and I am also not going to stand in this room and be grateful to her, because being grateful is the same shape of theft as being angry. Nobody inherits me. Not this one. Not in a worse month than this one."

"Then what is the rule for," Elias Rook said.

"The rule is for you," Mara Okafor said, "and you kept it. You told me, you stopped, you counted, and I said yes inside your four minutes, and if you had asked me one more question about your own capacity I would have said yes again. You kept the rule and then you asked a different question. That is not a broken rule. That is a man who has learned a thing and gone on doing the other thing anyway, and I watched him do it from nine feet away, and I am not going to be the one who explains it to you tonight."

---

The room came apart the way rooms do, in pieces, and nobody left it in a temper. Anum shut the laptop and put the schedule back on the shelf it lived on and said that it was not evidence of anything tonight. Tallow asked whether the four of them would step out and let her read the entry back before she wrote it, and read it back, and it was four lines and the last one was *no answer offered, no use made, one question asked*, and Elias Rook said that was correct, and she wrote it.

Joon Park did not take out the custody schedule. He had it in his coat and it stayed in his coat, because twenty-two items were twenty-two items and a thing said in a room above a shop at half past eleven in the morning was not going to be the twenty-third.

"Mr. Rook," he said at the door, "you have lost the cover of a rule you helped make, and I am not going to soften it and I am not going to say I told you so."

Then he went down a stair that shook.

Elias Rook stood in a room with a trestle in it and a form face down and an offer in the middle of the air that had cost a woman in another year eleven seconds of his mouth, and the reading was still there under the wood, faint and exact, and nobody had taken it off him, and the only thing used in the whole of the morning was him.
