# Chapter 142 — She Would Have To Say Yes

Cliff Marner was ninety-five days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and twenty-five days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and twenty-five days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Friday morning, the ninth of January, and the counter at number six was open until half past four and the yard was open and the board was open, and it was a working day inside a booking of twenty-eight with a night on it.

Joon Park had said the thing on the bus and then had not said anything for eleven minutes, which for him was a speech.

"I do not think she will say yes," he said. "I have thought about it since Thursday night and that is where I have got to. You are going to ask a woman of sixty-three to let four dead people out of a margin, and every reason she has for saying no is eleven years old and has never once let her down, and I am telling you on a bus because I am not going to be the one who has to watch you find out."

"You have written them on your hand three times."

"I have rewritten them on my hand three times because the first one went," Joon Park said, "and if you ask me where I got them I am going to say a woman on Cutbank Row wrote it down and that is the whole of what I will say, and I have said that in a room already and I am not going to improve on it for a bus."

---

Twenty-two Cutbank Row is a two-room flat with a boiler that moves furniture in the next room, upstairs over a shop that sells door furniture, and there is a chair on the table-side with a blanket over the back of it that is not for visitors and Winifred Alagoa said so before Elias Rook could move it.

She made tea twice, because the first pot was for measuring.

"Sit there," she said. "That is the arm that works. This is January and it is my boiler and you will be here long enough. And before either of you says anything, I have had a letter."

Elias Rook stopped in the doorway.

"It came on Wednesday," Alagoa said. "It is not a letter from a person. It is a printed card with a program reference in the corner and it says that a person with a document of record in the district may have that document verified at any time at the counter, and that verification protects the holder, and it has a box on the back for a signature and a line for a date and a line that says *document surrendered for check*."

"How many of those have gone out."

"Two hundred and six," Alagoa said. "That is not a guess, that is the number on the top of the sheet, and I have counted them, because counting things off a counter is the only thing I have ever been any good at." She put the second pot on. "It came on Wednesday and it has a photograph of a woman at a counter on it and she is smiling, and it says *your interests protected*, and Mr. Rook, I have been in this street thirty-eight years and I have had nine people be pleasant to me in a way that was a way of getting something."

"Which is why you have not sent anything."

"I have sent nothing, and I am not going to, and I am telling you both so that you cannot go and tell anybody in a building that I have." She looked at Joon Park. "You are the one with a list."

"I have twenty-two items on it," Joon Park said, "and I am not going to tell you what they are, and this is not one of them and it is not going to become one of them."

---

Then she put the sheet down on the table, folded in three, the way she has put it down for eleven years, and did not open it.

"Ask me the thing you have come to ask," Alagoa said, "and ask me it properly, and I will tell you what I think about it while you are still standing in my kitchen, because afterwards you will both be saying it to each other on a road and I will not be able to hear you."

Elias Rook asked her properly. He said that there were four people dead at the ninth of November and their names were in half an inch of margin in a kitchen pencil and that nobody had said so out loud since; that the two of them had come to ask whether she would let the four be used, in a room, in a document, in a district, and that neither of them was going to do anything with the answer until she had been asked first; and that the ask would cost her something and he did not know what and was not going to guess at it in front of her.

She did not answer for a while. The boiler went through its whole business.

"You have asked me properly," Alagoa said, "and I am going to say yes, and I am going to say it in a small way, and you are going to have to sit down and hear the smallness of it, because in about four seconds you had your face ready for a large yes and there is not going to be one."

He sat down.

"Two of them," she said. "I will give you two names to put in front of people, and I will write them out myself, in my own hand, in a column with a name in it, and that is what comes out of this room. The other two are not going on a sheet that goes on a nail in a shop."

"What is the difference between the two and the two."

"The difference," Alagoa said, "is that the two I am giving you are the two that a person on this row can still say out loud without being asked anything, and the other two are not, and I have been going round those four doors since November and I know which is which, and the two I am not giving you are the two whose families are not on this side of the cut any more, and if I put a name on a piece of paper in a shop then it is a fact about four strangers in a district that has already had four strangers in it, and I will not do that."

"That is your decision and not mine."

"It is my decision and I have known it since about eleven o'clock last night and I did not sleep much after it," Alagoa said. "And I am not being brave. I am being the same way I have been since 2031, which is that a person I cannot stand behind on my own road is not going on my paper."

She got up and opened the sheet and turned the second fold back and half an inch of margin came into the room that has been in no room and no system in the world except this kitchen since the ninth of November, and she read four names out loud once, in her own voice, in her own kitchen, at about eleven in the morning on a Friday in January, and the two of them wrote nothing down.

Meredith Prothero, eighty-one, of three Sallow Court. Gwilym Rees, sixty-six, of eleven. Nesta Morgan, seventy-nine, of nineteen. Aneurin Pugh, fifty-eight, of twenty-seven, who was the top of the block and whose van had been outside the door on the eighth of November and was not outside the door on the ninth.

"The last one is the one people will ask about," Alagoa said. "A man of fifty-eight with a van. I am not going to tell you a single thing about him and you are not going to find out, because I wrote his name down and that is the whole of what I did with him."

Elias Rook stood up.

---

Joon Park had gone across the road to the shop that sells door furniture at number six about four minutes earlier, on the grounds that two men and a sheet of addresses and a kettle in a two-room flat were one too many, and he was standing at the counter of that shop with his back to the window when the woman came up the stairs, and he did not come out of it.

She was about thirty and she had a folder and a good coat and one of the two hundred and six cards in her hand, and she had clearly been on that landing before, and she said *Mrs. Alagoa* in a voice that had been used in a room and not on a doorstep, and then she said that she was from the district office and that she had come about a document and that it would take two minutes and that she had a form.

Alagoa did not go inside. She stood in her own doorway with the door open eight inches behind her and did not put her hands anywhere.

"Two minutes," the woman said. "Verification protects you. It is on the card."

"My mother lived in that flat before me and there is not one thing in this doorway that anybody has asked me about in eleven years," Alagoa said, "and you have come up those stairs with a printed card and a photograph of a woman who is not you, and the sheet is on my table and it is going to stay on my table, and if you put one line of that page in a book anywhere in this city I will find out, and I will not find out by asking you."

The woman looked at the card in her hand and then at the woman in the doorway.

"It is verification," she said.

"It is a card with a reference in the corner," Alagoa said, "and there is a box on the back of it for a signature and a line that says the document is surrendered, and I have been on this row for thirty-eight years and I know what it is to be asked to surrender something in a doorway, and I am not doing it, and it is not about your office."

"That is not a decision I can take on the step."

"No," Alagoa said. "It is mine and I have taken it and it has nothing to do with you, and I would like you to go down those stairs, because I am sixty-three and it is cold and I have a boiler."

The woman went down the stairs and across the road and into the shop at number six and put the card on the counter and said, *she would not give it over*, in the tone of somebody reporting the weather, and Joon Park said, *thank you*, and did not ask a single follow-up question, and did not write it down, and Elias Rook did not write it down either, and neither of them said one word about it then or afterwards, and it went into a day like any other day in that street.

---

There is one more thing about the ninth of January and it is in an envelope and it cost nothing.

The depot runs Tuesdays and Fridays at six thirty in the morning. That is on the notice and it was on the notice on the twenty-seventh of November and it is the notice as it stands now, and the Friday half is an inventory half, and it has been empty in his week since the twenty-first of November, which is the last Friday it ran, and the one before that he was late for and could not get back, and nobody has taken it off the notice and nobody has ever said so to him out loud.

The letter came on the Friday, in the same hand as every other letter the depot has ever sent him, and it is one paragraph.

"Fourteen January is a Wednesday," Elias Rook said, to the yard at about four o'clock in the afternoon, in a voice that got carried. "So it is from the fifteenth that the hose is on the Thursday and I have the Tuesday, and the Friday that is left is the inventory and I have not had the inventory since the twenty-first of November, and nothing in that says my name."

"It does not say your name," Peart said. "It does not say anybody's name. It is a slot moving because a man in an office has decided the hose goes on the Thursday, and that is the ninth letter that depot has sent anybody in this district in a year and not one of them has ever been about anybody in particular."

"And it cost him an hour a week in October and it is going to cost him an hour a week in March, and there is nothing in that letter about him at all," Joon Park said, "and that is the correct arrangement and I would like it minuted in a book somewhere, because in about four years somebody is going to write that the man was re-rostered, and he was not, he was never touched, and the letter says so in nine words and I have read it nine times."

He put the letter back in his coat, where the envelope to Nell had been since September, with the reason four words long on it, and went to bed.
