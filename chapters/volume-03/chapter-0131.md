# Chapter 131 — Five Door Numbers

Cliff Marner was eighty-four days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and fourteen days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and fourteen days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Monday morning, the twenty-ninth of December, with the tube under his right arm and five pounds fifty in his hand, because the run was over and had been over since the Wednesday before Christmas and a man does not start one again because the yard has reopened.

The closure card was still in its frame behind the glass on the yard gate, and the typed line along the bottom of it was still in it, and the yard behind it was open and had two lorries in and a third waiting at the barrier for a slot.

`YARD CLOSED 25, 26, 27, 28 DEC · REOPENS 29 DEC`

Nobody had taken it down. It had been typed in a hurry in December and it was now the only thing behind the glass that was not true, and there was no procedure in the world that made a person take down a notice once the notice had stopped being about the day.

---

Nerys Hobbs had the booking open on the trestle in the survey office when he came in, and it was a Monday, and the yard was doing the thing it did on a Monday, which was everything at once and badly.

"Five till the fifth of February," she said, before he had got his coat off. "It opens on the fifth with Sundays off, and that is twenty-eight working days. Four nights in it and the first of them is Tuesday the sixth, and that is eight days from now, and this morning I have had a contractor on the phone asking me whether the street has to be shut on the sixth for the reading or on the first, which tells me that nobody in that office has read their own paperwork, and I have read theirs."

"What have you told him."

"I have told him the first," Hobbs said. "And I have told him that if a vehicle comes down that street on the sixth instead of the first then I will sign a sheet for a night clearance on ground nobody has read, and I will not do it and he will have to do it without me."

She put a hand on the planner on the wall without looking at it, the way a person touches a thing they have put up themselves.

"The fourth reading is Thursday," she said. "Ten past six, street shut from twenty past six, the reader on the alignment at a quarter past seven, the slip back with me about eleven. It is inside the contractor's window, and the argument about the window has been running since the twenty-ninth of November and I have now stopped having it."

"Who fixed it."

"The method officer fixed it," Hobbs said, "on Friday, in one line, in a room with three people in it, and he did not consult the works and he did not consult me and I am not going to complain about it, because a man who fixes a date inside a window instead of arguing about it in a corridor has saved me about a fortnight."

---

He asked her for it at twenty past ten in the room off the canteen with the door shut, because he had learned that much and had learned it in December.

"I want to be on the block on the sixth," Elias Rook said. "On the road. I am not asking to go up anything and I am not asking to be in a house. I will carry a ladder, I will hold a door, I will hold a light, I will do whatever a man in a coat is allowed to do. I have been on a stair in the dark and I have been the one who counted, and I am the only person in this district who has been that and I would like to be allowed to be that again."

Hobbs looked at him for long enough that he began to think she was going to say yes, and she did not.

"No," she said.

"That is a no to what."

"It is a no because there are four men on that crew and they have done it forty times and you have done it once, and the fifth man on a stair at eleven at night is not a help, and if that block goes wrong on the sixth of January then the last thing anybody on that road is going to want is a stranger from two miles off with a torch he bought in a shop on Christmas Eve." She picked her helmet up off the chair. "I have signed a sheet for every night of that booking and my name is at the bottom of it, and the night the sheet goes wrong is the night somebody reads my name and not yours."

"Fine."

"It is not fine and you know it is not fine, and I have said no anyway, and I am not going to soften it by offering you something else instead, because you would take the something else and call it a yes by Friday." She put the helmet under her arm. "What you are is a man on the road. That is allowed and it is worth about a fifth of what you think it is worth and I am not going to be the one who tells you otherwise at six o'clock in the evening with a torch in your hand."

"Then what am I allowed," Elias Rook said.

"You are allowed to stand at the bottom of a ladder and be the one who counts," Nerys Hobbs said, "and to be the one who is already in the doorway when the door opens, and to be four minutes slow about getting out of the way, and nothing else at all. I will not give you a key and I will not give you a card and you will not be on the works' sheet, and if you turn up on that road on Tuesday night I will not stop you and I will not have put you there."

She went out and shut the door behind her, which she had not done the last four times.

He walked 14 to 22 Marlow Street in the middle of the day because the works had a card for every door in that block and there were five door numbers on it and he wanted to know what five meant.

The numbers were five. Fourteen, sixteen, eighteen, twenty, twenty-two, in that order, on one side, and the odd numbers on the other side were still painted under the sills.

Number eighteen had a woman at the door with a folded pram against the wall. She looked at him, and then at the yard beyond him, and then back at him, and she did not open the door wider, which was correct.

"You are the man who has been in that district three weeks," she said.

"I have."

"You are going to be here on the sixth."

"I am going to be on the road on the sixth."

She looked at the row behind him, up and down, the way a person does before they say a number they have not said out loud before.

"There are nine kitchens in those five doors," she said. "There are nine and there has been nine since about 1978, and there is no number on any paper anywhere that says nine, and the woman at twenty has a room she lets, and the two above eighteen are one household and have been since before I came."

"I am not going to write it down."

"I know you are not," she said, "which is why I am saying it to you and not to the man in the high-visibility vest, and you can do what you like with it, and I would rather it went in your ear than in that office, because that office will want a number and then it will want a reason for the number and then it will want a date for the reason, and by then it will be a fact about a form instead of a fact about my street."

He did not write it down. He had the notebook out of his coat at the bottom of the row and he did not open it, and she watched him not open it, and neither of them said anything else about it.

---

The shift book was on the end of the bench under its rubber band and the terminal in the survey office was working and nobody went near the book, and Elias Rook did not go near it either, and did not think about it, which took him about four seconds of effort and he was aware of the effort.

Nev Peart was making the tea he had not made better in nineteen years.

"What is a lamp for," Elias Rook said.

Peart put the kettle down.

"That is a stupid question and you have thought about it before you asked it," he said. "You have been carrying that thing round in your coat since Christmas and you have not put it on a table and you have not put it in a book and you have not put it in that yard's inventory, and you want me to tell you what it is for so that you can stop hoping about it."

"Yes."

"A lamp is for a door," Peart said. "That is all it is. It is for a door that has been open for nine minutes while two people get somebody down a stair and somebody has to stand in the frame and hold the light on the other two. A crew carries four of them and they are on the lorry and they come off the lorry at seven and they go back on at six in the morning. A lamp is not a man. A lamp does not know whose door it is and a lamp cannot be asked whether anybody wants one, and if you put your lamp on my lorry I will have to write it on a sheet and then somebody can read the sheet, and I have spent nineteen years making sure that the only things on my lorry are mine."

"Understood."

"You have understood nothing," Peart said, "and you will understand it at about two o'clock in the morning on Tuesday, and I am not going to help you understand it sooner, and that is me being kind and it is also me doing my job."

He picked the kettle up again.

"Twenty-one of them are going to need one of mine on Tuesday," he said. "And I have twenty-one and I have four men, and I have had that since November, and nobody has ever asked me how I manage it, and the reason nobody has asked is that they think a door is a household."

---

At four o'clock Selwyn Anum was in the corridor outside the survey office with a laptop shut under his arm and a cup of tea he had not drunk, and he was forty-one and had not slept properly since the twenty-ninth of November.

"Thursday," he said. "Ten past six, street shut from twenty to, the reader is on the alignment at a quarter past seven and the output is with me about eleven. That is inside the window. I fixed it on Friday in one line and I have not told the works and Hobbs has told me she does not mind, and I want to say one thing about that before you say yours."

"Go on."

"The reading does not care who is standing on the street," Anum said. "That is not a criticism of you and it is not a reason to stay away. It is a vehicle on a shut street and a set of numbers off it, and the numbers will be the same at ten past six in the morning as they will be at four in the afternoon, and there is no version of you being on that street that changes one of them. I am telling you so that when you are standing there in the cold at six in the morning you do not spend the morning doing arithmetic about what you are for."

Elias Rook thought about that for a second, and it was true, and it was the first time anybody had given him permission to want to be somewhere and told him in the same breath that it would make no difference, and he had not known that those two things came as a pair.

"What time are you going to be on the ground," he said.

"About ten past six," Anum said, "because I have to be on it before it shuts, and I have to be the one who signs the check box."

"Then I will be on the ground at ten past six," Elias Rook said, "and I am not asking you for anything and I have not asked you for anything since the twenty-third of December, and if that is the wrong answer you can tell me and I will go and stand somewhere else."

"You are welcome on the ground," Anum said, "and nobody asked you, and I would like both of those on the record in whichever of your notebooks you have not written in today."

He went back into the survey office with the laptop still shut.

Elias Rook got the 11 at half past four and sat on the far side of the cut with five pounds fifty spent and an hour of nothing behind him, and eleven minutes each way had been the first cost every day since the twenty-ninth of November, and the sleep had been the second, and the money the third, and it had not once come in the other order, and there were four nights in January and a reading on Thursday morning and nothing whatever in his hands.
