# Chapter 148 — A Single Chosen Future Is Kinder

Cliff Marner was one hundred and one days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and thirty-one days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and thirty-one days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past five on the evening of Thursday the fifteenth of January, and there were about two hundred people in the church hall off Marden Row and there was a working microphone on a folding table and a kettle in the corner, and Marged Prosser had got it started at seven minutes past seven in about ninety seconds and had then sat down beside the table, which is not a thing a chair of a parents' group has to do.

There was a sheet of paper on a trestle by the door.

It was two feet long and it was not ruled and it had four columns drawn on it in pencil, and about forty people had already written on it in about forty different hands, and the first column was the address and the fourth column was empty on every single line, and somebody had written a heading over the fourth column in capitals and underlined it twice, and the heading was a name.

---

Tobias Ferrand came at five to seven and did not come to the front.

He is fifty-two and he had been a duty flood officer in 2027 and 2031 and 2036, and in December he had stood in this room and said a number out loud twice, and he stood at the back for four minutes listening, and then Marged Prosser said *can you come up, Mr. Ferrand, we are going to lose the hour*, and he came up and put the microphone in his own hand and held it at chest height.

"I am the Regional Director of the Bellwether Pilot Program," he said, "and that is what our paperwork says, and it is not what the people who built it say. **They call it the Bellwether Choir.** I am not going to explain the name tonight and there is nobody in this room who could stop me if I tried, and I have been in this district nine years and I have never once used it in a room, and I am using it now because I am about to tell you what I think this program actually is and I would rather do that under the name it has than under the name on the stationery, and if anybody in an office in the Meridian wants to write to me about the word they can have my answer, which is that I used it."

He did not say the word again for the rest of the evening, and the program did not correct him, and the leaflet on the chair at number six said BELLWETHER PILOT PROGRAM and the schedule said BWP and the verification log said Bellwether, and nobody in that hall noticed any of it, and it was not noticed afterward either.

"I am going to take questions and I am going to answer all of them, including the ones I do not like, and if I do not know one I am going to say I do not know one in this room rather than in a letter.

"So. Ask."

---

He asked the round himself, which is the thing that made him good at it, and answered eleven before anybody else spoke, and wrote nothing down, and told a woman that the school was not in the alignment and refused to promise it in a hall again, and then a woman of about thirty at the third row said, in a voice that carried, "You are the man who is killing us. Say the number."

"**Two hundred and eighty-five.**" He said it into the collar, the way he had in December. "That is the number I said in this room in December and it has not changed and I am not going to pretend it has. Two hundred and eighty-five addresses are classified `TRANSITIONAL` on a schedule I signed. **I say that number out loud every time and I do not apologize for it,** and the people who would like me to apologize are mostly people who have not lost anybody this year, and I have thought about that sentence for a month and I am going to say it anyway because it is true and because it is the only argument I have got."

The room did not move for him and did not move against him.

Then he put the microphone down on the table, not off, and stood behind it.

"You want me to hold uncertainty open. I was the duty flood officer in 2031 and I stood in a school gym at ten past three in the morning with two reports and forty-one people, and I could not tell them which one to stand in, and they stood in the one I was standing in. **Forty-one of them are alive. In the other report that school is under nine feet of water and there is no list anywhere of who was in it.**"

The hall was extremely quiet. He took the microphone up once in the middle of it and said one sentence into it that nobody at the back could hear, and put it down again, which was the first time Elias Rook had seen him do anything with a microphone that was not for the microphone's sake.

"You have spent a year producing lists and asking people to sit in the uncertainty while somebody writes down that they chose to. My program produces a district that is still standing and a list of who was in it, and the cost of the list is two hundred and eighty-five addresses, and I do not apologize for that number and the people who would like me to are mostly people who have not lost anybody this year."

"I have been wrong twice that I can produce and I have both in writing and I offered them in December. The first was in 2039 on the eastern bank and I was right about the ground and wrong about the rows. The second was this one, and I knew I was getting it wrong the day I signed it, and I have thought about that for a month and I am not going to soften the order those two things happened in."

"Mr. Ferrand." It was a man of about forty at the back, in a works jacket. "The retention schedule. The board's making a determination tomorrow. Is it going to stand or is it going to go."

"That is not mine," Ferrand said. "It is not my instrument, I do not control it, and I have not been asked for my view, and I am not going to guess at it from a platform about a thing I do not decide. I will tell you what I do know: the reading stands, the reading has stood since September, the reading is the reason that district is still standing, and there is not one person in this hall tonight who is going to be told to stand anywhere different because of what happens tomorrow."

---

He said a second thing at about ten to nine, and he said it to the room and not to anybody, and it is the sentence he will be remembered for and he did not know it.

"There is a version of this where you get to keep the uncertainty," Ferrand said, "and I am not going to stand here and tell you it is not kinder, because a single chosen future is kinder than what we have. It is kinder. That is not a trick and it is not a threat and I am not asking you to vote for anything. It is kinder for the people who are forty-one people in a school gym at ten past three in the morning, and it is worse for the people whose address is on a line somebody drew on a different day, and those two facts are the same size and they are both true and I have not been able to make one of them smaller in nine years, and I have stopped trying, and that is what I have come to say to you on a Thursday night in January."

He looked for somebody and found nobody and looked for somebody else.

"**Mr. Rook. You have never once been the man in the room when the water was coming and the reports disagreed. Everything you have built is a way of never having to be that man.**"

Two hundred people turned round and looked at a man in a coat at the side of the room, and he had a folder under his arm that he had not opened.

"No," Elias Rook said. "I have never had to."

He put the folder down on the chair, because holding it in front of his chest would have been a sentence, and he could not afford a sentence that night.

"And that is not because I am better at it," he said. "It is because there were twenty-eight people in a hall in the Cinder Quarter and the hall had its own lights and its own kettle and four dark hours from midnight, and there is a woman of thirty-one in that building who has the right to cut the power off a bar in the corridor and has used it, so that anybody who wanted to could not be pushed into a circuit, and both of those things are luck and scale and a building we happened to be standing in, and not one of them is me, and if you want the truth about the whole of it, the answer takes eleven days and a shed and a woman with a sheet of addresses in her own hand, and you cannot do it in a night, and you are not going to be able to do it in four years either, and I have not come here tonight to tell you that it is enough."

"Then what have you come to tell us."

"That I have got a piece of paper coming out of a regional office tomorrow morning and I do not know what is in it," Elias Rook said, "and I have been in this district since the twenty-ninth of November and I have not once stood in a room like the one Ferrand is describing, and he is right about that, and I would like everybody here to notice that the first man who said it out loud tonight was him."

Marged Prosser put the microphone on the table in front of Elias Rook and did not say anything and sat down.

Four minutes.

Nobody spoke. A man of about sixty put his head in his hands. Somebody at the back moved a chair. The hall, with a door open onto a cold evening, was extremely loud with nobody talking in it, and Elias Rook stood at the side of it with no folder and did not fill the four minutes, and was extremely conscious of the fact that he was not filling them, which was the worst part, because the not-filling was visible from twenty feet away.

Ferrand watched him do it and waited him out and did not rescue him, and then picked the microphone up off the table.

"Two hundred and eighty-five," Ferrand said. "And I will say it again at the end because a number said once is a rumor and a number said twice is a fact. **Two hundred and eighty-five.**"

He put it down and looked at the room for about a second and a half.

"You have all been very fair to me tonight and I would like to say one last thing and then I am going to let Mrs Prosser have the room. On Monday morning I am going to be a man whose document has been withdrawn, and I am not going to be able to sign another one, and about two hundred people in this room have believed a competent man's argument for an hour tonight, and I have not lied to one of you, and I want it noticed that the two things I am worried about are that I might be wrong about the program and that you might be right, and I do not know how to tell the difference between those two from this platform, and I have never known, and it is the only thing I have ever been sure of that I cannot put in a report."

---

He came out into the yard at ten past ten and stood by a skip with his hands in his coat pockets for about forty minutes, and about two hundred people had believed him, and nobody had lied to anybody, and about thirty of them had written their own address on a sheet of paper by the door on the way out and had stood looking at the fourth column and had walked out without writing anything in it.

The two of them and Joon Park and Inez Sato and Mara Okafor walked up Marden Row to the bus stop in the cold, and Inez Sato had not said one word all evening and had voted for nothing and had come eleven minutes on the 11 and had gone home, and she had told him in the corridor of the shop at number six on the Sunday night that the hall did not convene itself about a form and there was no meeting, no form, no line on a pad and no vote about the finding, and that had been true all evening and had been truer still at ten past ten, and the two of those things were sitting in the same man and neither one of them had got any smaller by the end of the night, and that was not a thing to be written down in a book that a nine-year-old could see, and so it went into a notebook with his right forearm across the page and it stopped there.
