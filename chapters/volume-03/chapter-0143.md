# Chapter 143 — The Second Of The Four Nights

Cliff Marner was ninety-six days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and twenty-six days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and twenty-six days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at half past five on the evening of Saturday the tenth of January, and the counter at number six was shut behind a locked door and the board was shut and the yard was open, and that was the whole of the week in this district.

Twenty-two to thirty-two Rennard, and sixteen to eighteen Murrow, crew of five. Peart had it on a card and Peart had it on the wall and the crew had it in the mouth of a man in the yard, and it was the third time in five days that the three of them had been compared and the first time anybody had made them agree.

---

The conditions came off Peart's own board at eight o'clock and they were in his hand and there were five of them and Hobbs had read all five and had not changed one word.

1. No scaffold on any ground off the bank of the Cut. 2. No vehicle over the soft road at any hour, loaded or empty, until the board's engineer says so in writing. 3. No daylight work in any block with a date on it. 4. A ladder for every stair, and the ladder stays until the last one is down. 5. Nobody's name goes on a sheet except the crew's own.

The third one was Peart's and the fourth was Doy's and the second was the driver's, and Hobbs had read them in that order and had wanted, briefly, to add a sixth, and had not.

"The low-loader will not go down Rennard tonight," Peart said. "Not with the tip up and not empty. That is the driver's condition and I have not argued with him and I would not have argued with him. So everything off that street tonight comes down on five backs, and there is a man of sixty-three on this crew and I have told him he carries what he likes and I have told the other four they carry what he does not."

---

They went on at half past seven. Rennard and then Murrow with a lane and a wall between them, and the wall at number twenty-four had a scaffold's worth of damage in it from Thursday morning and a board across the front of the garden gate and the sound of a lorry going past coming through the gap under the board all night.

At twenty-six the internal stair was half out.

That was the whole difficulty and it had been sitting there since twenty-six minutes past eleven on Thursday morning. Three men had been on it when the tower went over, and the works had stopped the make safe at noon, and what was left of the stair at twenty-six was seven treads and a stringer and a landing, and a woman of about eighty-two lived above it, and the block was on the notice for a night clearance at the end of the week.

"She is not going down that," Doy said, at about eight, looking at it. "Nobody is going down that. That is a handrail job and it is a daylight job and it is a two-man job and I am not doing it with a lamp."

Hobbs was on Rennard. She had been on Rennard since about half past five and she had a lamp of her own and a clipboard she had not written on.

"It has to be done tonight or it is not done tonight," she said, "and if it is not done tonight then at eight o'clock tomorrow morning a woman of eighty-two is in a house with no stair in it, in January, and on Monday morning somebody in an office is going to read about it."

"You did that stair on Thursday morning in daylight with a woman standing on it."

"Yes," Hobbs said. "That is why there are eleven names on a form in a yard and nine of them went home that afternoon. I am not going to argue with you about it at eight o'clock at night in the road. Two men, a lamp each, the far tread stays, and I will stand under it."

---

The woman of eighty-two came down at about twenty past nine at a rate of one step every forty seconds with a man's hand under her elbow and a second man's hand on the rail, and Elias Rook went down the seven treads backwards in front of her with a lamp in his right hand because Doy asked him to, and his left hand was in his coat doing nothing, which was its job, and it took eleven minutes and nobody said anything for any of it.

At the bottom she sat down on the step for a while, and then she said that she wanted to go and stand in the road, and they let her, and she stood in the middle of Rennard in a coat over a nightdress at half past nine at night in January and watched five men carry her furniture down her street past a boarded-up garden wall.

Eleven people had been hurt on that street at twenty past eleven on Thursday morning and nine of them were the nine off the clinic's list, and the woman of eighty-two was one of the nine, and she had a bandage on her left wrist and a cardigan over a nightdress and she stood in the road and watched, and Elias Rook carried a chest of drawers past her with his right shoulder and his right hand and put it in a vehicle at the end and came back for the next thing.

There was nothing to be had and nobody had anything to lend. It was five men and one road and about four hours, and everything that came off Rennard Street and Murrow between half past seven and twenty to seven in the morning came off it in somebody's arms.

---

At about two in the morning somebody said the word ladder.

Doy had it out of the vehicle and up against the outside of number thirty and a man of sixty-three was on it with a lamp, and the barrier came down thirty feet further along, and the man of thirty was on the barrier arm, and Elias Rook was on the road with a box.

"That man is sixty-one and he has been on a ladder for two hours," somebody said.

"He is sixty-three," Peart said, from the middle of the road, "and he is on that ladder because he put himself on it, and I asked him this afternoon whether he wanted the ground floor and he said he did not, and I have stopped asking people what they can do and started asking them what they would rather do, and it is a better question and it has cost me nothing this month."

---

At about half past five the crew walked Rennard and Murrow together, two men side by side, and at about ten to seven the barriers came down and the vehicle went out with the lamps in the rack and the ladder on it and nothing had gone over the soft road at any point in eleven hours and twenty minutes, which was a thing nobody had thought about on Thursday and everybody had thought about on Friday.

Nerys Hobbs signed it at about ten past seven on the morning of Sunday the eleventh of January, on the second of the four lines, with the date and the hour, and the barriers had been down for half an hour.

"Two of four," she said, when he looked at it. "The seventeenth is a Saturday and I will sign that one at about half past six on the Sunday morning because I have not walked a street in advance in my life and I am not starting in January."

"Is the stair the reason you are here tonight."

"The stair is the reason I was here at half past five on Thursday and the reason I was here at half past five tonight," Hobbs said. "The reason the stair exists is on a piece of paper in two rooms above a shop in a column headed NIGHT, and I have not written that sentence on anything and I am not going to, and I would like you to notice that I have said the word NIGHT out loud twice in six days and have not once said the other one."

He got the 11 at ten past seven and sat on the far side of the cut for about half an hour with his back against a wall and both hands in his pockets.

There was one thing he had been carrying all night that was not a box or a chest of drawers, and it was that on Tuesday the sixth of January a crew had gone to five door numbers on Marlow Street and had gone slowly, and nobody had been hurt, and that had been put in a book as an absence and had been right to be one, and that on Thursday morning five men had gone onto Rennard in daylight and eleven people had been hurt, and that on Saturday night five men had gone back onto Rennard in the dark and had carried everything down it on their backs because a lorry would not go over a soft road, and that one of the nine was standing in the road in a nightdress watching her own chest of drawers go past, and that not one person in this district had done anything on Saturday night that a foreman of fifty-one and a crew of five had not already worked out for themselves.

He wrote one line in the notebook.

*Tenth of January, Rennard and Murrow, five men, no vehicle on the soft road, eleven hours and twenty minutes, nobody hurt. A woman of eighty-two came down seven treads of a stair that was half out because we stopped at eleven minutes past eleven on Thursday, and she stood in the road and watched.*

And then, because it was true and because it was the second time he had written it and it did not get any better the second time:

*The same eleven. The hands were not there on Thursday and there is a whole explanation for why the same eleven were not hurt tonight, and the explanation is five men and a foreman and a road, and not one of those is anything to do with a room.*

---

At half past eight on Sunday morning there were two things on the gate of the works yard instead of one.

The first was the night schedule of the thirty-first of December, four rows, four nights, four crews, no addresses, a bent drawing pin through the top corner, and a line at the foot saying it was printed by the yard and was not a program document.

The second had been put up on Saturday afternoon by a foreman who had not been asked to and who had said he would take it down on Monday if anybody told him to, and it was the works' accident form of the eighth of January with a piece of string through the corner of it, and somebody had written under the last line, in biro, *11 injured — 9 home — no further injuries on 10 Jan.*

Eleven names were on it. It had been on the barrier at the bottom of Rennard Street for two days, in the road, in the rain, at the height of a person reading it, and it had been read by about four hundred people, and it was the only document in this district that had ever put those eleven names in the same place as those eleven people, and it was a form about injuries.
