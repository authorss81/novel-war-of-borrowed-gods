# Chapter 123 — How You Tell A Woman Of Eighty-Four

Cliff Marner was seventy-six days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and six days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and six days in, and Elias Rook did not get on a bus at all on Sunday morning, the twenty-first of December, because the yard was shut and the counter was shut and the shop at number six was shut and there was nothing in that district on a Sunday except the people in it.

Twenty-one days. Twenty-one fares and a hundred and fifteen pounds fifty. He had been in that district every day since the twenty-ninth of November except two Sundays, the fourteenth and this one, and he was not going to go on the twenty-first either, and both of those facts were a relief and a debt and he did not know which one was bigger.

---

Petar Lisk read the board at seven in the morning, out loud twice.

Who edited the Kestrel response record. What the Office believed a single approved outcome would prevent, with the second half of that still a bracket with nothing under it. Who benefits when a branch is officially deleted and why does the form call it an error, in capitals, in a nine-year-old's hand. Under the last two, in a hand nobody else in the building had put there, 19 November.

"There is no room," Petar Lisk said, and put the pencil behind his ear the other way round. "And there is not going to be a second wall, and if one of you builds one I will take the chalk off it myself, because I would rather be unkind in this building than be the man who tidied it."

Eleven people were in the front room and the four dark hours had ended at four. Cliff Marner was in the chair by the window with the blanket out of the store room and said nothing, because he said nothing in that room most mornings and his being in it was the whole of what he did there. Inez Sato had the lock-box and the power budget and twenty-two resident cards on the rail and twenty-eight people in the building and did not look up when she counted them.

On the page after the twenty-second of November, in Petar's own hand, was Saturday night's four minutes.

"That is not on the board," Inez Sato said.

"That is in a book," Petar Lisk said. "A book is a different animal and it can be taken off a shelf and read by somebody in a hundred years and it cannot be wiped with a sleeve. I would rather it was in a book."

"A rule with two people in it is a good rule," Inez said, "and it is worth exactly two people, and the day somebody takes it into a room with a form on the table is the day it stops being a thing that two people agreed to and becomes a procedure, and a procedure does not need anybody's yes, it needs a place to be filed."

She said it with the lock-box in front of her and did not look up, and two people in that room wrote nothing down, and the rule sat in a book on a shelf where it was supposed to sit.

---

Joon Park was at the table with the custody schedule in front of him, twenty-two lines, and the back of his right hand turned up on the wood because he was doing something with a pencil.

He was writing the four names again.

That was the whole of it, and he had been not doing it for a fortnight because the pencil had gone out on the fourteenth and he had not had a new one, and now he had one and it took him about a minute, and he did it in small writing on the ball of his thumb-side and did not press, because the skin there is thin.

"Forty-two days," he said. "Nine November to the twenty-first of December. That is the age of it in pencil and I have not had a second one of anything in a month."

"Write them on the sheet of paper," Elias Rook said. "Not on you."

"No," Joon Park said. "And you are not going to tell me why not, because I know why not, and if you tell me why not it will be a better reason than mine and I will use yours and it will be a bad trade."

The four names were on the back of a young man's hand and in half an inch of margin in a kitchen pencil in a house on Cutbank Row, and in no system in the world, and they were on no schedule and had no number, and they were going to be named in January out of a woman's margin and not out of a young man's fist.

"And now the other thing," Joon Park said, and capped the pencil. "You told her on Saturday night. That was one. You have booked the second for Tuesday morning and you told this room on Saturday night that you had, and I have written nothing down and I am not going to. But I am going to say one thing about it and then I am going to stop, which is a thing I do about once a month."

"Say it."

"I have watched you ask a room for nothing eleven times in thirteen weeks and I have never once watched you ask a person," Joon Park said. "There is no form in the world for the second one, and that is exactly why it is worth doing, and I am not able to help you with it, and I am not going to be in the room on Tuesday because a man of twenty-four with a custody schedule in his coat is a different animal in a room than a man with a pen."

"That is fair," Elias Rook said.

"It is not fair. It is a fact with a date on it." He put the custody schedule in his coat. "The fifth of December. The second of January and the sixteenth of January, and nothing before them, and I am not going to be asked about it in a room and I am not going to be chased and if anybody asks me in a corridor I will say the two dates and nothing else, because that is the only sentence I have got."

---

The hall's telephone rang at about half past two, and Inez answered it, and then she put her hand over the mouthpiece and said one word down the corridor to him, and the word was *doctor*.

Rhoda Mistry was forty-seven and ran Tidegate Community Health, and she had been in the church hall off Marden Row on the eighth of December with about two hundred other people, and she had come up to Elias Rook at the door afterwards and had twenty seconds with him and had asked him nothing, and she had been thinking about it for a fortnight.

"I saw you in the queue on Saturday," she said. "I got four words with you and I gave you this number and I have been ringing all morning because a clinic does not ring on a Sunday, and I have decided that a clinic can ring once."

"I am glad you did," Elias Rook said. "I have got about four minutes before this building starts a row about the heating. Ask me the thing you did not ask in the hall."

"How do you tell a woman of eighty-four that she is being carried out of her house on Wednesday night."

He had been in a yard, a church hall, a kitchen on Ash Street, a records room, a sealed tunnel, three corridors and a great many buses for thirteen weeks, and that was the first question anybody had asked him in the whole of it that he did not have a form of an answer to.

"I don't know," he said. "I have been in that district three weeks and I have watched a program that is very good at telling a large number of people a thing quickly and accurately, and I have watched it not have anything at all for the one woman, and I do not know how she gets told."

"You have not watched it tell anybody anything," Mistry said. "That is the thing you are getting wrong. It has not told anybody anything yet. Every letter in this district has gone out with the rates since September and every one of them says your address and your classification and what happens next, and not one of them has a person's name on it, and the ones I have read in this clinic have been brought in by the person they were about, which means the person found out from their own letter in their own hand and brought it to a doctor."

"And you have read how many?"

"Ninety-one people in eleven weeks," Mistry said, "and I counted them, and it is a count of people and not a count of doors, and I would like that on the same page as your number and I do not know anybody who will print it."

---

"Rhoda," Elias Rook said. "I am going to ask you for a list. I am telling you that I am going to ask, which is a thing I learned on Saturday night and have only done once, and I am going to do it in a room with your face in it and not on a telephone, and I am going to come to the clinic and it will be Monday and it will be in the morning."

"Then I will think about it over the weekend," Mistry said, "which I have not got, so I will think about it while I am talking to you. Go on, then. What is it for."

"To go to a foreman," Elias Rook said. "And not to a program, and not to a cell, and not to a form. A foreman has a crew and a torch and a stair and about four minutes per door, and what he has not got is any idea in the world which eleven people on that ground cannot get down a stair in the dark. You have got that."

"I have had it for three years," Rhoda Mistry said. "Since the second outfall, when I started writing down everybody I was worried about who lives alone, and I have never once been asked for it by anybody, and I have said no once, to a man from a different office who wanted to see it for a different reason, and I would like it noted that I said no and that I was correct."

"Noted," Elias Rook said, and wrote it down, and did not write the list down, and had not asked yet.

"Two o'clock," she said. "The clinic shuts at two for the holidays and I will be there until ten past because I have four people coming, and if you are ten minutes late I will have gone home and I will not answer the telephone."
