# Chapter 119 — A Reading You Cannot Repeat

Cliff Marner was seventy-two days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and two days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and two days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Wednesday morning, the seventeenth of December, with a copy of a nineteen-page schedule sitting on a Deputy Director's desk on the other side of the city and nobody in Tidegate knowing it but the three people who had put it in the envelope.

The query from the board's surveyor had gone up on the Tuesday with the copy in the same envelope. It had arrived in the Meridian on the sixteenth and reached the desk on the seventeenth. That was the whole of the reason anything after this could happen, and it was a pass-through and not a decision, and nobody in the cell had treated it as one.

The left hand was gone and it made the mornings slow. He buttoned his coat with the right hand and teeth, carried the folder under the right arm, and kept the tube against his side. The right knuckles could hold the rail now if he was careful. The plaster on the second knuckle was clean. He pinned the blanket with his right forearm while he folded it, because the hall took it in turns to air the bedding and this week the turn fell on the front room, and the learning had stopped being a lesson and had become the shape of the mornings.

---

He stood on Marlow Street at eleven with his right palm on the low wall by the corner shop, bare hand to brick, because Marlow was the street the line had moved onto and Sallow was the street it had moved off, and he wanted to know whether the offering was still where it had been.

The thread was live. Then it was not the same live.

It was still a proximity and not a voice. It still told him whether the ground he was being asked about was the ground he was standing on. But the reading that came through it had changed. Direction and stress still came, still not a repair, still unable to tell him how many people were on the street, still unable to be carried out of the place he stood. Hand still had to be on the structure. For about an hour afterwards he would still see stress he was not touching, showing a load that was not that building's load today, and it could not be refreshed or willed.

What had changed was the shape inside that. On Thursday and Friday and Saturday the line had run clean off Sallow Court toward Marlow, forty-four addresses wide, carrying where September said it carried. Now the edge along Marlow had softened by half a street, the way a line in three colors of ink softens when the hand draws it twice. It was not a different band and it was not a move of two streets. It was a change nobody had caused, in a gift nobody else could check, on a morning when no reading had been taken, no vehicle had gone down the alignment, no foreman had shut anything, and no method officer had drawn anything.

He lifted his hand and stood on the pavement with the folder under his arm, and understood that a changed future could alter the thread before it could be used. That was Stage 2's cost as it had been stated to him in no words at all: aftermath became easier to identify and not easier to undo. He could feel the offering shift and could do nothing about the shift except notice it.

He walked the length of Marlow to the corner where it met the yard approach, with the tube against his leg, counting doors without writing them down in the notebook he carried. The shops were open and the shutters were up. A woman carried two bags past him and did not look at him. The low wall ran for about forty meters and he put his hand on it twice more, once at the middle and once at the far end, bare palm to brick each time, because hand had to be on structure and one touch was never enough to be sure. Both times the same softened edge. Both times direction and stress and nothing else, not a repair, no number of people, nothing that could be carried. The second time a boy of about ten watched him and asked what he was doing and he said checking the brick for frost, and the boy said it was not frost weather and went on.

He told the empty street in the words it happened in, because the return required it out loud in a room and this was not a room and did not count.

"The line moved after you gave it to me," he said to nobody, practising the sentence the way a man practises a thing he will have to say where somebody can write it down. "I do not know by how much and I do not know why."

---

The church hall off Marden Row was booked for the hour by a group with no name on the booking sheet, and at half past three about thirty people were in it with a kettle in the corner, most of them parents from the school. Marged Prosser was fifty-five and chaired the group, and she had a card with six words on it from the Monday before last that said he did not have a third one.

Elias stood in the aisle with the folder under his right arm and did not open it, because he had learned on the Saturday that a stranger with a folder in front of nine people would be believed for being a stranger, and he had stopped himself then and was going to stop himself now.

A man near the front said, "Is the ground under my street carrying or not. Somebody tell us in one sentence."

It was the room that wanted a sentence about a seam. Thirty people, a kettle, a microphone that worked, and nobody with any obligation to answer them.

Joon Park was at the side table with nothing in front of him but his hands, the back of one still carrying four names in pencil so faint they could barely be read. He had come two miles and eleven minutes on the 11 because Elias had asked him to come and be the man who wrote things down, and he had brought no schedule and no roll and no custody file.

"We do not know that," Joon Park said, out loud, where the room could hear him. "The schedule says one thing and the reading says another and a man who put his hand on a wall says a third, and the third is not a record. If anybody in this city asks me where we got it I will say a man put his hand on a wall and that is the whole of what I will say."

Nobody argued with him, because the building had never once been told a thing it could not check, and the reason Elias did not understand for about a week the Friday before was still the reason.

Marged Prosser wrote something on a card and did not read it out. The man near the front sat down. The kettle boiled and nobody made tea until the meeting was quite over, and then everybody did at once. A woman at the back asked whether the school stayed open and Marged said the board had said in writing that it was not in the alignment, and that she would read the sentence to anybody who wanted it, because she had learned that trick from a man who was better at Monday meetings than anybody in them. Nobody asked about the schedule by name, and nobody needed to, because the difference between a reading and a schedule had been asked once in that hall and answered with a card saying he did not have a third one.

Afterwards on Marden Row, Marged said, "You have been in this district since before the twenty-ninth and you have not told us anything we can use. Is that the job."

"That is the job this week," Elias Rook said. "I have a reading I cannot carry out of the street I stood on. If I say it in a room it is my word."

"Then keep bringing the young man who says when it is not a sentence," she said, and went back in. She was right about most of what happened next and she was the constituency, and she was not on any list of the program's.

He stopped at the yard gate on the way to the bus and read the closure timetable again, open to closed, with the public side standable until the new year. The works notice board beside it carried the alignment figures, one thousand one hundred and forty addresses and two thousand nine hundred residents on the board's own model, adjusted twice, with the printed slip about writing to the surveyor. The oldest notice on the board was the Sallow Court clearance of the ninth of November, three printed lines, pale at the corners. He did not touch any of it, because none of it was his to move, and the envelope in his coat with the unsent letter in it stayed where it was for the same reason.

---

He got the 11 at ten past six with the folder under his right arm and the tube against his leg, and the thread went quiet over the water as it always did. At Ash Street Petar read the board at seven, three questions untouched, the nineteenth of November still the only date, the wall completely full. Twenty-two cards. Twenty-eight people. Four dark hours at midnight.

In the front room he told Joon Park in the words it happened in, before he acted on it, that the reading had changed and nobody had caused it.

"Then it is not a finding," Joon Park said. "It is a thread that moved before we could use it. Write the date in the margin and not the meaning."

Elias wrote nothing, because a man with four names on somebody else's hand had no way of knowing whether he had got one of them right, and the same applied to a line that softened by half a street on a Wednesday afternoon. Inez Sato came through with the power budget and asked whether the observer had been in the building and he said she had a hand on the counter log and asked fair questions, and Inez nodded once and said that was the shape of the door. He counted the fares with his right hand on the stairs, two pounds seventy-five each way, eleven minutes each way, travel and sleep and money in that order, and nobody paid them but him. He put the notebook back in his coat with the envelope unsent inside it, the reason four words long, and went to bed with a back that would hurt on the Tuesday.
