# Chapter 129 — What She Reads Out Loud On A Saturday

Cliff Marner was eighty-two days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and twelve days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and twelve days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Saturday morning, the twenty-seventh of December, and did not go into the district to do anything, because there was nothing to be done in it today and the run of twenty-four days was over on Wednesday night and he was not going to invent a reason to start it again.

The shop at number two had its door propped open with a brick because of the wind, and there was a queue of seven, and there was a printed works notice on the inside of the door with a line at the bottom of it that said the public side of the alignment was not to be stood on from the fifth of January, and the day after that the closure timetable behind glass on the yard gate said the same thing, and the two of them had been printed in two different offices by two different people for two different reasons and neither of them had read the other one.

---

There were eleven people round the bollard on Cutbank Row at twenty to eleven and by half past there were fourteen, and Winifred Alagoa was reading the sheet out loud the way she had every Saturday since the fourth of March 2031, in her own hand, at her own pace, and nobody in that street thought there was anything remarkable about it.

He stood on the far side of Cutbank Road and listened for an hour and ten minutes and did not say one word.

She went Rennard, and she went Murrow, and she did the two of them in a different voice, and the difference was not a performance, it was the difference between a street she was reading out of a book and a street she had walked on the eighth of December with a man who wanted to know whether the people in it were in it.

"Rennard," she said. "Twenty-two to forty-one, and the odd side."

"Rennard, and the odd side," a man of about sixty said. "Rennard and the odd side. My mother's is Rennard and the even side and that has been the case since 1971 and I have the deed."

"Then your mother's is not on this sheet," Alagoa said, "and I am telling you that in the street, in December, and I will tell you the same thing in January, and there is nobody in this street who can put your mother back on it, and I am not going to try."

"Right you are," the man said, and looked at the sheet and did not say anything else.

At Sallow Court, which she does last, she stopped for about two seconds longer than she stopped anywhere else, and said thirty-nine doors, and about eight of the fourteen stopped listening, and two of them did not, and one woman of about forty said, "Thirty-nine?" in the tone of a person who has a number of her own.

They did not finish it. Two people asked her about a stop on a bus and a man asked her about a boiler, and she answered both of them properly and at length, and then she rolled the sheet along the front of her coat and put a rubber band round it and put it in her bag, and about eight people said thank you and about six did not because they had heard it before.

The woman of forty who had said thirty-nine was still standing there.

"Who keeps yours," she said.

"I do," Alagoa said. "Since 2031, in this coat. I have never once written a person on it, and that is not a complaint, it is the only way it is any use to anybody."

"Right," the woman said. "Because I have been at number twenty-two since 1988 and there are two flats over me and a yard behind and I have never once known how many doors anybody says are in this street, and I have never wanted to, and now I have heard a number and I would like it to be the right one." She put her hands in her pockets. "It is a strange thing to want. I have never said it out loud before and I am not going to say it again."

"You may say it out loud," Alagoa said, "as often as you like, in this street, and I will tell you what I know and I will tell you when I do not know, and I have never once known a number that was true on a day somebody asked me for it. Thirty-nine is what I have written down. The board says forty-one. Both of those are on paper and one of them is wrong and it is not my job to say which, and I am sixty-three and I have got a drawer for not-knowings and it is nearly full."

Elias Rook did not go over.

---

"Mr. Rook," Alagoa said, when the street had emptied out. "You are going to tell me or you are not going to tell me, and either is fine, and I have a kettle on."

"What I know," he said, "is whether you are right about it, and I cannot promise you that I will ever find out, and that is the true version and not a deflection. I have got four words on a piece of paper and I am not going to give you a sentence I have not stood behind."

"That is a worse thing to be given than a number," she said. "A number I can be cross about. A not-knowing from a man in a coat, I can put in a drawer with the rest of the not-knowings, and there is a great many of those in this district at Christmas and I have a drawer for them."

"Thirty-nine doors," Elias Rook said. "Your sheet says thirty-nine. The board's printed list for the same block says forty-one households. I have had that since June and I have said it to you once, in your kitchen, in December, and I am saying it to you on a Saturday because it is on a street and not in a room. Two is two. It is not a rounding. Somebody is wrong by a person, and I cannot show you who, and the reason I cannot show you is that it is not my arithmetic to correct."

"I know it is two," Alagoa said. "I have known since June. I did the sum on the back of an envelope the same week and I have never done it again, and I have never told anybody, and I have never gone round either side of that street with a tape, and the reason is that I would like it to be the board who is wrong about a number, and if I go round with a tape then it is a woman of sixty-three with a tape, and that is a different kind of story and I do not want to be that story on my own road."

"The gap of two is a fact about lists," Elias Rook said, "and not about anybody, and I have had three weeks of wanting it to be about somebody and it is not, and I am telling you that because you have just been told a number out loud in a street by a woman with a sheet, and I am not going to be the next voice on that corner doing it."

"No," Alagoa said. "You are not. You are the fourth one to stand on that pavement since last Saturday and you are the first one who has stood still."

---

He went back to the newsagent and bought a paper he did not read and stood in the seven-person queue and thought about the thing he had not done, which was to take four dates out of his coat and put them in her hand on a Saturday morning in front of a bollard.

He had the dates. He had had them since Wednesday. Rennard comes on the twenty-second and Murrow on the twenty-third, and a woman of sixty-three was standing in the cold reading those two streets to fourteen people on the twenty-seventh of December, and one of those fourteen was a man whose mother is on the wrong side of the street.

He did not give it to her. There was a card in his coat with four dates on it and no addresses, and she had not asked him for anything, and on the eighteenth of December he had stood on a bridge and told Joon Park that a document handed to a third place without the woman who owns it is not a favor and is not a duty, it is a thing you do because you would like to be the one who did it. And the four dates would have been exactly that. And a woman who has read a thousand doors out loud at a bollard every Saturday for eleven years would have taken them and used them and been grateful and it would have been the best afternoon of his life and it would have cost her the drawer.

So he took the 11 at ten past twelve and he carried the dates in his coat, and that was the second time in nine days he had had something in his hands that he was not going to give anybody, and he has never claimed to be a man who does it twice and then stops.

---

At Ash Street in the afternoon Joon Park had his left forearm out on the table with the two dates in pencil on the inside of it, and the four names in ballpoint on the back of his right hand, freshly gone over, and he turned both over as Elias Rook came in.

"Forty-eight days," he said. "Nine November to the twenty-seventh of December. And I have had one look at those four and I have copied them out of my own hand, and it is a piece of paper with four names on it in a room with twenty-eight people in it, and a woman of sixty-three has just read a thousand doors out loud on a corner and has not been asked for anything by anybody in this city since 2031 except once, by a man with a pencil, and she gave him those four and asked him to leave them on his own hand."

"You are not going to hand them to her," Elias Rook said.

"I am not," Joon Park said, "and neither are you, and I want that written down in a book if Petar is willing, and he is not, because the wall is full and there is no room and the rule is that it stays where the woman put it, and I am going to say the thing I have been carrying up the stairs all week and then I am going to stop."

"Say it."

"They are going into the record in January," Joon Park said, "out of her margin, in a room, with her asked first, and I do not think anybody in that room is going to like it, and I do not think she is going to say yes, and I want it noticed that I have not gone to anybody's door with a pencil, and that I have not put them in a parents' card index, and that you have not taken four dates out of your coat on a Saturday morning and given them to a woman at a bollard, and that this is the fourth week in a row that a thing this building could have done has not been done, and I am the only one in it who is keeping the list of those."
