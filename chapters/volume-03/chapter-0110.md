# Chapter 110 — The Number He Says Out Loud

Cliff Marner was sixty-three days out of the ground. Della Marner was ninety-three days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was ninety-three days in. Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past six on Monday evening, the eighth of December, and there was a program leaflet on every chair in the church hall off Marden Row and about two hundred people in it, and the folding table at the front had a microphone on it that worked.

She had put the leaflets on the chairs herself. Marged Prosser is fifty-five, chairs a parents' group in this district, lost a son in the 2039 outfall, and is the reason a regional pilot program's leaflet went into a school in September and not only into letter boxes, because she went to the school and asked, and a man in a high-visibility vest told Elias Rook on a Saturday that it was the cleverest piece of administration anybody in this city had done in ten years.

She got the meeting started at four minutes past seven, in about ninety seconds, and then sat down beside the table, which is not a thing a chair of a parents' group has to do.

---

He came in at five to seven and did not come to the front.

Tobias Ferrand is fifty-two and had been a duty flood officer in 2027 and 2031 and 2036, and he is Regional Director of the Bellwether Pilot Program, and he stood at the back for four minutes listening, and then Marged Prosser said *can you come up, Mr. Ferrand, we are going to lose the hour*, and he came up and he was better at it than anybody in that room including the man who had come to speak.

He did not take the microphone off the table. He put it in his own hand and held it at chest height, and the first four things he said he said into his own collar.

"I am not going to stand here and tell you the program is a good thing, because there is a leaflet on your chair and you can read it and it is a good leaflet and I did not write it. I am going to take questions, and I am going to answer all of them tonight, including the ones I do not like, and if I do not know one I am going to say I do not know one in this room rather than in a letter.

"So. Ask."

---

He asked the round himself, which is the thing that made him good at it. He went down the room asking what people wanted to know, wrote nothing down, and answered nine before anybody else spoke.

A man of about sixty wanted to know whether the wall goes through his garden. A woman wanted to know whether her postal address changes. Two teenagers wanted to know whether the school stays open. It is the board's decision and not his, and he had asked and been told in writing that the school is not in the alignment, and he got about half a sentence into saying so and then stopped, and said, "I will not stand up here and promise you a school. I have asked, and I have been told the school is not in the alignment, and I have been told that in writing, and I will read you the sentence if anybody wants the sentence."

A man in a wheelchair by the aisle said, "I lost my wife in the outfall in '39."

"I know who you are, Mr. Pritchard," Ferrand said, not unkindly. "I signed the letter."

"I do not want a list I have to argue with," the man said. "I have got one. It is in a drawer and it is four pages and every name on it is right and I still cannot get anybody to say a sentence about it. If you put four hundred names on a piece of paper and they are all right, what have I got."

"You have got a piece of paper," Ferrand said, "and I am sorry that is the answer, and I am going to come back to it."

And then he said the thing, and he said it into the collar, and nobody had asked him for it, and there was a pause before it and the pause was about a second and a half.

"Two hundred and eighty-five addresses in this district are classified `TRANSITIONAL` on the schedule I signed, and I am going to say that number twice tonight, because I have noticed that people would rather hear a number twice than hear it once.

"**Two hundred and eighty-five.** Those addresses are not in the retained district. That is a fact. It does not mean anybody has been told to go anywhere, and it does not mean there is a destination, and I am not going to stand here and be clever about the difference, because the difference is real and it is also the whole difficulty.

"Now I am going to say four other things and all four of them are true, and I would like the people at the back to hear all four."

---

"One. The reading is right. It has not lost anybody in the field, and I will put that in writing to anybody who asks me for it in writing this week.

"Two. The schedule is the only document anybody in this room can defend, and a schedule you cannot defend is a guess, and I am not going to be the man who asks the people in this room to act on a guess.

"Three. I signed it. On the second of December, in the Meridian, with my name on it, and it is my signature and not the program's, and I will sign it again in the spring when the next reading comes, and I would like somebody in this room to understand that I am not a messenger and I have never been a messenger, and if I am wrong about four hundred people then four hundred people find out on the morning they start, in front of everybody. I would like to be able to tell the difference between that evening and this one, and the difference is that on this one you can ask me about it.

"Four." He looked at the room and did not find anybody. "**The second reading came in on the twenty-ninth of November and the band moved two streets. Forty-four addresses in this district are inside the September reading and outside the November one. I signed the schedule on the September reading. I have not rebuilt it, and I have not been asked to, and I am not going to pretend to you that I do not know what that sentence is.**

"Here is why, and you will not like it, and I would rather you had it from me in a church hall than find it in a fight. If I rebuild that schedule every time the band moves, then every time the band moves I am rehoming four hundred people in an afternoon. There is no version of an afternoon that is not bad. People die in the moving. There is a foreman in this district whose entire job is to make sure this district does not have that happen twice, and he is better at it than I am, and I am not going to overrule a foreman at eight o'clock at night in front of the people who live here.

"So I signed it on the reading I had, and I signed it again knowing what I know, and that is mine, and if you want to be angry with somebody about it, I am available on a Tuesday and I am not going to be offended."

---

Marged Prosser asked the question in the round and it was the best question anybody asked all night.

"What is the difference between your reading and your schedule, and which one is the wall going to be built on?"

"The wall is built on the alignment," Ferrand said, "and the alignment is the board's and not mine, and the board chose it on a flood model from 2039 that they have adjusted twice and told anybody who asks. My document is not the alignment and I am not going to stand here and pretend the two are the same shape, because they are not, and because I do not think the board would thank me for saying they were."

"And which one says whether I am still living here on the morning they start?"

"Yours does, and mine does, and they are not the same document, and the honest answer to your question is that I do not have a third one."

Marged Prosser wrote that down. Elias Rook watched a woman of fifty-five write *he does not have a third one* on a card, in a church hall, on a Monday evening, and she was right to write it down, and she was going to be right about most of what happened next.

---

And then a voice near the back said his name, and he answered it from the floor with no microphone, and it was the worst thing he did all week and he knew it while it was happening.

"Tobias Ferrand. Has anybody in this program ever been wrong?"

The room was not silent. It was worse than silent, because about forty people turned round to look at who had asked.

"Can I take that into the microphone," Ferrand said. Not unkindly. "Somebody said something and I am going to say it again so that people at the back get it, and I am not going to do it to make a point, I am doing it because the man asked it in a room and a question asked in a room deserves to be heard in a room."

He put it into the microphone exactly as it had been asked.

"Has anybody in this program ever been wrong."

"Yes," Ferrand said. "Twice, that I can produce, and I have both in writing and I will give you both. The first was in 2039 on the eastern bank, where a block of addresses came off the survey with an error in it and I signed it and people were told the wrong thing for six weeks, and I was right about the ground and wrong about the rows. The second was this one.

"I am not going to be modest about that. **I got this one wrong the day I signed it, and I knew I was getting it wrong, and I signed it anyway, and I have just told you why, and I would like you to write down that I said it in that order and not the other order.**"

Somebody at the back said "oh" in a way that was not a heckle.

Then a woman on the front row leaned over and put the microphone on the table in front of Elias Rook, and did not say anything, and sat back down.

Four minutes.

Nobody spoke. A man in a wheelchair looked at the floor. Somebody at the back moved a chair. The hall, with a door open onto a cold evening, was extremely loud with nobody talking in it, and Elias Rook stood in an aisle between the chairs with a folder under his arm that he had not opened and did not fill the four minutes, and was extremely conscious of the fact that he was not filling them, which was the worst part, because the not-filling was visible from twenty feet away.

Ferrand watched him do it and waited him out, and did not rescue him, and then picked the microphone up off the table.

"Mr. Rook. You have been in this district four days. I have been in it nine years." He said it without any edge at all, which was the cruelty of it. "You have a page in a notebook and I have **four hundred and twelve** addresses that are still standing. Not one of them is on any list of mine that I have had to explain to a coroner.

"And I will say this. If you would like to make the schedule a better document, I will get you the working folder — the one with the drafts in it and the September overlay and the thing I sent back twice — and you can have it on Monday, and I am not doing it as a favor and I would like it noted that it is not a favor, because I would like this program to be checked by people who are not in it."

Elias Rook said, "Thank you," because there were two hundred people in the room and it was the only one available.

"Monday," said Ferrand, and put the microphone down.

---

He went out into the yard at ten past eight and did not say one word to anybody about what he thought, and did not write it down either, and stood by a skip with his hands in his coat pockets for twenty minutes while a district emptied out around him.

The inside back cover of the notebook had two dates on it from Friday. The back of the week — Thursday, Friday, Saturday, Sunday, Monday, five days, five pages — had one line on it, and it was a date, and it was the near end.

**2 JANUARY.**

The whole of what he had learned in four days about a program that had a name and a face and an accurate reading of the ground, and it fit on a line on the back of a page in his own hand, and the line was a date that somebody else was keeping for him.

That night the district learned that there was a man from the Cinder Quarter who had come four times and asked good questions, and a man from the cell who had come once and answered all of them, and the first one went home on the 11 and the second one stayed and shook about forty hands at the door.
