# Chapter 101 — The Board At Seven, And Then Tuesday

Petar Lisk read the board at seven on Saturday morning, the twenty-ninth of November, the eighty-fourth day, and read it out loud twice, and then said the thing he had been thinking about since Friday, which was that there was not room for any more.

Cliff Marner was fifty-four days out of the ground, in the chair by the window, and he had got a blanket out of the store room on Thursday because the heating in this building is a war of opinions and the store room wins. Della Marner was eighty-four days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was eighty-four days in, inside a tunnel that was sealed on the thirty-third day and classified in nine words, and nobody had gone to the loading lip in twelve weeks and nobody was going to and the handset line was dead.

"There is no room," Petar Lisk said. "Not for a line and not for a name. When it is finished there will be a space the shape of a shoe and whoever comes in here after me is going to think somebody was tidy."

"Whose shoe," said somebody at the back.

"That is not the interesting question and I know it is not and you are all going to ask me anyway."

There were seven people in the front room at seven. It was the eighty-fourth morning he had done it. Twenty-two resident cards and twenty-eight people, four dark hours a night, and the headcount had not moved since the fourteenth week and there was no reason on earth for it to move today, and there was a woman making tea at the end of the corridor who would be gone by nine and would be back by four.

Elias Rook read the wall. The clerk's column at the top with its heading that says *items* where it should say *people*, and twenty-eight lines under it, and the nineteen hands in nineteen sizes under those, and the two names in chalk, one of which is a name in a system in another part of the city as of Thursday and the other of which is under a street in this one and is not a rescue and is not a deadline.

Then the three questions, all in the same nine-year-old capitals, and he had read them about four hundred times and could have said them with the wall covered:

> **WHO EDITED THE KESTREL RESPONSE RECORD?**
> **WHAT DOES THE OFFICE BELIEVE A SINGLE APPROVED OUTCOME WOULD PREVENT — [ ]**
> **WHO BENEFITS WHEN A BRANCH IS OFFICIALLY DELETED — AND WHY DOES THE FORM CALL IT AN ERROR?**

The bracket under the second one is still a bracket with nothing in it. The nineteenth of November is still the only date up there, and the wall is full underneath it, and the last line stops three inches short of the skirting.

---

Elias Rook had the letter in the inside pocket of his coat, folded in four, and he had carried it since Friday afternoon and had not shown it to anybody and had not written one word about it.

It was one paragraph. Dated the twenty-eighth.

> **HAVENPORT RESCUE SERVICE. Re: E. Rook, field technician, case reference HC-42-0417.**
> **The extension of the suspension from seam assignment made on the twenty-seventh of this month expires at the end of this day and is not renewed. You are restored to ordinary field duty with effect from 1 December 2042. The revocation of three endorsements of 27 November 2042 is unchanged and is not affected by this notice. Case reference HC-42-0417 remains open. It is not for the department to close it.**

Two facts and they are not joined up. He was a field technician again on Monday, and three endorsements he had held for thirty-one years were still gone, and both of those were true at the same time in the same paragraph, and the department had put them in that order for him, and it had not put anybody's name to it, and it had not said one word about the fact that a month had ended.

A sentence about a month is a sentence about a month.

He got it out of the pocket and put it back in the pocket. He did not put it on the table in the front room, because a table in the front room is a thing other people read out of, and because nobody had asked him how it felt, and because if he put it down somebody would have to pick it up.

He read the board out loud once after Petar had finished. It was not asked for. He did it badly — he got the order wrong and put the two names before the column, which is not the order it is in, and Ru Sabar from the doorway said *it is upside down* and he said *it is the order I do it in* and she said *then it is your order, not the wall's*.

Then he went and did the round with the person whose round it was, which is not his and which he does because he is the only man in the building with both hands available on a morning, one of them.

---

At half past eight he went out to the corner and the eleven-minute bus was due at ten past, and he stood there with a satchel with the week's inventory sheet in it.

**The Tuesday slot at the depot on the far bank is his and has been his since the end of October.** R. Dunleavy's letter gave back both halves of the old arrangement in the words *hose certification and inventory* and said the slots would not be held twice, and the Tuesday has been on the depot's notice every week since and will be on it next Tuesday morning at half past six, and the Friday half of it is an inventory half and it is still on the notice too. Neither of those is a thing anybody gave him and neither of them is about the letter in his coat, and the letter in his coat takes effect on Monday, and he could not sit still on a Saturday morning, and he had decided that the reason for going was the sheet and that the reason for going on the twenty-ninth of November specifically was the letter, and that he would be perfectly honest about that to anybody who asked him and that nobody was going to ask him.

The 11 came. He got on it at the front and paid with the change out of his coat and did not use a card because the machine takes four seconds and he was the only person on the deck with time.

It is eleven minutes from the corner of Ash Street to the Tannery Cut. He had ridden it four times in 1988 and never once since, and the whole of the route was still there — the depot, the gas holder, the two bridges, the low road where the water comes up in a hard winter, and then the long flat run along the cut with the boatyards on your right and the whole west bank of the city in front of you getting wider and lower and more like a place where people live rather than a place where people commute.

**Tidegate.** The stop was the fourth past the cut. Two miles, eleven minutes, and a district that had a name he had known all his life and had never once been inside.

---

The hoarding went up the whole length of the fence at the north end where Cutbank Row turns into the yard approach, corrugated steel, and a man in a fluorescent vest was standing in front of it with a roll of tape and a clipboard and a box of leaflets, in the way of a man who has been standing there since eight and enjoys it.

Elias Rook got off the bus and walked up to him.

"Morning."

"You've come on the wrong bus for the depot."

"I know."

"It's fine. Everybody does." The man laughed, friendly, entirely without malice, and turned the clipboard around. "Do you want a leaflet or do you want to argue with the leaflets? Because the ones who want to argue, it's usually better to have read it first."

Elias Rook took the leaflet.

It was good paper. That was the first thing he noticed and it was the thing that stayed with him all the way back. It was better paper than anything the Continuity Office had put on a table in eleven weeks. Four sides, one color of ink, a photograph of a wall being built against weather.

> **BELLWETHER PILOT PROGRAM**
> **Tidegate — works notice**
> **Fourth Stage Tidal Defense, Havenport Tidal Board**
> **Reading office: above 6, Cutbank Row. Open Monday to Friday, 09:00–16:30. Closed Saturday and Sunday.**
> **Your address, your classification, and what happens next: inside.**
> **Ref. BWP/TG/4SD/2042**

And at the bottom, in the same ink, in the same size, on the same piece of good paper:

**Tobias Ferrand, Regional Director.**

Elias Rook stood and looked at it for longer than a man looks at a leaflet.

"Forty-one thousand people got one of these," said the man in the vest. "Not me. It went out with the rates. There's one on every door in the district and one on the school one, which is Mr. Prosser's idea, and I think that's the single cleverest piece of admin anybody's done in this city in ten years, because if it's on the school then it's not a threat, it's information."

"And how many of them have read it."

"Enough." The man in the vest shrugged. "That's not my department either. My department is the tape."

---

He did not go into the reading office. It was Saturday and it had been shut since six o'clock the previous evening and there was a printed card in the door about that, and there was another printed card under it about a public counter on the ground floor of the same address, and a third one about the Tuesday and Thursday evening sessions, and the whole arrangement was the arrangement of a body that intends to be dealt with and has decided exactly how much of that happens at a counter and how much happens in a room.

Instead he walked.

The west bank in November is a low place and Tidegate is lower than most of it. Four walk-up blocks off Cutbank Row, a strip of low workshops along the cut with roller doors and paint on the concrete, a boatyard with a hull up on a cradle and a tarpaulin over it, a health center with a sign that has been repainted twice and still has the old name showing underneath, a school on Marden Row with a fence and a bicycle rack and about nine children in a yard the size of a tennis court.

The school was open. That was the thing that undid the first part of the morning. It was a Saturday and the school had nine children in it and a woman on a folding table handing out sheets of paper, and when Elias Rook asked what the sheets were, a girl of about eleven said they were for the parents.

"For what?"

"For the meeting on Monday. There's a meeting." She looked at him the way children look at a stranger who is standing in their playground. "You don't live here."

"No."

"Then you can't come."

That's a rule and not a discrimination, and she was enforcing it correctly, and he had no answer to it, and he stood in a playground on Cutbank Row for about nine seconds and then went and looked at a fence.

---

He did the arithmetic from a notice board at the yard gate, which is what it is for.

**TIDEGATE — FOURTH STAGE ALIGNMENT AREA. 1,140 addresses. 2,900 residents (works board model, 2039, adjusted twice).**

The little printed slip under it said the model had been adjusted twice and that a model was a model, and that anybody who wanted to know what the adjustment had been could write to the board's surveyor and get an answer inside twenty working days, and Elias Rook stood and read that sentence four times because it is the same sentence in a different font, and he has been in a building where four people have said a version of it out loud in eleven weeks, and here it was printed on a card at a gate in a different city, and the man who printed it had a surveyor's job and no idea at all what he had done.

He wrote nothing down. That was the discipline and it was his own, and it turned out to cost him more than the notebook did.

At ten past ten he got the 11 back and rode it into the city and put the inventory sheet into the drawer in the records room where it belonged, and it was the only thing he had done all morning that anybody anywhere had asked him for.

Then he went back in and put the week's inventory sheet into the drawer in the records room where it belonged, and then he went and telephoned the hall, because the corridor light on the second landing had been out since Thursday and somebody in that building was going to have to own it, and he owned it, and it took four minutes.

After that he had the rest of the Saturday, and he had nowhere to be in it, and that was new.
