# Chapter 102 — A Woman Who Counts Addresses

He got the 11 at ten past nine on Sunday morning, the thirtieth of November, the eighty-fifth day, because it was the only one he could afford at short notice and because he had worked out on Saturday night that a bus fare was going to be a cost of this and that he should start paying it in the cheapest currency available.

Cliff Marner was fifty-five days out of the ground. Della Marner was eighty-five days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was eighty-five days in, and the wall on Ash Street had no room left on it, and both of those were facts that had nothing to do with why he was on a bus at ten past nine on a Sunday.

Cutbank Row on a Sunday morning in late November is a street of people carrying bags. There was a queue at the newsagent and post office at number two that went out of the door and turned, and a man in a high-visibility vest at the end of it taking a leaflet off a pile and putting it back on the pile. The boatyard along the cut had a radio going in an open door. Everything on that street was open, and nothing on that street was free, and the two facts sat on top of each other the way they always did in a place where people are being asked to leave.

He walked it end to end twice looking for what an address was, and could not tell, and that turned out to be the first useful thing he learned.

---

The woman he wanted was at number nineteen with her front door open eight inches and a folded sheet of paper in her right hand, and she was holding it the way you hold a thing you have been holding for a long time.

Elias Rook was four steps away when she went in and shut the door, and it was not fast, and it was not a slam, and there was no second sound at all, and he stood on a doorstep on a Sunday morning and understood that a door had just closed on him in a way that was not rudeness.

"Good morning," he said to the door.

"Morning."

"I was going to ask whether anybody keeps a list of addresses in this street."

The door opened about four inches again. "Why."

"Because I am trying to work out what an address is in a district that is being cleared, and I have been in a building for twelve weeks where the answer to that question is written down by somebody in a different building, and I have started to think the answer is not written down anywhere."

The woman looked at him for about four seconds through a four-inch gap. She was sixty-three or thereabouts and she had a kitchen chair on the landing behind her and a folded sheet of paper still in her hand.

"Two," she said.

"Sorry?"

"Two people. I know two." The door came another inch. "Come in out of it, you're letting the cold up the stairs for everybody."

---

Her name was Winifred Alagoa. She had lived on Cutbank Row for thirty-eight years and the flat above her had been her mother's before that, and she made tea twice because the first pot was for measuring and the second was for drinking, and she did not offer him milk because there was none.

The door at the bottom of the stairs was shut and the door at the top was shut and there was a folded towel on the sill of the one behind her, and he understood the towel about an hour later.

"Milk," she said, when he asked. "No. Sorry. There is not any. Have the tea strong, it is that or it is nothing."

The kitchen was a two-room flat with a boiler that made a noise like somebody moving furniture in the next room, and a table with a cloth on it, and a chair on the table-side that was not for visitors and she said so before he could move it.

"Sit there. That is the arm that works. This is November and this is my boiler and you will be here long enough."

He was there for four hours.

---

"I do not call it a record," she said, in about the first twenty minutes, when he had used the word. "And I would not call it a document either, and if you wrote down *document* I would want to know why."

"What would you call it."

"The sheet." She said it as though that were a completely reasonable answer to a completely reasonable question. "Mrs. Ogundipe at number nine has a folder. I have a sheet."

"How long."

"Since 2031." She put the second pot on. "Eleven years this coming spring, and the exact date is the fourth of March because it is my husband's birthday, and the year is the year the board put a list up on the fence at the yard with a number on it that did not have the flats in it."

She looked at him when she said that, to see whether he understood, and he did not, and she told him, and it took six minutes and it was not complicated.

"There is a difference," she said, "between a list of houses and a count of people. Both are a number and both go on a board. A count of people is a thing that can be out by a person. A list of houses is a thing that can be out by a house. And when it is out by a house, then the house is not missing, the house is still there, and the only thing that has gone wrong is that somebody has written down the wrong number, and a person cannot be inside the wrong number."

She put her hand on the folded paper on the table without straightening it.

"**A count of people is the thing I would not hold. A list of houses is the thing I would.** I have watched both. I watched a man come through this street in 2031 with a clipboard and a headcount and he did the ones who answered the door on a Saturday, and you know what a Saturday gets you. And I have watched a board put up a figure twice since and be wrong twice, and both times it was buildings, and both times the street was fine."

"How many have you got."

"One thousand one hundred and sixty-three."

He did the thing his whole working life does to a number and could not do it, because she had not said what she was counting and there were two answers and he had to ask.

"Addresses," she said. "Houses. Doors, if you want to be pedantic, and I am not being pedantic, a door is a door. One thousand one hundred and sixty-three doors on this side of the cut and up the hill as far as the school."

She watched him do the other arithmetic instead of the one he had come for, and let him.

"The board has a model that says one thousand one hundred and forty," she said, before he got there, "and I have never seen that model and I am not interested in what it says. I have got a sheet and a sheet is what I can check. I go down it on a Saturday, same as I have done since 2031, and I put a mark by anything that is empty that was not empty in the spring, and I have never once got to the end of it and been wrong about a door."

---

He asked her the questions he had come with, one at a time, in the wrong order, and noticed he was doing it in the wrong order at about the second one and did not reorganize.

"Who else has one?"

"Two people that I know of and I am not going to tell you who they are, and it is not secrecy, it is that the day I tell one person that I have got a sheet, the day somebody from a building comes and asks whether they can have a look at it, and then it is their sheet. I stopped asking in about '35."

"Has anybody ever asked you about it."

And that was the one that cost him, and he knew it was the one that cost him while he was asking it, and he asked it anyway because the other two were easy.

She took about four minutes over it, and she did not get angry, and she did not get grand, and she looked at the tablecloth.

"Eleven years," she said. "Nobody. A woman from the health center asked me in the spring about the boiler and wrote the answer down and did not ask one single thing about the sheet, and she was in a hurry, and that is not a complaint about her. And in 2034 a man from the board stood on that step and asked me a great deal and I told him a number and he wrote the number down and went away, and the number was right, and he did not come back, and I do not think that was anybody being cruel. That is what happens. You ask, you get an answer, and you have got your answer."

"That is not what I meant," Elias Rook said, and stopped, because it was exactly what he had meant, and it was the reason he had asked.

"Then you will have to ask me something else," Winifred Alagoa said, "and it will cost you the same and you may as well have had the tea."

---

He did not ask for the sheet.

He came into that flat at ten past ten in the morning with the whole shape of what he wanted in his head — the second list in existence, the only one written by a person, the thing a room could be given and be changed by — and he did not ask, because a woman had told him that asking was the moment a sheet stopped being hers, and because she had not said that at all. She had said a different thing, about a man in 2034 and a number that was right.

He asked her instead whether he could write down the fact that the sheet existed. Not the numbers, not a page, not a street.

She thought about it for a long time, and the boiler moved furniture in the next room, and outside a lorry went past on the soft road and the whole building took it.

"You may write down that it exists," she said. "That is all. And you will say where you got it, and if anybody comes with a form I want to be told there is no second copy and that it is the sheet and not a copy of the sheet. Do not write down the number. If you write down the number, then it is a thing that exists in two places, and the first place is better than the second place."

"That is fair."

"It is not fair, it is eleven years." She got up and put the two cups in the sink. "You may write down that it exists. **You may not have the sheet, and I am not saying no to you out of meanness, and I want that in whatever you are writing, because I have been in this street thirty-eight years and I have watched about nine people be pleasant to me in a way that was a way of getting something, and this is the first one that has been the other way round.**"

"You are saying yes to a smaller thing than I asked for."

"I am saying yes to the smallest thing you asked for," she said, "and you asked for it, and I have noticed that, and I am going to leave it there."

---

The tea went cold twice and she made it again and the second time she put the milk in even though there was none, out of the habit of a person who has poured for somebody before.

Elias Rook found out at about twenty to two that she had been born in a house at the top of Marden Row in 1979, that her husband had been a man who could mend a boat and had died in 2033, and that she had had no children and had wanted two.

What he wrote down, in the notebook, on the Sunday night, on a page he did not tear out, was the fact that the sheet existed, and where he got it, and that he had not asked for it, and underneath that, because it had been going round his head all afternoon:

> **A thing nobody has ever asked her about, for eleven years, is worth more to her than a thing I could have been given in a minute. I have spent twelve weeks learning what a refusal costs. I have never once been in a room where the refusal was about eleven years of nobody bothering.**

He read it and did not underline it and did not show it to anybody, and on Monday morning it was still true, and by Friday it had cost him something he had not gone looking for.
