# Chapter 139 — Five On The Back Of My Hand

Cliff Marner was ninety-two days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and twenty-two days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and twenty-two days in, and Elias Rook was on the east side of Marlow Street at half past five on the morning of Tuesday the sixth of January with a lamp in his coat and about eleven hours in front of him.

The barriers went up at six. The lorry came in behind them with the ladder on it and four lamps in a rack, and the crew came in in four vehicles behind that, and the first thing the foreman did was walk the block once at a quarter past six with his hands in his pockets, from the low wall at one end to the low wall at the other, and say nothing to anybody.

---

There were five door numbers on that side. Fourteen, sixteen, eighteen, twenty, twenty-two. He had counted them in daylight on the Monday of the previous week and had been told there were nine kitchens, and he had not written it down, and at about six in the evening a woman came out of number sixteen with a suitcase, set it down on the pavement and stood next to it, and did not pick it up again until the street had been walked.

The crew was four men and Peart had a fifth man for the lorry and the barriers, and none of the four would talk to Elias Rook for about an hour, and then one of them, a man of about thirty called Doy, told him where to stand and why.

"You stand there because that is out of the way," Doy said, "and if you are standing anywhere else you will be in somebody's way at about ten past seven and I will have to move you, and I do not want to move you, because you will not go where I tell you and I will have to argue with you in front of five doors."

He stayed where he was until the barriers came down.

---

Between six and half past seven he moved a cone four times, held a barrier arm while a lorry reversed, carried a flask from a car to a woman sitting in her own front room with her coat on, and was sent to the shop at the bottom of Cutbank Row and back for a bag of bread and butter for a crew of four, which took twenty-two minutes and which nobody thanked him for and which is in no document anywhere in the world.

"What actually happens," he said to Doy, at about seven, when nothing was happening.

"Nothing happens until half past seven and then everything happens for ten hours," Doy said. "We knock. We say who we are and who we are with. We give you time. We do not go while you are in the room, we go when you are in the vehicle, and if you are not in the vehicle by half four in the morning we go anyway and the woman at the top of the ladder has to be told that by somebody in uniform." He pulled his gloves tighter. "The whole thing is as slow as the slowest person in it and that is the entire design, and the office does not like it, and the office has never once been here at seven in the morning to see what it looks like."

At about half past seven the first door came off its latch, sixty feet away from him, and he did not go and look, because the crew had not asked him to and because he had been told that the inside of those five doors was four men's business and his business was a road.

At about eight a lamp went into the frame of number eighteen and stayed there. At about eight another came out of number twenty and was handed to a man who carried it back in. At about a quarter to nine a kettle went off somewhere behind number fourteen. At about nine a man came down from a flat over number eighteen, stood at the barrier for two hours asking four different people the same question and getting the same answer, and went back up.

At about half past ten a woman at number twenty put her head out and asked Doy whether she was the last one, and Doy said she was the second and he would come and tell her when she was the last, and she said, *will you knock or will you shout*, and Doy said he would knock.

---

At about eleven o'clock a lamp went flat in the frame of number twenty and the man who was holding it came out of the doorway and shouted for a spare, and the spares were in the rack on the lorry and the lorry was a hundred yards away at the barrier with a man sitting in it.

Then a woman came out of number twenty with her hands full and a man of about sixty came out behind her and could not get his lamp and his wife's cardigan and the door frame at the same time, and Elias Rook took the lamp out of his coat and put it in the frame and held it there with his right hand, and the left one hung at his side and did nothing, which was its job, and he stood in a doorway in the cold for about two hours holding a rubbish lamp bought for a pound and something in a shop on Christmas Eve while two people in overalls got one person out of a first-floor flat and into a vehicle, and nobody asked him to and nobody told him to and nobody looked at him except Peart, once, at about half past eleven, from the middle of the road, and then looked away.

Nobody said anything about it afterward at all.

---

At about four in the morning two men from the crew went to one of the five doors and were inside it, or on it, or under it, or all three, for the better part of an hour, and came out and went back to the lorry, and Elias Rook did not know which door it had been and did not ask and was not going to, because two of the eleven were on that street and he had known that since the previous Tuesday, and the whole of what he had for eleven hours was the fact that somewhere in five doors a crew had spent an hour and he had been sixty feet away from it on a road with a lamp in his hand doing nothing at all.

He did not sleep. He sat down twice on the low wall at the north end and stood up again both times, and at about half past five in the morning the crew walked the street at the end, two of them together, from one low wall to the other, looking at everything, and one of them said *that is it* and the other one said nothing, and the barriers came down at twenty to seven, and the lorry went out with the ladder on it and the four lamps in the rack.

Nobody was hurt. That was the first thing Nerys Hobbs said to him, standing at the trestle with her pen already out, and she said it before anything else, and she said it the way a woman says a thing she has been saying in her own head for eleven hours and has just found out is true.

"Nobody was hurt," Hobbs said.

"Two of the eleven were on this street," Elias Rook said. "I have known that since Tuesday. I do not know which doors and I have not asked and I am not going to."

"You are not going to," Hobbs said, "and neither am I, and it is not a compliment to you. Those two are on eleven cards in a wallet in a drawer in this building, and the cards are the works' own, and the name on either of them is the only copy in any system in the world except the back of a three-year-old discharge list at a clinic. If either of those women had gone down in that street tonight, the only record of it would be a form in my hand and a foreman's word, and a foreman's word about a woman he was told about by a clinic manager who got it off the back of a sheet she has never been asked for by anybody."

She signed at ten past seven. One line out of four, signed, dated, with the hour.

"One of four," she said, when he looked at it. "The other three are the tenth, the seventeenth and the nineteenth, and the last of those is a Monday, and I sign that one on the Monday at about half past six in the morning, and I would like it noticed that I have now said the word Monday out loud in January and that nobody in this building has yet come up with a reason to move it."

He had five on the back of his left hand in biro, written with his right hand just after the barriers came down, on a cold pavement, by a man sitting on a low wall because his legs had stopped working about two hours before that. It was the correct number of door numbers on one side of one street. The woman at number eighteen had told him on the twenty-ninth of December that there were nine kitchens behind those five, and both numbers were true, and nobody had said a word about either, and he was not going to.

He got the 11 at ten past seven in the morning and sat on the far side of the cut for about twenty minutes with the lamp back in his coat and the biro still on his hand and the thread on under the whole of Marlow Street for eleven hours and not asked for anything, and the next night was in four days and it was a Saturday and it was Rennard and Murrow and a crew of five, and after that the seventeenth with Cutbank Row, and the fourth was Rennard and Cutbank Row on a Monday because a booking is a booking. He had been on a road for eleven hours and had held one door frame for about two of them, and there was no document anywhere on earth that said any part of it, and there was not going to be one, and the last person who had said anything useful to him about it was a foreman on a bench on Monday afternoon, in about forty words, and had not said it to be thanked for.
