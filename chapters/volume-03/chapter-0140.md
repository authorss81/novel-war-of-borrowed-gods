# Chapter 140 — What He Did Not Write Down

Cliff Marner was ninety-three days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and twenty-three days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and twenty-three days in, and Elias Rook got into the front room on Ash Street at nine on Wednesday morning, the seventh of January, having slept about three hours, and told the room what had happened on Marlow Street in the words it had happened in, standing up, with a biro mark still on the back of his left hand.

There were nineteen people in that room at nine on a Wednesday morning in January, which was more than there usually was, and nobody had come to hear him.

"Four men," Elias Rook said. "A ladder out and not a carry. Five door numbers on one side of that street and a woman at eighteen told me in December there are nine kitchens behind them and both of those are true. A lamp battery went flat at about eleven at night and the spare was in the lorry a hundred yards away, so I gave them mine and held the frame for about two hours, and a man of sixty got his wife and a cardigan and a door frame out at the same time, which I would not have managed. Eleven hours and ten minutes, from half past seven at night to twenty to seven in the morning. A man from a flat over eighteen came down at nine at night to ask four people the same question and went back up. At about four in the morning two men spent an hour at one of those five doors and I did not know which one and I did not ask. The street was walked at about half past five in the morning. Nobody was hurt."

He put his left hand out, palm up, on the table edge, and did not go near it with the right one, and there was a five on it in biro.

"That is the number of doors," he said. "That is what I was given to do, on Monday, by a foreman, and I did it, and it took four minutes, and I have written it on my own hand and it is not in any document and I am not going to put it in one, and if the number of doors is what this district is going to be counted in for the next three weeks then I would like somebody to tell me tonight, in this room, before I get it wrong on a Saturday."

Nobody answered him, and one woman near the back said *no* under her breath, and that was the whole of the consultation.

---

In the front room at about eleven, after the heat came on, Inez Sato read the two lines in Petar Lisk's book, the vote and the four minutes above it, and then looked at him for slightly too long.

"You came in here on Sunday morning and told me a lamp in your coat was not this building's," she said, "and I voted for a light over the stairs on Sunday night and eleven pounds and fifty went out of a float, and you have come in here on Wednesday morning with a mark on your hand and told a room that you held a door frame for two hours, and I want to say the thing I am going to say and then I am going to go to bed. Those are two different lamps and they are going to stay two different lamps, and the day this building merges them is the day the float is my decision and not six people's, and I am not going to let that happen because a man from Tidegate turned up with a good story on a Wednesday."

"That is fair."

"It is not a kindness," Inez Sato said, "it is housekeeping. You said in December that a lamp on a table in a room with twenty-eight people in it is a thing that gets voted on within a week, and you were right, and it got voted on, and it is in a book, and the book is where it is supposed to be and not on a wall. Your lamp is in your coat. That is all I have ever understood about it and that is enough."

She closed the lock-box and stopped with her hand on the lid, and went to bed at half past eleven in the morning because she had been up since five.

---

Cliff Marner had not spoken until the room had mostly cleared.

"Ninety-three days," he said. "I have got a piece of paper with my name on it from the sixth of September and a name in chalk on a wall in four buildings and a woman whose name is chalk and not in a system, and I have not asked anybody about any of it in three weeks because I worked out in November that I was the only person in this room who was allowed to ask me things, which turned out to be a thing I had made up and was wrong about."

He turned his head about an inch toward Elias Rook's hand on the table.

"Was the man who held the frame the man who counted the doors," he said.

"Yes."

"Then somebody is going to have to write that down one day," Cliff Marner said, "and it is not going to be any of us, and I have thought about that and it is the correct arrangement, and I am not bitter about it, and I would like it noted that I noticed."

---

Joon Park came in at about two and did not take the custody schedule out of his coat.

"I have had a list since the twenty-seventh of December," he said, "of four things this building could have done in four weeks and did not do, and I have been the only one keeping it, and I have told you that once and I am telling you the rest of it now. It is a record of what we did not do, and it is not going on a pad, because a list of things you should have done is a form with a column for a person in it, and I have never once been able to write one of those without a name at the bottom of it."

He put his hands in his sleeves.

"Last night was the first night since the twenty-seventh of December that nothing went on it," he said. "There is not a line on that page for Tuesday the sixth, and there was no line for any night in December either, and I have not written anything down tonight, and I am saying it in a room on purpose, because it is the only thing that happened in this district in four weeks and it is an absence, and I would like it to be in a room rather than in my head, and I would like nobody in this room to stand up and call it a win, because I have been in a room before where an absence of harm got written down as an achievement by a man with a form, and it is on that list, and it is the reason there are four items on it and not none."

---

He wrote the notebook up at the table in the front room at about four in the afternoon, under the two dates that were still the second and the sixteenth of January, and the second of January was five days ago and had come back with nothing, and he did not write that down either, because Joon Park had said the near end out loud in a room at a counter and had not chased it and he was not going to chase it in a book.

He wrote one line.

*Sixth of January, 14–22 Marlow Street. Four men, a ladder, five door numbers, eleven hours and ten minutes, nobody hurt. I held a door frame for two hours with a lamp that cost a pound and something. Nothing I did is in any document and nothing I did was in anybody's paperwork, and nobody asked me to be there and nobody refused me.*

And then he sat with the pen down for a while, because there were five other things he wanted to put under that line and he did not put any of them there.

Two times on a page with nothing written between them, from Thursday morning. Forty-four addresses on a page in a tube upstairs that was not the page he was on. A number on a nail at a yard gate and a different number in the mouth of a woman on Marlow Street. A sentence said at a bollard on Saturday morning to about fifteen people in the cold, which belonged to her and had not been asked for by anybody. And a page in a folder in the Meridian with a copy in it that could not be checked.

He left all five out, and the reason was not a rule and not a promise and not an argument he had with himself at length, and the reason was that in December a woman in a room off a canteen had told him that he had brought her a piece of paper she could not use and had been kind about it, and that a man's word in a notebook is not a document, and that on Tuesday night he had found out what the second half of that sentence is worth, because he had a genuine fact and there was nobody in the world he was entitled to give it to, and he had wanted to, and had not.

He closed the notebook and put it in his coat with the lamp in the other pocket, and the envelope to Nell was where it had been since September, unsent, the reason four words long.

In three days it was the tenth of January, which was a Saturday, and it was Rennard and Murrow and a crew of five, and after that the seventeenth with Cutbank Row, and the nineteenth with Rennard and Cutbank Row on a Monday at the end of it because a booking is a booking. There is a vote in a book on a shelf and four minutes above it and a name on a list that is not in the custody schedule, and three questions on a wall with no room on it, and twenty-two cards on a rail and twenty-eight people, and four dark hours from midnight, and a man with a rubbish lamp in a coat who has now been on one of four nights and has three to go and a notebook with nothing in the middle of two lines, and no part of any of it is on a form.
