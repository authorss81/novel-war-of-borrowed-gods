# Chapter 115 — Three To Twenty-Seven

Cliff Marner was sixty-eight days out of the ground. Della Marner was ninety-eight days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was ninety-eight days in, and the Havenport Tidal Board and the Bellwether cell's public counter had shut at six o'clock on Friday night and were shut until Monday, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Saturday morning, the thirteenth of December, with his right hand still aching from the day before.

The left hand was gone and it made the bus slow. He carried the folder under the right arm and the tube against his leg, and held the rail with the right hand where the two middle knuckles had locked the day before and then loosened overnight. He could close the hand now and could hold a cup if he was careful. He put a chemist's plaster on the second right knuckle before he left Ash Street because the skin had split on the brick on Sallow Court, and by Cutbank Row the plaster had come off inside his glove and he left it there.

---

Winifred Alagoa read her sheet at the bollard on Cutbank Row on Saturday mornings the way she had done since 2031, and Elias Rook stood on the far side of the street and listened without crossing.

She did Rennard Street first, one to thirty-one, and Murrow Street second, and Marlow third, street by street, number by number, with the dates she had gone and looked. About twenty people stopped and none of them thought it was remarkable. A man in a high-visibility vest from the yard stood with a sandwich in his hand and listened for his own street. Two women came out of the post office at number two and stood behind her.

"Sallow Court I do last," she said, "because I have to be careful with Sallow Court."

She did it last and said the thirty-nine doors the way she always did, and did not say why she was careful, and Elias Rook understood that the difference between forty-one and thirty-nine had been on her table since June and was not closed and was not going to be closed by anybody standing on Cutbank Row on a Saturday.

He did not go near her. He had four names in his head that were in her margin and on the back of a young man's hand, and the rule had run out on Thursday night, and he had decided on Thursday that the answer was not a knock on four doors. He lifted his right hand once when she looked across, and she nodded once, and that was the whole of what passed between them.

A woman of about sixty-five with a shopping bag asked him whether the list was the program's and he said no, it was a woman's own sheet and had been kept since 2031 because the board's list had been wrong twice. The woman said that was a long time to be right without anybody asking and he said yes, it was, and she went back to listening. He stood for the full hour the way he had stood the Saturday before, on the far side of the street, saying nothing, because the Saturday before he had learned that the practice he had spent twelve weeks learning had been happening on a corner without him and did not need a wall.

---

The yard gate carried a printed card he had not stopped to read before, ordinary works practice, behind glass. The alignment went from open to closed in three stages, with dates, and after the closure the public side could not be stood on. He read the dates twice because a season was a window and the window was the thing the whole district was living inside. Sallow Court was on the public side until the new year. After that a man could not put his hand on a pier and learn anything, because he could not stand there. He copied no date into the notebook, because the card belonged to the works and not to him, and he had the folder and the tube and nothing else that needed carrying.

The yard ran six days and Saturday was when the program's own people were most visible. The canteen did lunch and the notices went up on the trestle by the gate, and the generator hummed behind the canteen door the way it always did on Saturdays. Nev Peart was fifty-one and had been foreman nineteen years, and his shift book was on the tail of the bench by the kettle with a rubber band round it, and Elias Rook did not put a hand on it.

"Morning," Peart said. "You are not on my sheet."

"I am not here for the sheet," Elias Rook said. "Before I say anything else I am going to tell you that I have not told this yard what I am about to do, and I am telling you now and not afterwards. I am going to put my hand on the gate pier and look at what is under this block. It will take about three minutes. Then I am going to tell you what happened in the words it happened in."

Peart looked at the gate pier, which was brick with a stone cap, part of the works and not part of the program.

"My pier," he said. "Put your hand on it and do it, and then tell me."

He laid his right hand on the stone cap, bare palm to the cold, because the left would have told him nothing and would have slipped. The thread was live before he had settled his weight. It was live the way it had been live in the room above number six, faint and exact. Off on the far side of the cut. Off in the Cinder Quarter. Live here, on the alignment, on ground he was being asked about.

Direction and stress came through the stone the way it had come through brick and wood. It revealed where force wanted to go and nothing else. It was not a repair. It could not tell him how many people were on the street. It could not be carried out of the place he stood — he knew he could not write it and take it to anybody. Hand on the structure was required. He kept it there for three minutes while the yard went on around him, a lorry moving on the low road, the canteen door opening and shutting.

The line was off Sallow Court. It had been off it on Thursday and on Friday and it was off it now. The block the clearance had run on, on the nights of the eighth and ninth of November, was not carrying what the September roll said it carried. The December schedule still called every address on it transitional, and the schedule was dated the second of December and governed where the reading did not, and all of that was true at once and nobody had lied.

He lifted his hand and Peart looked at him.

"It is quiet," Elias Rook said, in the words it happened in. "The ground on this block went quiet a month after four people died on it. That is a fact about force and not about anybody. I cannot write it and I cannot hand it to you."

Peart nodded slowly. "A month after," he said. "The ninth of November to the twenty-ninth. That is a month in anybody's book."

"It is not a document," Elias Rook said. "You cannot build to it and you cannot sign it."

"No," Peart said. "I have a book I write in with a rubber band round it and I am not going to write that in it, because my book is for what my crew did and not for what the ground did."

---

The aftermath came on the bus stop on Marden Row, the way the pre-canon of this kind of work said it would and the way nothing in his thirteen weeks had prepared him for.

For about an hour afterwards he could see the stress in structures he was not touching. The shelter frame at the stop showed a load running through it that was not that shelter's load today, a weight coming down at an angle the poles were never meant to carry, pale and exact at the edge of sight. It showed for the chemist's shopfront, for the school fence, for the low wall by the newsagent. None of it could be refreshed and none of it could be willed. He looked at it and then looked away, and it stayed whether he looked or not, for the hour, and then it thinned.

He sat on the bench with the folder under his right arm and did not write anything, because there was nothing that could be written that anybody could use. His nose bled at about half past two, suddenly, without any pain, onto his glove. He held the glove to his face until it stopped, and lost the hour between half past two and half past three the way a man loses an hour to sleep without sleeping. The shelter frame still showed the wrong load when he looked again, then thinned to nothing by the time a bus came. When he got up the folder was still under his arm and the tube was against his leg and the plaster was still inside his glove with blood on the edge of it.

He got the 11 at ten past four with his back bad and his right hand locked again at the two knuckles, and rode into the city with the thread quiet over the water. Two uses on consecutive days had locked the hand for an hour and a half yesterday and had locked it again today. One Saturday had given him a nosebleed and a lost hour. The strain was bodily and it was not a meter, and there was no version of it that could be shown to anybody as proof.

At Ash Street he washed the glove in the laundry with the right hand and hung it up, and put the bloody plaster in the bin, and Joon Park looked at the back of his own hand where four names in pencil were fading and said nothing about Thursday night. The hall held twenty-eight people and twenty-two resident cards, and the four dark hours came at midnight, and nobody in that building asked him what he had stood on that day.

He counted the fares on the stairs with the right hand because the left could not hold the coins. Two pounds seventy-five each way, eleven minutes each way, nine days and then four more, and about twenty-two dollars across the first batch and more since, which was the figure Inez Sato had made him say out loud on the second of December so that Joon Park would work it out and not ask. The sleep was the Tuesday at half past six that cost him an hour a week for the rest of his life. The travel was the 11. The money was the fares. They came in that order and nobody paid them but him, and the hall did not become a second headquarters for a district two miles away, because the districts stayed separate and the cost stayed his.
