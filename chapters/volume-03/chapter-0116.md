# Chapter 116 — What I Have Not Told This Room

Cliff Marner was sixty-nine days out of the ground. Della Marner was ninety-nine days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was ninety-nine days in, and Elias Rook woke on Sunday morning, the fourteenth of December, in a hall on Ash Street with no bus to catch and no counter open anywhere in the city, and his right hand still stiff at the two knuckles.

The left hand was gone and it made Sundays slow in a different way. He dressed with the right hand and teeth, pinned the blanket with the right forearm while he folded it, and carried the folder under the right arm down to the front room because it was the only thing he owned that Tidegate had given him and he did not know where else to put it. The notebook was in his coat. The envelope to Nell was in the front of it, unsent, and the reason was four words long. The road is sealed.

Petar Lisk read the board at seven in the morning, out loud twice.

Three questions, in chalk, in his own hand and in two other hands. Who edited the Kestrel response record. What the Office believed a single approved outcome would prevent, with the second half still a bracket with nothing under it. Who benefits when a branch is officially deleted and why does the form call it an error, in capitals, in a nine-year-old's hand. Under the last two, in a hand nobody else in the building had, 19 November. There was no room left, not for a line and not for a name, and when it was finished there would be a space the shape of a shoe.

Elias stood in the doorway with nine people in the front room and listened without adding anything. Twenty-two resident cards were on the rail and twenty-eight people were in the building, and the four dark hours had ended at four. Nobody took anything off the wall and nobody added to it and nobody answered it. Petar put the pencil behind his ear the other way round and said there was no room and whoever came after him would think somebody had been tidy, and Inez Sato said nothing, because somebody had to build another wall and it was not going to be anybody in that building.

Cliff Marner was in the chair by the window with a blanket out of the store room because the heating in that building was a war of opinions and the store room always won. He had been in the front room on the Thursday when a man of sixty-four explained why people disappear, and he had said nothing then and said nothing now, and his being in the room was the whole of his entry most mornings. Ru Sabar said it is upside down when Elias read the board in the wrong order the week before, and this morning she only nodded at him, because Sundays in that building belonged to Petar and nobody else needed to perform them. Elias pinned the folder with his right forearm while he moved the chair out of the corridor, because the corridor light on the second landing had been out since the twenty-sixth of November and he owned it and had telephoned about it on the twenty-ninth, and the chair left in the corridor was still where somebody had left it. The heater in that room ticked and gave up about every nine minutes, and four people had learned not to say anything across it.

---

Joon Park was at the table with the custody schedule in front of him, twenty-two lines in his own hand, and the back of his right hand still carrying four names in pencil, faded now at the edges from washing.

"You have been in that district every day but today," Joon Park said. "Fifteen days, and this is the day off. Are you going to tell this room what you have got."

It was the room that wanted a sentence. Nine people, Sunday morning, a folder that had not been opened in three days and a tube against the wall. Elias put the folder on the table end to end with nothing else, and stood up, which he did not need to do.

"Before I say anything else I am going to tell you that I have not told this room what I am about to do, and I am telling you now and not afterwards," he said. "That is step four and I am saying it with nothing behind it."

Inez Sato looked up from the lock-box. "Then say it with nothing behind it. This building has heard worse."

"I was given a reading," Elias Rook said. "On Thursday, on Sallow Court, with my hand on a wall. A future engineer nineteen years ahead offered it, and I told Miss Hobbs the terms in her room the same afternoon and Mr. Anum in his on Friday, and the terms are the reason I am telling this room and not the counter. I said yes."

He said it in the words it happened in, before he acted on it, to the people who would pay for it if he got it wrong. The offer went to the room the way Mara's rule required — any offer went to the people who paid for it, in the words it was said in, before it was acted on. He did not ask anybody whether he should have taken it. He did not ask for silence to mean anything. The bilateral practice was somebody else's and he had not started it.

"The line moved," he said. "Off Sallow Court and onto Marlow Street. I felt it on Thursday and on Friday in the room above number six and on Saturday on the gate pier. It is live when I am standing on ground I am being asked about and it is off on the far side of the cut and off in this building. I cannot tell you how many people are on a street, and I cannot carry any of it out of the place I stood, and nothing I am saying this morning has repaired anything. And for about an hour after I take my hand off a structure I can see loads in buildings I am not touching, and there is no way to refresh that and no way to will it."

Nobody said anything for about four seconds.

"Can you show us," Petar Lisk said.

"No," Elias Rook said. "That is the whole of it. I have a gift nobody in this room can check. If I say it in this room it is my word and not a document, and nobody can build to it and nobody can sign it."

Joon Park looked at the back of his hand and then at the custody schedule with its twenty-two items, and did not pick up the pen.

"We do not know that," he said, quietly, to the room that wanted a sentence about a seam. "We do not know whether the line moved. Elias says it moved and I believe he is telling us what happened to him, and what happened to him is not a record. There is no second copy and no form and no date anybody else can check. If anybody in this city asks me where we got it I will say a man put his hand on a wall and that is the whole of what I will say."

It was the only sentence anybody in this city could say in a room that wanted a sentence, and he had said it, and the room let it stand.

---

Afterwards Inez walked him to the corridor where the door did not close properly, and neither of them raised their voice at any point, and he could not have said afterwards who had started it.

"You told a yard foreman and a method officer before you told us," she said. "That is the right order. The people who pay for it first."

"I told Hobbs in a room with the door shut," Elias Rook said. "I told Anum in his own room. I told Peart at his own pier. I did not tell the counter and I did not tell the board and I did not put a Tidegate address anywhere near the residents' file."

"Good," Inez said. "The districts stay separate and the hall does not become a second headquarters. You paid the fares and the sleep and you came home. That is the arrangement."

She went back in. He stood in the corridor with the folder under his right arm and the left hanging empty, and thought about the terrible use the way he had thought about it on the bus on Friday, with nobody watching. He could become the schedule. He could walk into Tidegate with a thread that told him which ground was live and put readings into rooms that nobody could check. He thought about it, and he did not do it, and he told nobody he had not done it, and nobody let him off, because there was no notice in any building that told him he was allowed to stop.

At nine o'clock he went to bed with a back that would hurt on the Tuesday, and the notebook in his coat, and the four names still on the back of another man's hand, and a gift that could not leave the rooms it was taken in.

Before that Mara Okafor telephoned from the city, because Sundays were the day she caught up on paper, and he took it in the corridor with the door not closing properly.

"The chemist on Marden Row is still going," she said. "Out of his own van, out of his own money, forty older residents, told on the twenty-fifth of November that a transitional address cannot be billed. He runs out in the middle of January. About sixty people."

"Ninety-one came with letters," Elias said. "You told me the number and not what it means."

"That is still the arrangement," Mara said. "Four refusals intact and I am not explaining them. I am telling you because you are going to be in rooms with people who can make it go away, and I am not. That is my question and it is clinical and administrative and nobody has made the moral point, and the moral point is not mine to make."

"Understood," Elias said, with the folder under his right arm and the left hanging empty. "Four o'clock is what you said on Tuesdays and you mean it."

"Four o'clock is what I said and I mean it," she said, and put the handset down.

He stood in the corridor for a minute with nothing in his hands that anybody could check, and then went upstairs.
