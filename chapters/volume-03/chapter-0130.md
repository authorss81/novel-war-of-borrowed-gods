# Chapter 130 — What You Would Have Had To Do

Cliff Marner was eighty-three days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and thirteen days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and thirteen days in, and Elias Rook was on Ash Street on Sunday morning, the twenty-eighth of December, because the yard was shut and the counter was shut and the board was shut and the card behind the glass on the gate said twenty-five, twenty-six, twenty-seven and twenty-eight in a typed line and meant it.

The four dark hours had ended at four. There were twenty-two resident cards on the rail and twenty-eight people in the building and it had been that number every day since September.

---

Petar Lisk read the board at seven in the morning, out loud twice, and the second half of the oldest was still a bracket with nothing under it and the nineteenth of November was still the only date on that wall, and there was no room and there was not going to be a second wall.

He put the file of working logs back on the top shelf of the records room at about eight, with the 1998 one at the bottom of it, and did not take it out again, and told nobody he had done it, and was asked by two people and answered both of them the same way, which was that it was on a shelf where it had been and that was the correct place for it on a Sunday.

The four minutes were in a book on a shelf in the front room. The refused question was on the back of a form in a building in another district, in a method officer's ink, because there was nothing on the front of it. Two dates were in pencil on the inside of a young man's forearm. Four dead people were in half an inch of margin in a kitchen pencil in a house on Cutbank Row and on the back of the same forearm in ballpoint, and were on no schedule and had no number, and were going to be named out of a woman's margin in January or they were not going to be named at all.

Nobody took anything off the wall and nobody answered anything on it.

---

He read the works' sequence out in the front room at eleven on Sunday morning with thirteen people in it, because that is the arrangement and the arrangement is Inez Sato's.

Four rows in the works' own words. A night, a block, a crew number. The sixth of January was the first of them, the nineteenth was the last of them, and two of the four were on Saturdays, and there was nothing in it about who lived at any address, and he said so, in those words, standing up, with his left hand hanging at his side.

"Miss Inez is right that a rule with two people in it does not travel. This is me telling a room what a piece of paper is and not what it is, and I am not holding it up, and I am not going to write it down for anybody, and there is not a Tidegate address in this room and there is not going to be one."

"What is on it, then," Petar Lisk said.

"Four nights and four crews," Elias Rook said. "That is all it has ever had. If somebody in a good position gives you the whole of that document, and somebody will, and it will be done kindly, then you should know before you are given it that there is nothing on it about a person, and that a crew number is a number of men and not a count of who is in the houses they go into, and that the reason I am telling you this on a Sunday in a room with no paper in it is that I do not want this building able to say later that it did not know."

Cliff Marner said, from the chair by the window, without moving: "You have said it four times now in a fortnight, that you do not want us to be able to say later that we did not know."

"That is true."

"Then it is the third most useful thing anybody has done in this building since September," Marner said, "and I have not got the other two and I am not going to ask."

"Then here is the fourth," Elias Rook said. "Four nights, four crews, and there is a man in that yard who will put a torch in his own mouth and go down a stair backwards with a woman in his arms and take forty minutes, and there is an engineer who will sign a sheet for every one of those nights and who has a jar of four photographs on a shelf above a terminal, and there is a woman at number thirty-one who wants to be last, and there is a foreman who has already written eleven words on eleven pieces of his own card. I am not going to tell you that is enough. I have got a lamp and it is rubbish and it costs two pounds ten to run, and a lamp is not a man who has done it before, and on a bad night in a block nobody has walked in daylight the difference between those two things is a person's hip."

"So what are you telling the room," Petar Lisk said.

"That the answer is four men and a torch and about forty minutes a woman, and that it is not in the sheet, and that the sheet is not going to be beaten by me or by anybody in this building, and that I am going to be on that ground on those four nights with a lamp in my coat and I am going to do the one thing I can actually do, which is be the person who has already been on a stair in the dark, and that I am telling you now so that if it goes wrong you can say afterwards that you were told."

---

At about half past one Joon Park put the custody schedule in his coat and came back to the table and sat down across from him, and he did not have a pen, and that was deliberate, and Elias Rook understood it about four seconds afterwards.

"You have had five days," Joon Park said, "and two of them you spent in a building with twenty-eight people in it doing nothing you would write down, and one of them you spent in another district on a Saturday, standing on a pavement in the cold, being told no by a woman with a sheet. So I am going to ask the one question I have been carrying since Tuesday, and then I am going to stop, and it is a question about the twenty-third and not about anybody else."

"Ask it."

"What would you have had to do," Joon Park said.

Elias Rook did not answer it for a while. The heater gave up about every nine minutes, and Inez Sato was at the table with the lock-box, and Petar Lisk had a pencil behind his ear and was not writing, and Ru Sabar was in the doorway.

"About eleven seconds," he said. "That is the honest length of it and I have thought about it for four days and it does not get longer. I would have had to put my hands on eleven people I have never seen, off a list, on a night, in a building, and I would have had to do it in a room with a form in it, and I would have had to do it having already invited a program officer to watch, and I would have called it keeping them alive."

Nobody said anything.

"That is what it would have been," Elias Rook said, "and the reason I have not been able to put it down for four days is that I have spent thirteen weeks building a version of myself who could do that and be right about it, and on Wednesday I found out it is not a different man, it is the same man with a torch in his hand and eleven seconds of mouth in front of him, and the only thing standing between me and it was a woman in another year who had said no to a page."

"You will not be able to do it," Joon Park said. "That is the sentence I came up here to be told and you have not said it."

"I will not be able to do it," Elias Rook said, "and that is not the same as not doing it, and I do not know yet which of those two it is going to turn out to be, and neither do you, and you have said we do not know that about a reading and a wall and you were right then."

Inez Sato closed the lock-box and kept her hand on the lid of it, which she did perhaps twice a year, and said, "Then the arrangement stands and the hall is not a headquarters, and the four minutes are in a book, and I am going to bed."

---

He had bought a hand lamp on Christmas Eve from a shop on the corner for a pound and something, out of a box of eight, and the man in the shop had told him it was rubbish, and it was, and it took two batteries and had a clip on the back of it for a belt, and the batteries cost him a pound and ten and he had fitted them on the 26th and tested it in the corridor at half past nine in the evening with the door open onto a dark stairwell, and it worked.

It was a lamp and not a machine. It was not on the residents' file and it was not on a pad and it was not in a book in the records room and it did not go near the power budget, because it ran on two batteries that a man in a shop sold him and not on a feeder anybody in that building had voted on. It was in his coat. He had thought about putting it on the table in the front room and had decided against it, because a lamp on a table in a room with twenty-eight people in it is a thing that gets voted on within a week, and the day it gets voted on is the day it is the building's, and he would rather it be a man with a lamp in his coat for about four nights in January and then nothing.

---

At ten past midnight the four dark hours came. He went up with the notebook in his coat and the lamp in the other pocket and the envelope to Nell in the front of the notebook, unsent, the reason four words long, and the run was twenty-four days and two Sundays and one Tuesday that cost an hour of sleep a week for the rest of his life, and the costs of going to that district had come in on him in that order every single day since the twenty-ninth of November — eleven minutes and two pounds seventy-five each way, then the sleep, then the money, and not once the other way round.

On the inside back cover of the notebook, in his own hand, there were two dates.

**2 JANUARY / 16 JANUARY**

The yard opened on Monday the twenty-ninth. The works' booking started on Monday the fifth of January and it ran twenty working days to the fifth of February, and a woman of forty-seven had to sign a sheet for every night in it, and on the first of them there would be four crews and a man at the bottom of a ladder, on a ground where a foreman had put eleven cards on a nail with his own initials at the foot of every one of them, and an engineer with a jar of four photographs on a shelf above a terminal that was working again.

Elias Rook took the notebook out with his right hand and wrote one line under the two dates, and it was not a sentence anybody else could use, and it was this.

*Asked on the 23rd for hands I did not need. Told on the 19th. Everybody who did it: a doctor, a foreman, a clinic manager, a man with a ladder.*

He put the notebook back in his coat and the lamp in the other pocket and lay down on a Sunday night in a building with twenty-eight people in it, four dark hours starting at midnight, a wall with three questions on it and nowhere to put a fourth, a book on a shelf with four minutes written in it, and a sheet of a woman's own doors going into January on a Monday with a foreman on it, and nothing whatever done, and a lamp.
