# Chapter 150 — The Nail And The Back Of A School Exercise Book

Cliff Marner was one hundred and three days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and thirty-three days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and thirty-three days in, and Elias Rook was on the far side of the cut at ten past six on Saturday morning, the seventeenth of January, on a day the depot does not run. The depot on the far bank runs Tuesdays and Fridays at half past six, the Friday hose certification had moved to Thursdays on the ninth of January by a letter that said the slots would not be held twice and said nothing whatever about the days, and there is no Saturday on any notice in that yard; R. Dunleavy was in there with the gauge out, and neither man said one word about it. The letter gave him nothing else and it took nothing away, and the Tuesday was the same Tuesday it had been since the end of October.

R. Dunleavy did not do the talking part of it. The needle went up and stayed up and he wrote two figures in a book in his own hand and turned the book round so the page faced the man who had brought the sheet, and the left hand pinned it and the right hand took the pen off him when it was done.

"Twenty minutes," Dunleavy said.

"That is the whole of what you have got to say to me."

"That is the whole of what I have got to say to anybody," Dunleavy said. "I have got a hose and a gauge and a book and the gauge says what it says."

---

He did the arithmetic out loud on the wall by the boatyard on the far side of the cut, on his own, to a man of about seventy who was painting a door and who had not asked him a single question and did not look up.

"It is a quarter to seven on a Tuesday morning when I get to the water," he said. "It is twenty to seven on every other morning of the week, and I have been getting up at twenty to five for a Tuesday since the end of October, and that is an hour and ten minutes, every Tuesday, fifty-two Tuesdays, which is sixty hours and forty minutes a year."

The man with the paint did not look up and said, "Sixty hours is a bit over two days."

"Sixty hours and forty minutes is two days and a bit over twelve hours," Elias Rook said. "So it is two days and thirteen hours a year, which is one day for every calendar year and a half, and the letter that moved the hose to the Thursday has not given me the Thursday, and the Friday that is left is the inventory and I have not had the inventory since the twenty-first of November, so the answer is two days and thirteen hours a year for the rest of whatever this is, and I have got the arithmetic right twice, and I am going to stop."

He had the 11 at ten past eight and the fare was the fare and the run was over at twenty-four days and a hundred and thirty-two pounds in December and had not been restarted and was not going to be.

---

There were two people at the newsagent at number two on Cutbank Row at about ten o'clock with a length of paper and a nail and a hammer, and a third person on a chair from the boatyard holding the middle of it up while they hit.

It was four feet long. It was not ruled. It had four columns drawn on it in pencil, and the first column was the address, and the second was a date, and the third was whether anybody had come to the door, and the fourth was a name, and the heading over the fourth column was in capitals and underlined twice, and about two hundred people had written on it in about two hundred hands in eleven days, and it had started on a trestle at a parents' meeting on the sixth of January and it had been off that trestle and on somebody's kitchen table and in a church hall on Thursday night and in the queue at a shop, and it was going onto a nail above the counter at number two because there was nowhere else in this district for it to go.

"There is a place for it above the door," Mrs Postridge said. "I have a nail for leaflets. It is a good nail, it is a proper nail, and it is a foot and a half from anybody's hand, which is a foot and a half further than I would like."

Mrs Postridge is about sixty and she has run the post office and the newsagent at number two for twenty-nine years, and she was not interested in any of it and had not been asked to be, and she had taken the paper when it came in and had held it while they nailed it up and had gone back to her counter.

"More than two hundred lines and about forty of them still blank," Elias Rook said, from about eight feet away, and did not go closer.

"A hundred and ninety-one names in the fourth column," Alagoa said, from the other end of the pavement. "I counted them on Friday night at about half past ten with a shopkeeper's pencil and I am not going to be the one who is correct, so take that as a number somebody put in a room, and it will be wrong by nine by Tuesday and that is how it should be."

"Then why is that one in a different pencil."

"Because that one is mine," Alagoa said.

---

There are two of the four on it. They are on the rows for eleven and nineteen Sallow Court, and they are in a woman's hand and in a different pencil from the other hundred and eighty-nine. The other two are not on it and are not going to be, because a woman of sixty-three decided on Friday morning in January that she is not putting the name of a person nobody on that row can stand behind onto a sheet that is going onto a nail in a shop, and they are in half an inch of margin inside the second fold of a sheet that has been in a drawer upstairs since the fourth of March 2031, in a kitchen pencil, from about eleven o'clock at night in November. The only other document in this city that has ever held anything at all about those four doors is a schedule drawn on the eleventh of September, which was withdrawn from a board room on Friday afternoon at four minutes to two and is going into a file, and nobody in this district is satisfied with any of it and nobody is going to be.

"They are the four," Elias Rook said out loud, to a pavement on a Saturday morning, and stopped, and understood that he was not going to say anything else about it in this district, ever, and that the reason was not a rule and had never been a rule and was a woman in a doorway on Friday saying *it is not about your office*.

A determination came out of the board on Friday and the schedule of the second of December is withdrawn, and the block that is being emptied tonight is fourteen to twenty-two Cutbank Row and thirty-one Cutbank Row, and thirty-one is a woman of seventy-nine who has been on that row forty-six years and is called by her husband's name by everybody including the post office, and she came out of number thirty-one at about half past ten in the morning to look at the nail, and looked at it for a while from about four feet away, and went in.

The block starts at fourteen and it ends at twenty-two, and twenty-two is the flat over the shop that sells door furniture, and the woman who lives in it has known since the sheets went round the shops in December that her own front door is inside a line somebody else drew, and she has not said so to anybody, and there is no form in this city that asks a person whether they are inside one, and she was on the row at about ten o'clock this morning while three people nailed a length of paper to a wall above the counter at number two, and she did not say one word about any of it, and nobody asked her, because since November not one person in this district has asked her a question she had not offered the answer to first.

---

The works ran the third of the four nights on the seventeenth with four men and a ladder, and there are three signed lines on a sheet on a trestle in a survey office and one blank line on it, and the booking runs to Thursday the fifth of February, and after the fifth of February the board has to come back for money and in her experience it does not come back in the same year it has already spent it, and a foreman of fifty-one has told a yard with his hands in his pockets that he is going to hand the shift book in in February, and called it how a yard works and not a threat and not a gesture, and a hardback book with a ruled page a day stayed on a shelf in two rooms above a shop on Cutbank Row and is still there and is not on an index, and the eleven names on the cards in a drawer in a canteen have got a street written on the back of each of them in a foreman's own hand, which is the first time in nineteen years that a works card has carried one, and nobody ever wrote a form that asked for it, and a woman at a health center on Marden Row has eleven names on the back of a discharge list that nobody ever asked her for and never will.

And in the front room on Ash Street there is a wall with three questions on it and no room left on it, and the second half of the oldest is still a bracket with nothing under it, and the nineteenth of November is still the only date, and the four minutes and a lamp vote are in Petar Lisk's book on a page after the twenty-second of November, and nobody has taken anything off that wall and nobody is going to.

In a two-room flat on Cutbank Row there is a boiler that moves furniture in the next room, and a table with a cloth on it, and a chair on the table-side with a blanket over the back of it that is not for visitors, and on the table on Saturday morning there was a school exercise book with a plastic cover, bought for two pounds in a shop on Marden Row in the autumn by a woman of sixty-three out of her own money and in her own mouth, and it had been lying on that table since the autumn with a kettle ring on the front of it and it was not a record and it was not a document and it had never been shown to anybody.

She went in at about eleven and he followed her up because she left the door open eight inches, and she opened the book at the back, past the sums, to a page with a sum on the other side, and wrote with a ballpoint, and did not put it on the wall and did not put it on the nail, and did not read it out to anybody including the man in a coat who was standing in her doorway at the time.

"I am not writing that for a record," Winifred Alagoa said, before he could ask her anything, "and I am not writing it for a committee and I am not writing it for that shed, and I have not written it down because anybody asked me, and I bought that book with my own money in a shop in September because I have got eleven years of doors on a sheet and there is nowhere in this city to put a question. I cannot sleep, that is all it is. I cannot sleep and there is nowhere else to put it and that is the entire reason and I would like it left where it is, and it is not going on the wall at yours and it is not going on the nail at hers."

"I am not going to ask you for the exercise book," Elias Rook said.

"You were going to," Alagoa said. "You have been in my kitchen four times since November and every single time you have wanted something out of it and have not asked, and this is the fifth, and I watched you not ask, and I would like you to go and sit down, because you look as though somebody has taken something off you."
