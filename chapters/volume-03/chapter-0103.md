# Chapter 103 — The Cell Above A Shop

He was a field technician again as of midnight, in a letter one paragraph long, and on Monday morning at half past nine he was standing on a pavement on Cutbank Row in a district he had never been inside, doing the thing he has done every working morning of his life, which is to look at people first.

Cliff Marner was fifty-six days out of the ground. Della Marner was eighty-six days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was eighty-six days in. None of it touched the morning and all of it came with him on the bus.

Mara Okafor came out of the health center with a box of gloves under her arm and stopped when she saw him, and did not stop for long.

"You are going to be here all week," she said.

"I am going to be here all week."

"Then come and find me after four. I am here until Thursday and I am doing a session at ten and a session at two and I am not a tour guide and I am not a rescuer in this district, and if anybody in that building asks you whether I sent you, I did not."

She looked at him for a second, the way she had on Friday on the landing.

"How long have you been out?"

"Since midnight."

"Then you have not slept," she said, "because that letter is not a reason to lose a night, and I have written that sentence down twice in my life and I am not writing it down a third time for you. Come at four."

She went back in through the door and the door shut, and there was a printed card beside it about the works and the district and a public counter on the ground floor, and Elias Rook read the card and went to look at number six instead.

---

Number six was a shop that sold door furniture and had a counter at the back of it, and above the shop there were two rooms with a stair on the outside of the building, which is the oldest kind of arrangement there is and the cheapest, and the stair shook under him.

Four people. That was the whole of it, and the whole of it was smaller than he had expected in a way that he did not understand until the Wednesday.

Selwyn Anum was forty-one and stood up when he came in, and had a hand out before Elias had got the door closed, and said, "Rook. You have got one hand."

"I have."

"Good. Do not sign anything. It saves a step." He said it as a joke and it was a joke and about half of it was not a joke. "Sit there. Do you want the short version or the method?"

"The method."

"That is the correct answer and nobody gives it to me." Anum turned round and pulled a roll of paper off a rail against the far wall and unrolled about four feet of it, and it was not paper that had been through a printer. **It was a band. A wide margin drawn in a soft line, street by street, in three colors of ink, and it went across the whole west bank and stopped in the middle of a street.**

"Sit down and look at it," Anum said, "and tell me what you think it is."

"I think it's where the ground is carrying."

"Correct. That is a securing band and it is the eleventh of September and it is the reason a district of two thousand nine hundred people is still a district." Anum rolled it back up and rolled it out again, further, and there were four more like it on the floor behind the door, rolled, in different states of age. "That is a reading. It is not a repair and it is not a prediction and it does not stop anything happening. What it does is tell you the direction force will want to travel through the ground under that line, which is a thing you can measure and a thing you can argue with, and that is the whole of why this program exists."

"How do you get it."

"**I do not. I convert it.**" He said it cheerfully. "That is step one and if you want the method sit down."

---

There was a man at a desk with a laptop and a woman at the counter and neither of them said anything for forty minutes, and neither of them was stupid, and Elias Rook understood that the reason they said nothing was that they were being polite and that they would have talked all day if he had asked.

Anum talked all day because he had been asked.

"Step one, you take the band. Step two, you take the occupancy class off the structures inside it, and that is a building attribute, it is on the survey, it has been on the survey since 2039, and you do not get an opinion about it. Step three, you apply the status rule. Step four — and step four is the one everybody thinks is a formality, and it is not — step four is: before you say anything at all about the plan, say out loud that you have not told this room what you are about to do, and that you are telling them now and not afterwards. Then stop talking."

Elias Rook put his cup down on the edge of the desk, carefully, with one hand, and Anum watched him do it.

"**That is step four of a method I have read once, in a front room on Ash Street, in eight pages that a man of sixty-four put on a table in November and said was a method and not a confession, and I have not shown them to anybody and nobody in this room has ever seen them.**"

He said it in one sentence and stopped.

The woman at the counter did not look up. The man at the laptop did not look up. Anum said, "Right," and went to the kettle.

Nobody in that room reacted to it at all, and Elias Rook understood about eleven seconds late why, and it was the same eleven seconds he had been losing all autumn: because the thing he had recognized was not a fact about a room in Tidegate. It was a fact about a building he had been sitting in for twelve weeks, and he had brought it up two miles and eleven minutes and put it on a stranger's desk, and it had landed in a room where it was not about anything.

"Step five," Anum said, coming back. "Stop talking and let them stop you."

"Yes."

"That is the hard one."

"That is the hard one," Elias Rook said, and waited, because a man who has written five steps down does not stop at five.

Anum sat down on the edge of the desk.

"If nobody stops you, you have not done step five, and you go back to step two and take it to somebody else." He looked at the woman at the counter, who was putting lids on cups. "Nobody has ever stopped me. I want that understood. I have run this method in four districts since 2039 and I have had two hundred people in rooms and not one of them has ever said *stop*, and the reason is that they think they are being polite, and the reason they think they are being polite is that I did step four well enough that they felt told."

Elias Rook tried it three times that day.

The first attempt was at about half past eleven, and it was a question about what happened if a room did stop him, and Anum answered it happily and at length and it was the wrong question.

The second was at about one, and it was closer, and it got as far as *but that is the same six people* before Anum said "what is the same" and meant it.

The third was at about four, and by then Anum had stopped being pleased about the whole thing and had gone back to his own work, and Elias Rook got out the sentence *a room told in advance is a room and a room not told in advance is an audience* and had to put it back, because he did not know where he had got it from and he was not going to say it in this room to a man he had known for five hours.

He gave up at about ten past four and rode the 11 back and wrote two lines in the notebook and one of them was the sentence he had nearly said.

---

The Retention Schedule came out of a drawer in a folder with a printed label on it, at about half past three, unasked.

"You have not asked for it," Anum said, "and you are going to, so I have taken it out, and if I gave it to you after you asked it would be a favor and I have enough of those."

Elias Rook took it. It was nineteen pages, stapled at the corner, on the same good paper as the leaflet, and the front page said:

> **RETENTION SCHEDULE — FOURTH STAGE TIDEGATE**
> **Ref. BWP/TG/4SD/2042 — schedule of 2 December 2042 — 697 rows**
> **Address · Structure · Occupancy class · Status**

Four columns. He has spent twelve weeks in a building where a missing column was the entire case, and he counted, and there were four, and they were address, structure, occupancy class, and status.

**There was no column a person went in.**

He read it twice standing up. Then he sat down and read the second page, where the footnote was, in italics, four lines long:

> *The band is indicative. This schedule is the operative document.*
> *Where a reading and a schedule differ, the schedule governs.*
> *Readings are taken at intervals and are not continuous.*
> *Current reading: 11 September 2042.*

Four hundred and twelve rows said `RETAINED`. Two hundred and eighty-five said `TRANSITIONAL`.

"Why is it dated tomorrow," he said.

"Because it is operative from tomorrow and a schedule that did not carry the date it takes effect would be a schedule nobody could tell what it was for." Anum said it without looking up. "That is not clever. That is in the guidance. The guidance is four pages and I can give you a copy if you turn up with a reference from a recognized body, and I am going to assume you have not, and I am not going to insult you by asking."

He turned round and pulled a second roll off the rail.

"You want the conversion, and you are about to be disappointed, because there is nothing in it. You take the band, you take the occupancy class off the structures, you apply the status rule, you sign it, and you date it. **And the date is the day you did it, and not the day the ground moved.** Those are the two things people get wrong and one of them is on that page and one of them is in a lecture I give twice a year."

He said it as a professional explanation. He said it the way you explain a step to a new method officer who has just been handed the whole thing and has not yet understood that it is not a mystery. He said it about four hundred people being rehoused, and he said it with a slight lift in his voice, because he liked being asked, and he said it as though it were completely obvious that it was a thing a reasonable person would do on a Tuesday, and then he stopped and put the kettle on.

Elias Rook said nothing, and did not ask him a second time, and that was the growth and there was nobody in Tidegate to see it.

---

He read the footnote on the bus at ten past five, with the folder on his knees under his right hand.

*The band is indicative. This schedule is the operative document.*

He read it four times, on a bench at the near end of the cut, with the light going, and it did not make sense to him then and he could not have explained to anybody in a room what was wrong with it, and the reason was that on Monday the first of December he did not know there was a second reading.

The second reading was two days old.
