# Chapter 144 — Three People, No Notice, Every Office Shut

Cliff Marner was ninety-seven days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and twenty-seven days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and twenty-seven days in, and Elias Rook was at the yard gate on Sunday morning, the eleventh of January, at about twenty past eight, because Hobbs had said the barrier was down at ten to seven and he had missed it and was not going to say so, and the yard was shut because a yard shuts on Sundays, and the counter at number six was shut behind a locked door and the board was shut and the hearing room was shut, and there was no opening anywhere in this city on Sunday the eleventh of January except a shop that sold door furniture and a newsagent that shut at one.

There were four ruled lines on Nerys Hobbs's sheet and two of them were signed and two of them were blank, and she had told him on the Saturday morning at ten past seven that she would sign the second one on the Sunday at half past six in the morning, and had signed it at ten past seven, and had been right to.

---

He was on Cutbank Row by half past ten because Saturday is the day Winifred Alagoa reads her sheet out loud and Sunday is the day she does not, and he had come down for no reason he could have put in a room, and it was about a quarter past ten when he saw them.

They were at the end of the row by the wall of the boatyard, three of them and a bag each, and nobody was talking to them, and it was not a queue and it was not a disturbance. It was a man of about forty and a young man of about nineteen and a woman of about sixty with a hand on the young man's arm, and they were standing the way three people stand when they have arrived somewhere and have not yet worked out whether they have arrived.

The man of about forty had a plastic wallet with documents in it and was holding it against his chest the way a man holds a thing he has been asked for and has not handed over. The young man had nothing. The woman had a hand on his arm and was about two feet shorter than him.

Elias Rook came down the row at the speed a man comes down a row when he has decided something, and stopped four feet away, and asked two questions.

"What is your name," he said, "and what is your address."

The man of about forty looked at him.

"That is all you are going to ask me."

"That is all I am going to ask you," Elias Rook said. "I am not going to ask you anything else today and I want you to have that in your head before you answer the first one, because people who have been asked two questions in a row usually wait for the third and then they cannot get the first two back."

The young man said his name before the other two had decided anything, out of pure fright, and gave an address, and the woman gave hers, and the man of about forty gave his and then stood there with a wallet against his chest.

Emrys Vaughan, forty-one, of a flat in a building on a street that is not here. Marek Drzewicki, nineteen, of an address eleven minutes from this bollard on the other side of the cut. Sabina Hough, sixty-three, of a house on a road in this district that is not on the alignment and is not being cleared.

Elias Rook wrote the three on the back of a page in a notebook with his right forearm across it, and did not ask a fourth question, and there was a version of himself that went looking for a different word to use.

---

"Do not," said a voice behind him.

Winifred Alagoa had come out of the newsagent at half past ten and had been standing in her own doorway for about three minutes.

"I am not asking them anything," Elias Rook said.

"I know you are not," Alagoa said. "I have been standing in here for three minutes waiting for you to ask them the third question, because that is what a man in a coat does, and I want you to hear me say that whatever you were about to ask, you are not going to ask it here, and not in the yard, and not in a room above a shop either."

"Then what am I going to do with them."

"Nothing, today, because it is a Sunday," Alagoa said. "That is the actual answer and it is why I came out. There is nowhere in this city I could take those three people this morning. Not the board, not the office at number six, not the clinic, not the church hall, and the newsagent shuts at one and Mrs Postridge does not do this. So they are three people standing at the end of my road in the cold with nowhere to be sent, and you have a name and an address each, and if you write them down and take them somewhere on Monday then that is the correct arrangement and it is an arrangement with a day on it."

"You have not asked them anything either."

"I have not asked them anything at all," Alagoa said, "and I have been in this street for thirty-eight years and it is the hardest thing I do every week of my life and I do it at a bollard on a Saturday with fifteen people standing about who are not asking me anything either."

---

Nothing had come. That was the thing he kept coming back to on the walk down the row: for four months there had been a thing that arrived, and it had not arrived, and the three of them were standing at the end of a street in the cold with plastic wallets and no warning of any kind, and he had felt nothing on the wall at the top of Cutbank Row at half past ten and there was nothing in his hands and nothing in the air in front of him and nothing to carry, and he had told a woman in a corridor on the sixth of December that silence means no, and there had been no voice to say no.

And then Piotr Drzewicki came up the row at about eleven o'clock at a speed that is not a walking speed, fifty-four years old, in a good coat, and he came from the direction of the wall of the boatyard, and he went to the young man and said a word in Polish that Elias Rook did not understand, and the young man said a word back, and the two of them stood in the road about a foot apart for about four seconds.

Then Piotr Drzewicki turned round to Elias Rook and said, in English, and not loudly, "You have his name."

"I have his name and his address."

Emrys Vaughan, who had not said a word since the bollard, spoke from inside the newsagent's doorway and he spoke to nobody in particular.

"There is a register in this city with my name on it and a woman on it called Ceri Vaughan," he said, "and Ceri Vaughan is a person, and I have never met her, and I have wanted since the Wednesday before last for somebody in an office to look me in the face and tell me I am wrong, and two people have done it today and it has not made me wrong, and I would like somebody to stop telling me that things are being looked into." He stopped, and then said, "My wife is called Bronwen, and I have said that name out loud on this pavement twice this morning and both times it has been the wrong name for this city, and I am not going to stop saying it, because I would like somebody to write it down and tell me in a month that it is not on anything."

The woman of sixty-three said, "Sabina," and that was the first thing anybody had said to any of the three of them that was not a question.

"I have not asked him a third question," Elias Rook said, "because a woman has just told me not to on her own doorstep, and she is right and it is her doorstep. You are going to have to do this on a Monday in a room with chairs in it and a man behind a counter, and he is going to show you the same page."

"Then you have a form," Piotr Drzewicki said, and did not take it out of his coat.

Piotr Drzewicki stood in the middle of Cutbank Row and put both hands flat on the sides of his coat, which is a thing a man does when he is trying not to touch anything.

"I have a birth certificate in a drawer in a house eleven minutes from here," he said, "and it does not have him on it, and it has a son on it, and my wife is not in this country and I have not been able to explain to anybody in eleven weeks. I have been to that office on a Tuesday. I have been there four times. I have a form in a coat and I cannot make the man in the office put a name on it, and he is not a fool and he is not unkind, and every single time he has shown me the same page and said *sir, this is what we hold*."

"Then it is not a mistake."

"It is not a mistake," Piotr Drzewicki said, "and that is the whole of what is wrong with it."

Declan Hough, thirty-one, came down the row at about ten past eleven with a car and parked badly and got out of it, and Sabina Hough turned round and looked at him, and neither of the two of them said anything at all.

"You are her son," Elias Rook said, to Declan Hough, and then stopped, because he had not asked himself whether he was allowed to say that to a stranger, and he had said it out loud on a pavement.

"I am her brother's son," Declan Hough said. "That is what my mother says. I have a form at home that says I am her son and I have known her since I was four. I am thirty-one. I went to her funeral in 'thirty-nine and I am telling you that now so that you know it is not a thing that came out of a drawer on Saturday morning." He looked at the young man on the pavement, and did not go near him. "I did not come down here for him. I came down here because she telephoned me at eight o'clock this morning and said she was on a street in this city and had a document and could not get anybody to open anything, and I have been in a car for two days and I am going to say to you the same thing I said to her on the telephone, which is that I will do whatever is put in front of me and I am not going to decide anything today."

Nobody said anything for about a minute, on a pavement, on a Sunday.

Elias Rook wrote two words on the back of his hand under the three addresses. He did not ask anything else, and he did not go to the counter at number six, because the counter at number six was shut behind a locked door and it was a Sunday and it was a Saturday this week that the street would be emptied and there was nowhere in this city to put three people on a Sunday.

He walked them to the newsagent, which was open until one, and Mrs Postridge let them stand inside out of the wind on the strength of nothing at all, and nobody explained them, and Mrs Postridge did not ask.

Alagoa went back in and shut her door eight inches and then, after a moment, opened it another four, the way she has done with a man in a coat since the twenty-ninth of November, and did not come down.
