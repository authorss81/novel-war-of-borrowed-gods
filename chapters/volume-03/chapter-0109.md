# Chapter 109 — Sallow Court

Cliff Marner was sixty-two days out of the ground. Della Marner was ninety-two days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was ninety-two days in. Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past nine on Sunday morning, the seventh of December, with Joon Park, who had asked to come and had been told there was nothing to come for and had come.

The yard does not run on Sundays. The gate is shut, and there is a works notice on the outside of it, and there are three of them on it now, and Elias Rook had walked past that gate on six days in one week and had not read one of them.

He read it on Sunday.

> **WORKS NOTICE — 3 TO 27 SALLOW COURT**
> **Clearance, 9 November 2042. Nights of 8 and 9. Residents relocated by arrangement.**
> **Clearance complete, no outstanding.**

Three lines and a date. Underneath, a second notice about a skip and a third about a water supply, both of which were newer, and the paper on the first one had gone the color the paper on the ninth of November goes to by the seventh of December.

Nobody had taken it down. Nobody had covered it. Nobody in that district had ever seen it, and it was not hidden, and no program officer had stood in front of it and said nothing, and it had been on a gate at the end of a road for twenty-eight days while a man with a folder went past it with his eyes on a piece of paper in his hand.

---

"Clearance complete, no outstanding," Elias Rook said. "That is the whole of it. That is the entire record of a night and a night, and it is on a gate, and it is in the third person as far as anybody is concerned, which is what a word like *complete* is for."

Joon Park read it twice.

"Where is it kept," he said.

"I do not know. He does not know. Neither of us has asked, and neither of us knew it existed on Wednesday, and that is the part I do not like and the part I am not going to be able to put anywhere."

"Which part is that."

"That I have spent a week in a district reading documents out of a folder that somebody gave me on purpose, and did not walk to the end of the road."

---

Winifred Alagoa was in her flat with the door shut on the draught and the boiler moving furniture in the next room, and the sheet was on the table where it lives, and she let him in and did not make him ask for anything.

"I did Sallow Court last," she said. "And I did not tell you why, and I have thought about it since Saturday and I am going to tell you anyway, so you can decide what to do with it."

"Go on."

"Sallow Court is the one I get wrong." She put the second pot on. "Not wrong wrong. I get it wrong and I get it right and the two things are the same number. In the spring I had thirty-nine doors on that block and I have thirty-nine now and the board's figure is forty-one and I have known that since June and I have not done one thing about it, because I have a sheet and a sheet says thirty-nine and the sheet is what I can check with my feet."

"There is a difference of two."

"There is a difference of two and it has been sitting on my table since June and I have not told anybody about it because there is nobody to tell, and I am not going to start now and be a woman with a grievance at sixty-three." She poured. "That is the wrong of it. That is the bit I do last. And the rest of it is the other thing, and the other thing is in the margin."

---

The margin of the sheet is about half an inch wide and it is on the inside of the second fold, where you would not see it if you were holding the sheet the way you hold a sheet, and there are four names written down the margin in a different pencil from the one the sheet is in.

"I did not have a pencil of my own on the night," she said. "I had a kitchen pencil. I wrote those four names on the ninth of November at about eleven at night with the kitchen pencil while a woman from the works stood in my doorway with a torch she did not need, and I did not know then that it was unusual that a woman should come with a torch to a street where there was no power. She was very polite. She asked me if I knew of anybody at three to twenty-seven who might be elderly and living alone, and I said there were four, and I gave her the four names, and she wrote them in a book she had, and then she went up the street.

"And about half an hour later I got the kitchen pencil out and I put them down on my own paper as well, because she had written them in a book and I did not know what the book was, and if the book went anywhere then the four went with it, and I am sixty-three and I have been keeping a sheet since 2031 because of a number on a fence and I do not need to be told twice."

She put the second pot on and did not look at him.

"And they are dead, Mr. Rook, and that is the part nobody has said to anybody. I knew by the Wednesday which end it had gone. Nobody has come to this door. I have not gone to anybody, and I am sixty-three, and I am not going to stand on a step in that street in December and ask a stranger about a woman I met once at a gate. Their names are not on the board's list and they are not on my sheet, because neither one of those is a list of people. **The only place their names are written is that margin, and I would know, because I have been checking that board since 2031 and it has been wrong twice.**"

Elias Rook read the four names twice.

He did not write them in the notebook. That was not only restraint. The notebook is a thing he carries, and the four names were four people he had never met, and he had no idea in the world what he was entitled to write down, and a man in a hostel on the near bank on a Sunday night could not be trusted with them because there was nobody in the world who could tell him he had got one of them wrong.

"You can have them," Winifred Alagoa said, before he asked. "I have been waiting for a day when somebody wanted them. You are not having the sheet and I have said that to you and I will say it to the next one. **You can have the four because the four are not on the sheet.** The sheet is doors. Doors do not die. The four are in the margin in a kitchen pencil and they have been in the margin since the ninth of November and they are the only copy in the world and I would like them to be in two places tonight, and I would like that to be my decision and not yours."

"That is your decision."

"It is my decision. I have been waiting eleven years for somebody to want something off this table and I am telling you what I am willing to have and I am not going to be thanked for it because I have not done anything for you."

---

Joon Park asked the only question that mattered, from the other side of the table, and he asked it in about four seconds, which is the fastest he ever does anything.

"Mr. Rook, I need to know what I am being handed."

"What do you mean."

"I mean is this a record." He had his pen out and had not put it on the paper. "Because I hold things, and everything I hold is on a sheet with twenty-two lines on it, and every one of those twenty-two lines is a thing somebody asked me to hold and I said yes to, and the sheet is the reason I have never lost anything in twelve weeks. And if I put four names in my pocket on a Sunday morning then they are in a system, and in a month somebody is going to ask me where they came from, and I am going to have to say a woman's kitchen, and then they are in a system."

"It is not a record," Elias Rook said. "It is four names in a margin in a pencil in a kitchen and there is no second copy of anything."

"Then I will take it anyway." Joon Park wrote the four names on the back of his own hand, in pencil, small, and did not put it on the sheet. "The taking is my decision and not hers, and I am not going to describe it as anything, and it does not go on here and it does not get a number, and if anybody in this city ever asks me where I got it I will say a woman on Cutbank Row wrote it down and that is the whole of what I will say."

"That," said Winifred Alagoa, "is the correct answer, and it is the first correct answer anybody has given me about anything in about four years, and I would have said no to the word *record*. Do not call it a record in this room. It is a pencil."

"It is a pencil," Joon Park agreed, and left his pen down beside the paper, and did not write anything else.

---

Here is the other half of it, and he had to be told it, and he did not like any part of it.

The works board's own address list for 3 to 27 Sallow Court says forty-one households. It is a printed schedule and it is available to anybody who asks and it came out of the same envelope as the rates. Winifred Alagoa's sheet says thirty-nine doors for the same block, in her own hand, counted on foot, on a Saturday, for eleven years.

Forty-one less thirty-nine is two.

"And the four are in neither of them," Elias Rook said.

"The four are in neither of them, and neither of those two things is a list of people, and that is why the four are in neither of them." She put two fingers on the sheet without straightening it, which is a thing she has done since Sunday. "Forty-one households is a number somebody wrote down about buildings. Thirty-nine doors is a number I wrote down about buildings. You could take both of those numbers to a meeting and have an argument about them for an hour and you would be arguing about two buildings.

"And a person cannot be inside the wrong number. I told a man that in 2031 and he wrote it down and went away."

Elias Rook sat in a two-room flat on Cutbank Row and looked at a margin half an inch wide.

"Two," he said.

"Two," said Winifred Alagoa. "I have got two and I have never been able to do one single thing about them, and I would have told you in June if you had asked me in June, and I am telling you now because you did not ask me in June."

---

The thing he did not do on Sunday was the thing he had wanted to do since about two o'clock in the afternoon.

**There were four people dead in this city for twenty-eight days and there was no piece of paper in the world that said so.** There were four doors on that block where nobody had been told anything, and there was a woman in this flat who had gone to all four on the night, politely, with a torch she did not need, and she had written their names in a book, and the book had gone somewhere, and the only place on earth where the four names were still written was half an inch of margin in a different pencil.

He could have gone to four doors. He had the tube under his arm and the notebook and a bus at twenty past five.

He said it out loud once, in his own voice, to a man who did not need to be told anything.

"I am not going to use these for four days," he said. "I am not going to go to their doors today, and I am not going to use one line of this in a room tomorrow night, and I want it said out loud to somebody before it is said out loud to a room."

"Write it down," Joon Park said, and did not write it down.

They got the 11 at ten past four. On the back of the eleven-minute run, over the low road where the water comes up in a hard winter, Elias Rook sat with a cardboard tube across his knees and four names in pencil on the back of a hand that was not his, and did not open the tube, and it was the hardest physical thing he had done in a district that had been handing him easy things all week.
