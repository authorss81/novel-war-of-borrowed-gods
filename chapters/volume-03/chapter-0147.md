# Chapter 147 — I Have Been Reading A List As If It Were The Method

Cliff Marner was one hundred days out of the ground. Della Marner was one hundred and thirty days at a family-reception desk in Cordell. The man behind the wall was one hundred and thirty days in, and Elias Rook got the 11 at ten past eight on Wednesday morning, the fourteenth of January, and there were two cars outside the shop at number six at a quarter to ten that had come from the Meridian, and the counter was open, and the board was open, and the yard was open, and it was a working day.

The room above number six was booked in his name from ten until one. Wendy Tallow came at ten to nine and told him what the room was for, and it was not a meeting.

"They are not having a meeting," Tallow said. "Mr. Park worked it out on Sunday and he has told me and I am telling you and I am not writing it anywhere. One of them has asked for a room with a door and no table. One of them has asked to be told what this program is, in words, by somebody who is not paid by it, before anything else happens in this room. And the other one has asked to come for an afternoon and to be in the district and has not said why, and he came for an afternoon in December and said one thing about a document, and I am not asking him about it because he is owed a room and a chair and not a question."

---

Sable Arden was fifty-eight and had signed the pilot authorization in August and had never been in this district, and she came in at a quarter to ten with no folder and a coat over her arm and stood in the room for about two minutes before she sat down, looking at the wall, and at the trestle, and at the door of number six with the cell's two rooms behind it, and at the four photographs in a jar on a shelf that she did not know were there.

"This is the smallest room I have ever given a briefing in," she said, "and I have given a great many, and I am not going to complain about it, and I want to say that before anything else, because the room is the correct size and I am aware of what that means."

Then she sat down, and put her hands on the table, and did not make a note of anything.

"I am going to say one sentence and then I am going to be quiet for a while, and I have said the sentence in a room twice this month and both times it was to a man who was not going to use it in a way I would have minded, and this time I am saying it to you and to a man of thirty-four with a book, and I have not decided whether you are going to use it in a way I would mind, and I would rather decide that afterwards."

Nobody said anything.

"**I authorized a method. I have been reading a list as if it were the method for four months because the list was working.**"

She did not say anything else for about a minute and a half.

"That is the whole of what I have come to say on my own account," Arden said, "and I am not going to soften it and I am not going to be helped to soften it, and I have not brought a piece of paper about it because there is not a piece of paper about it anywhere in this city, in my office or in anybody else's, and that is the finding and I have had four months to make it into something else and I could not."

"Have you read the schedule," Elias Rook said, which was a question and not an accusation, and he heard himself make it that way.

"I have read the schedule," Sable Arden said. "I read it on the second of December when it came out of the region and I read it again on Boxing Day when the letter went out and I read it this morning on the train with a pen, and I am telling you what I did with it, which is that I put it in a drawer with the authorization in it and I have worked from it ever since. I have never once compared it to anything on the ground, because nobody ever brought me anything from the ground, and because the program is very good at not needing me, and because the district I picked in September was the district where the ground was soft and the wall was the cheapest, and that is not a coincidence and I want it in the room: **I chose this district because the ground was soft.** The reading was right. The ground is soft. I did not choose a ground. I chose the ground I was given by a man in a shed, and I have been right about that for nine years, and there is a piece of paper going round this district that I have not seen and that nobody has sent me, and it is the only paper about this district in nine years that a person living in it could pick up and argue with, and I am not going to be told about it in a car on a Sunday."

The thread was off in that room. It was off on Cutbank Row and off over the water and off on Ash Street, and it was on eleven hundred yards away under a street where five men were carrying a bed, and it had been on for two days and he had not asked it for anything, and there was nothing in his hands and nothing in the air in front of him, and Sable Arden was fifty-eight years old and eleven minutes from him and he could not have told anybody in that room which street she was standing on.

"I am stopped," Sable Arden said. "Not the pilot. The method is not withdrawn and the authorization is not withdrawn and the reader is still yours and the band is still the best thing anybody in this city has produced about a district in nine years, and I would fight anybody who came at it with a pair of scissors, and I have a letter in my coat that says the conversion is mine and it is stopped, and I have signed it this morning, and it is dated today, and it is in with Selwyn, and it is one line and it is the only line I have signed this year that I am not going to be able to take back."

"And the schedule," Elias Rook said.

"The schedule goes to a determination on Friday," Arden said, "and I have been told what the determination is going to say and I have not been asked whether I agree and I have agreed with it and I have not been asked to be a hero about it."

---

Nathan Rusk came in at eleven and stayed until about twenty to one, and he came in on his own, and he was fifty-nine and he had spent a piece of his own authority in the last quarter of a year and said publicly what it had cost him.

He had two things. He said them in a room, in this order, and he said them once, and he did not amplify them, and he was not asked to say them a second time and nobody in that room asked him for anything else.

"**A pilot is by definition a thing that can be stopped.**"

He let that sit for a second, the way a man does when he knows a room is going to wait.

"**Stopping it is not a finding against anybody. The finding against anybody is in four years' time,** and it will not be this room's finding and it will not be mine, because the four years have not happened yet, and anybody in this district who is waiting for somebody to say who was at fault is going to be standing on this corner in 2047 with the same sheet of paper and I would like them to have read that sentence first."

Joon Park had the custody schedule in his coat and did not take it out, and said, at the end, "I am the only person in this building who is allowed to say I do not know that, and I am going to say it about the second half of that sentence rather than the first. I do not know what the finding is in four years. Nobody does. I have a sheet with twenty-two lines on it and it is not a form about the future."

"That is the correct use of a mouth," Rusk said.

"It is the only use there is."

---

At about ten past one the room broke up and the two men from the Meridian came out into the corridor above number six, and Joon Park went down the stairs ahead of them, and Elias Rook stayed in the corridor for a minute with the folder that had not been opened and had the tube under his arm with a page dated the first of January in it, and the town outside was doing what a town does on a Wednesday in January.

Down on Cutbank Row there were three people who had come back into a district on Monday and had not gone away, and a woman of sixty-three who had been writing addresses on a piece of paper all week in her own hand, and about two hundred people who were coming to a hall in the evening, and a band that had come off two streets in the first week of January, and a schedule that had been drawn once on the eleventh of September and was still on Friday the sixteenth going to be the operative document of a public works department unless a determination said otherwise, and a form in a drawer in a canteen with eleven names on it and no street on it, and a hardback book on a bench in a yard with a rubber band round the corner of it that had one page in it that said what a foreman had been told at five to nine on the eighth of January, and a woman in a good coat on a landing on Saturday who had gone back down a flight of stairs with a card in her hand, and a woman of forty-seven who had read a street with a level and a steel pin on a Tuesday and had not read it with anything else at all, and none of it had a name on it, and none of it was in a form, and every single piece of it was going to be argued about in a room on Thursday evening by about two hundred people, and the argument was going to be better than anything any of them had brought with them.
